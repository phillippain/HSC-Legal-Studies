How to use this site

Every question the HSC has set on Crime, Human Rights, Family and Shelter since 2011, sorted the three ways you are likely to want them. Nothing here is a test — click about.

1Pick how you want to search

The three tabs at the top of the left-hand column are three different questions you might be asking.

  • Content — “what has been asked about bail?” Follow the syllabus itself, topic by topic.
  • Themes — “where does discretion come up?” The themes and challenges every answer is supposed to weave through.
  • Directive — “give me every evaluate question.” Grouped by the verb the question sets and by how much it demands.

Or type into the search box — press / from anywhere to jump there, and Esc to clear it. Search looks through content points, themes and the questions themselves.

2Moving in and out of a topic

The Content tab has three levels, and you walk down them one at a time.

Level 1The four topics Crime and Human Rights are the cores; Family and Shelter are the options. Click one to open it.
Level 2Syllabus sections The big divisions of that topic — “Sentencing and punishment”, for instance. Click one to open it.
Level 3Dot points “Students learn about…”, straight from the syllabus. Click one to see the questions built on it.

Getting back out. Once you are inside a topic a trail sits at the top of the left-hand column, like this:

All topics › Crime › Sentencing and punishment

Every step except the last one is a button. Click Crime to go back to its other sections, or All topics to return to the four cards — you never have to press Back twice. The Home button beside the search box goes all the way out and brings up the coverage map.

Opening a section or a dot point does not clear what is on the right — the questions stay put until you choose something new, so you can climb back up and look around without losing your place.

3What the numbers beside each row mean

  • 3/8 — eight questions touch this, and three are built on it. The first number is the core count, the second is the total.
  • ↑12 — nothing is tagged to this dot point on its own, so the twelve come from the section above it. Extended responses are tagged to a whole section, because a 25-mark answer draws on one.
  • — — never examined. That does not make it unexaminable; it makes it overdue.

4Narrowing what you see

  • The four section buttons turn each part of the paper on and off. Revising essays only? Turn off Section I and Section II A.
  • From limits the years, for when only the recent papers matter.
  • Core focus only drops the questions where your point is just supporting evidence, leaving the ones genuinely about it.

5Attempting, then marking

Answers and marking criteria start hidden, so you can write first. Click Reveal once for the answer or the band criteria, and again for NESA's sample answer where one was published. Each question also shows its marks, its directive verb and roughly how long it should take you.

6Building a worksheet

Tick the questions you want as you browse — ticks are saved in this browser and go nowhere else. All and Clear tick everything currently shown. Print worksheet then produces a paper with writing lines, and the marking criteria on pages of their own at the back.

In a hurry? Random pulls one question out of whatever you have filtered — from a whole topic, or from a single dot point. Home shows the coverage map, where the empty cells are the points the HSC has left alone.

Lost? Open the guide — how to search, how to move in and out of a topic, what the numbers mean, and how to build a worksheet.

What is in here. Every question from 2011 to 2025, the life of the 2009 syllabus: 300 Section I objective-response questions with their options and NESA's answer key, 51 Human Rights short answers, the 15 Crime extended responses, and both alternatives of the Family and Shelter option questions — 426 in all.

Two levels of tagging. Section I and Section II Part A are tagged to a single syllabus dot point, because a one- or two-mark question turns on one. The extended responses are tagged to whole syllabus sections, because a 15- or 25-mark answer draws on a section rather than a dot point.

How questions are matched. Each question carries a core focus — the elements it is built on, which you cannot answer without — and supporting elements, expected as evidence or context. Selecting a content point or theme shows both, and “Core focus only” narrows to the questions genuinely about it. This is a reading of the questions, not an official NESA mapping.

Section III sets two alternatives. Candidates answer part (a) or part (b), so the thirty alternatives in each option are thirty separate questions for revision but only fifteen papers.

Ticks are private to this device. They are kept in your own browser and are not sent anywhere. Ticking is also how the worksheet is built — tick the questions you want, then print.

Marking criteria. Band criteria are shown for every short answer and extended response, 2011 to 2025, with NESA's sample answers where they were published and the “answers could include” notes where NESA gave them — a recent practice, and much fuller from 2024. They unfold in stages, so a question can be attempted before the marking is read. Two gaps are NESA's own rather than ours: 2020 published no sample answers at all, and the 2013 guidelines set out their “answers could include” notes in a way that cannot be reproduced faithfully here — use the link to that year's guidelines instead.

Papers and marking guidelines. The examination papers and NESA marking guidelines for 2015 to 2025 sit beside this page, so a year link opens the paper itself and the guidelines are one click away. The 2011 to 2014 papers and guidelines are not held here; those links go to the Board of Studies archive, which still serves them.
