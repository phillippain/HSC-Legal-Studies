The *Online Safety Amendment (Digital Duty of Care) Bill 2026* (Cth) was introduced on **15 October
2026**, after an exposure draft released 8 September with an **18-day** consultation. Minister Anika
Wells said on the day of introduction that the government would consider letting Australians **sue**
platforms, including by class action — a right the draft did not contain.

## Reactive to proactive

| | *Online Safety Act 2021* now | The bill |
|---|---|---|
| Core obligation | Non-binding Basic Online Safety Expectations plus industry codes | One enforceable duty to ensure a safe online environment, so far as reasonably practicable |
| Model | Complaints-based takedown | Safety by design and risk management |
| Risk assessment | Only under existing codes | Written assessments at least annually, records kept six years |
| Children | Under-16s barred from accounts on age-restricted platforms | Also a duty to keep under-16s from recommender and endless-feed features |
| Algorithmic feeds | No legislated opt-out | Users over 16 may turn recommendations off |
| Maximum penalty | $49.5m for minimum-age breaches | About $100m per breach, flat cap |
| Right to sue | None | None in the draft; now under consideration |

The duty's "so far as reasonably practicable" test is **modelled on work health and safety law**.
The draft also lets eSafety and approved researchers use false ("sock puppet") accounts to test
compliance. Most duty provisions would start 12 months after Royal Assent.

## Update: the bill passes the House (28 October 2026)

The **Online Safety Amendment (Digital Duty of Care) Bill 2026** was introduced on 15 October and passed the House on
**28 October**, absorbing Kate Chaney's amendment on children's dependence on AI chatbots. The Coalition (Ted
O'Brien) calls it censorship; with the Coalition opposed, Labor needs the **Greens** in the Senate. The Senate
committee takes submissions to 30 October and reports by **20 November 2026**.

| | Now (OSA 2021) or September draft | Bill as introduced |
|---|---|---|
| Core obligation | Codes, standards, Basic Online Safety Expectations | One overarching **duty of care** |
| Defining harms | Draft let the minister add categories | Ministerial power removed; only Parliament can add harms |
| Maximum penalty | About $825,000 | Up to **$108.2 million** per breach |
| Under-16 design | "Reasonable steps" only | Must stop under-16s using recommender systems, endless feeds |
| News and public interest | No exception in the draft | s 25F excludes news and public-interest material ("public interest" undefined) |
| Cost of compliance | Weighed in the draft | "Appropriate and effective" test; cost cannot be considered |
| Right to sue | None | Still absent; Greens seek one, Wells open to it |

A 12-month transition would follow passage before the duty starts and the codes are repealed. The duty implements a
recommendation of Delia Rickard's 2025 review of the Online Safety Act (67 recommendations).

## Update: faith groups push for a right to sue (SMH, 9 November 2026)

More than **80** groups (the article's figure), including the ECAJ and the Islamic Council of Victoria, back adding a
**private right of action** before the Senate votes; the committee reports by **20 November 2026**. An Australia Institute
poll found **72%** support compensation for people harmed by platform content.

| | Bill as passed by the House | What the Greens and advocates want |
|---|---|---|
| Who enforces | eSafety: directions, information powers, civil penalty proceedings | Also individuals suing, including class actions |
| Harms covered | Lists sexual and gender-based violence; no standalone hate category | Add racist, homophobic and religious hate |
| Penalties | Fixed maximums, headline about A$109.2m (Ashurst) | A percentage of global revenue |
| Individual remedy | Complaints to platforms and eSafety | Compensation through the courts |

- **eSafety Commissioner** Julie Inman Grant told Senate Estimates a private right of action is needed; Minister Wells will
  consider it "in good faith" but no amendment has been announced.
- **Google** argues the bill is modelled on WHS law, and the model WHS Act (s 267) confers no civil right of action.
- People can already sue in negligence or under the Australian Consumer Law, but without a specific statutory duty.
- The **Royal Commission on Antisemitism and Social Cohesion** (final report due 18 December 2026) heard criticism of X and
  Telegram; these are not findings. eSafety recorded 27,581 illegal and restricted content complaints in 2025–26.

## Google's privacy warning (AFR, 9 November 2026)

At the Senate hearing on 9 November Google warned that proposed **s 27D** — age checks wherever children could foreseeably meet
pornography — could reach **email and private file storage** such as Gmail and Drive. It sets children's protection (CROC arts 17,
34) against the right to privacy of correspondence (ICCPR art 17). Failing s 27D carries up to **60,000 penalty units**; services
must offer at least **two** age-assurance methods. The headline's "scan" is a conditional warning, not a plan, and the article has
no government or eSafety response. [Class evidence summary](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1gIlpdX02-nfWgHWkfCjsajnzFr2jFppe/view).

## Use it for

- **hr.3.1 (contemporary issue)** — a live issue with every rights actor visible at once: Parliament,
  a statutory regulator, the AHRC, NGOs, the media and the platforms.
- **hr.2.9 / hr.2.7 (statute law, incorporation)** — children's rights under the **CROC** reach
  Australian law only through statute. UN **General Comment 25** (2021) says children's rights apply
  online as offline, and frames them as including participation and information, not protection
  alone. The AHRC urges consulting children on the harm definitions.
- **hr.2.14 (a Charter of Rights)** — the AHRC says the statements of compatibility with human rights
  attached to online safety bills have been weak on digital rights; an argument for and against
  stronger rights machinery.
- **The balance (hr.t.values)** — protection from harm against freedom of expression and privacy. The
  Coalition says vague tests could capture political and religious debate and push platforms to
  over-remove; the AHRC, which **supports** the duty, warns about the same vagueness, broad
  ministerial rule-making and privacy risks from age assurance.
- **chg.2 / chg.3 (law reform)** — the full Commonwealth sequence: the Rickard statutory review
  (67 recommendations, tabled February 2025), a government response in April 2026, an issues paper,
  an exposure draft, submissions, introduction, a Senate inquiry due to report before Parliament
  rises in late November, then a Senate vote Labor can only win with the Greens or the Coalition.
- **Comparison** — the UK *Online Safety Act* allows fines up to £18m or **10% of global turnover**;
  the Rickard review recommended the greater of 5% of global turnover or $50m, where the draft chose
  a flat ~$100m cap.

## Caution

A **class-made summary** of a **news report of a minister's comments** on the day of introduction —
not the bill. Much of the detail comes from law firm analyses of the **September exposure draft**,
which was changed before introduction, so the penalty, the harm categories, the opt-out and the
commencement dates may all differ in the bill as introduced: check the APH bill page and the
explanatory memorandum. The openness to a right to sue is a **statement, not a commitment**, and the
political reactions quoted are partisan. Anything about the bill's fate is unresolved. In the 9 November summary the
"80+ groups" figure and Google's submission could not be verified.
