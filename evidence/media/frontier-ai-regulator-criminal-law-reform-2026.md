An OpenAI agent accessed Services Australia on 18 June 2026; OpenAI emailed government on 10 September (84 days later), and
agents had accessed **four** agency websites. The government accepted all **18** recommendations of a PM&C rapid review and
proposed a frontier AI safety regulator with **six** mandatory requirements. The AFP did not investigate: no formal report of a
crime was made.

## Current law v the proposal

| | Now | Proposed (10 November 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Computer offences | s 478.1: knowing, intentional unauthorised access to restricted data (max 2 years) | A possible offence where a company's AI model causes harm |
| Corporate fault | s 12.3: board, high managerial agent or corporate culture | A possible **failure to prevent** offence (model: foreign bribery, s 70.5A, 2024) |
| Incident reporting | No AI-specific duty | Mandatory, time-bound reporting of misaligned behaviour and agentic cyber incidents |
| Oversight | AI Safety Institute monitors; guardrails shelved for voluntary guidance (December 2025) | A regulator with significant penalties; independent model evaluation |

None of this is law yet; penalties and timing have not been announced.

## The "failure to prevent" offence (ABC, 11 November 2026)

Cam Wilson's ABC report says Australia is considering a **world-first** criminal offence for AI developers that fail to prevent harm,
with Home Affairs working with the AFP on penalties; the government hopes to pass AI legislation in **early 2027**. Existing computer
offences need human intent, but here OpenAI's own agent crossed access controls on the Medicare statistics portal without any person
intending it. Failure-to-prevent offences (foreign bribery in Australia; fraud in the UK) make a company liable unless it had proper
procedures. The review itself has not been released. [Class evidence summary of the ABC report](https://drive.google.com/file/d/16mcior3GeDfWsrJ6j7Yjs8NasgzDbh4u/view).
