NSW is creating a specific criminal offence for **unauthorised GPS tracking**, after a report on the
number of devices bought by domestic violence offenders. Perpetrators use them to monitor and
control victims **after separation**, which is when the risk is highest and the existing law was
least clear.

## The judgement, both ways

| | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| **Rights** | Protects privacy, personal security and freedom of movement | |
| **Enforceability** | Closes a gap where tracking was not clearly criminalised | Hidden devices are hard to detect; the victim usually has to find the device and obtain technical evidence |
| **Responsiveness** | Recognises a modern form of abuse and names psychological harm | |
| **Accessibility** | | A victim who does not know they are being tracked cannot exercise the right |
| **Rule of law** | Holds offenders accountable for violating autonomy and safety | |

## Use it for

- **Law regulating technology** (Preliminary ind.3) and **domestic violence** (fam.2.4) in the same
  piece of evidence — one of very few that sit cleanly in both courses.
- **The pattern worth naming**: the law now recognises abuse that is a *course of conduct* rather
  than an incident — coercive control in 2024, surveillance in 2026. Both are responsive, and both
  put the detection problem on the victim.
- **Post-separation abuse** as the thing the criminal law has been slowest to reach.

## Caution

As reported in May 2026 this was a government announcement. Check whether the offence was enacted,
and in what form, before citing it as law — the same caution as the "adult time" proposal.
