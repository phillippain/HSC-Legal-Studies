Burn-Murdoch's argument is the most useful thing a Legal Studies student can read about **crime statistics and public perception**, because it refuses the two easy answers. It does not say the public is simply wrong, and it does not say the media invented the fear. It says both the falling numbers and the rising fear are measuring something real — just not the same thing.

## The paradox

- Violent crime in **Britain and America** is on course for its lowest rate in decades. US violence and homicide are **down more than half** from their 1990s peaks and approaching record lows.
- Yet British concern about crime has been rising for years, and most Americans say crime is rising locally and nationally.
- In the late 1990s, **25 per cent** of Britons were worried about violent crime; by 2025, **8 per cent**. On that specific question, the public tracked reality precisely.

So people are not globally incapable of reading the trend. Burn-Murdoch cites Will Jennings' 2016 work: people are bad at judging *prevalence* but reasonably good at judging *direction of travel*.

## The resolution: visibility, not volume

The crimes that fell are the ones people rarely see. The crimes that rose are the ones they see constantly.

| Falling | Rising |
|---|---|
| Violent crime (surveys **and** hospital admissions) | Shoplifting — highest in at least 15 years |
| Homicide | "Snatch theft" / theft from the person — highest in at least 15 years |
| | Fare evasion (London and New York) |
| | Dog-bite hospitalisations — more than doubled over 20 years |
| | Careless driving |

Why that changes perception: just over half of violent crime victims **know the perpetrator and have been attacked before**, and much of it happens out of public view, so it reads as distant. Snatch theft is "almost always carried out in public by a stranger on a first-time victim" — so it reads as *this could happen to me*.

Shoplifting does something extra. People witness not only the act but its consequences — security staff, protective casings and locks on everything "from steak to cosmetics". The environment itself becomes a signal that things are out of control, with "no media refraction needed".

Burn-Murdoch links this to US researcher Charles Fain Lehman's claim that a waning violent-crime problem sits alongside a mounting **public disorder** problem, measured through proxies like fare evasion, driving offences and dog attacks. He adds a final compounding factor: arrest, charge and prosecution rates have been **falling in both countries**, feeding a sense that offenders act with impunity.

## Use it for

- **crime.1.8 — factors affecting criminal behaviour.** Situational opportunity and low perceived risk of prosecution, argued from data rather than assertion.
- **crime.1.9 — crime prevention.** The strongest available argument for **situational** prevention being double-edged: locked cabinets and security guards prevent theft *and* broadcast disorder. Good essays use that tension rather than listing CCTV and lighting.
- **Reporting and recording crime, and the dark figure.** Note that the UK claim is backed by *two independent sources* — the Crime Survey for England and Wales (victim survey) and hospital admissions — which is exactly the triangulation students should demand of any crime statistic.
- **Law reform pressure.** The article is a diagnosis of why "tough on crime" announcements keep working when crime is falling. Pair it with the NSW "adult crime, adult time" announcement of September 2026, where police and ministerial pressure built on a perception of escalation.
- A direct **counter-argument to punitive reform**: if the serious crime is already falling, raising maximum penalties is responding to disorder with a tool built for violence.

## Caution

This is **UK and US data only**. Australia is not in it. Do not write "crime is falling" and cite this article as the authority for Australia — use BOCSAR and ABS *Recorded Crime — Victims* for NSW and national figures, which do not move identically. (NSW has its own divergences, including rising domestic-violence assault recording.)

It is also a **newspaper opinion column** in the FT's Data Points series, not peer-reviewed research. The charts are the author's own analysis of official sources (Crime Survey for England and Wales, FBI/Real-Time Crime Index via Jeff Asher, NYPD, TfL, MTA, US CDC, NHS England). Attribute the analysis to Burn-Murdoch, and note that "public disorder" is his and Lehman's constructed category — the proxies (fare evasion, dog bites) are chosen, not standard, and Burn-Murdoch says so himself: it is "hard to track".

The final line is the one to quote and then test: America and Britain "are less dangerous than ever, but more disorderly than they have been in some time."
