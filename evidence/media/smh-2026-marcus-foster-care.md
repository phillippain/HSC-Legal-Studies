Marcus (name changed) is Aboriginal, in his thirties, partially deaf, and suing the **State of NSW in
the Supreme Court**. Records obtained by the masthead run to **nearly 1700 pages** of concerns raised
by teachers, psychologists and caseworkers across his childhood.

## What the records show

- Across **eight years**, the department investigated and **confirmed nine reports** of domestic
  violence, alcohol abuse and extreme neglect in his birth family. He was removed only after his
  father smashed a bottle over his mother's head, by which time he was undernourished with fungal
  lesions, chest infections and decaying teeth.
- After four placements he and three siblings were placed in an **overcrowded home**, sleeping on
  mattresses in the living room. He told caseworkers he was **"slapped and hit"**; a formal
  notification of abuse in care was recorded and **the children were not removed**.
- A psychologist found him **"thin", "wary" and self-harming**, and described the placement as
  **"not ideal" but "adequate"**.
- An untreated ear infection became a life-threatening tumour; he is now partially deaf.
- When he disclosed **sexual abuse** by his foster father to the visiting child safety officer, he was
  **told to stop lying**; on another occasion officers put the allegation to the father, who beat him
  for it.

## The legal question

The State's defence is that an **NGO** placed him and was responsible for vetting and supervising the
carers. The claim tests whether the February 2026 High Court decision in ***AA v The Trustees of the Roman
Catholic Church for the Diocese of Maitland-Newcastle* [2026] HCA 2** (24 February 2026) reaches
foster children. There the Court held an institution owes a **non-delegable duty of care** in respect
of intentional criminal acts by a person it placed in a position of authority, supervision, trust,
care or control — an obligation that also extends to schools. That reopened a path closed by
***Bird v DP (a Pseudonym)* [2024] HCA 41**, which held a priest was not an employee and the diocese
therefore not vicariously liable, and which had forced survivors into reduced settlements.

> "When a child is placed in the care of the Department, the Department assumes responsibility to care
> for the child and decides where that child goes. The child is vulnerable and wholly dependent on the
> Department's decision-making." — Michelle Martin, North Star Law

## Use it for

- **Care and protection** at its sharpest: not an absence of law but a **failure to act on the law
  that existed**. Mandatory reporting worked; the response did not.
- **Enforceability** — the remedy arrives decades later, through tort, and only if a duty of care can
  be established. That is the strongest available argument that the child protection system's
  accountability mechanisms are not contemporaneous.
- **Aboriginal children in out-of-home care**, read with the Family Is Culture material.
- **Non-delegable duty** is the concept to name: a state cannot contract out of a responsibility it
  assumed by taking the child.

## Caution

The article names neither High Court decision. Both were identified and their citations confirmed
from legal-practice commentary on 19 September 2026 — *AA v Trustees* [2026] HCA 2 and *Bird v DP*
[2024] HCA 41 — but the judgments themselves have not been read here, and *AA* is reported to have
departed from *NSW v Lepore*, which is worth checking before the point is pressed in an answer.
