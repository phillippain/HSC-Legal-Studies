The best use of this article in a Legal Studies answer is as the **mirror image** of the coercive-control reporting. The technology is identical. The legal and moral character of its use turns entirely on consent, capacity and purpose — and the law has no clean way to test any of the three inside a family.

## What the article reports

- Sue Easton's 84-year-old father, who has dementia, got lost for more than two hours driving to her house. She found him on **Life360**, directed him by watching his position, then had her husband collect him.
- Robyn Holland checks her 95-year-old stepmother Val Harvey's location "a few times a day" on Apple's **Find My**. "If I see her at the local shops, or the church... then I know she's fine. But if she doesn't move for a few days, then I might give her a call."
- Life360, listed on the ASX, has **80 million global users** since 2008 and built its business tracking children. Its chief executive Chris Hulls said: "Apple has helped make location sharing not creepy, which it once was."
- The company planned a **2026 wearable** with fall detection, automatic family notification, an SOS button, step counts, and a display of when the person last used their phone.

The scale figures the article relies on: six in ten people with dementia will wander at least once; about **35,000 Australians are reported missing each year**; falls are the leading cause of hospitalised injuries and injury deaths among older people, at **77 per cent** and **71 per cent** of incidents in that age group.

## The consent problem, in the participants' own words

Holland's stepmother consented and values it — "It's really given me great comfort. I think it's great that Robyn cares enough to do it." Easton's parents, one with cognitive decline, "knew that it was very much from a position of care."

But University of Sydney digital health expert **Associate Professor Jessica Orchard** identifies where the consent actually sits:

> "You can't negotiate with the app developer. It's very much take it or leave it. I would bet that most people don't read the fine print."

So there are two consents in play, and both are weak. The consent of the tracked person may be given at a point of declining capacity and is hard to withdraw once family practice has formed around it. The consent to the company's data handling is a click-through contract of adhesion. The article notes tracking apps' history of "mishandling of user data and breaches of privacy".

Orchard also makes a hard practical point about **false alarms**: unsophisticated, poorly tested sensors send wrong fall or SOS alerts, and "the more false alarms you get, the more you think, 'Maybe I won't wear that'." A protective device that is not worn protects nobody — a useful line for any effectiveness evaluation of technological safeguards.

## Use it for

- **fam.1.5 / fam.3.2 — rights, obligations and the changing nature of family responsibility.** Care of an ageing parent has no equivalent of parental responsibility: there is no statutory package of rights and duties, so families improvise, and the improvisation is now technological.
- **Capacity and substitute decision-making.** Where capacity is declining, tracking sits between guardianship (formal, supervised, reviewable) and nothing at all. Pair it with NSW guardianship and enduring power of attorney arrangements to show the gap.
- **fam.2.4 — domestic violence.** The strongest paired comparison in the whole Family topic. Put this beside reporting on tracking apps used in coercive control and ask what legal test distinguishes the two. The *Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007* (NSW) s 54D coercive control offence requires a course of conduct intended to coerce or control — intention, not technology, does the work.
- **Law reform and technology.** A clean example of technology outpacing the law: no Australian statute speaks directly to a family member tracking an adult relative with impaired capacity.
- **Privacy.** The *Privacy Act 1988* (Cth) regulates the company's handling of the data, not the family's use of the information — which is where the dignity question actually lives.

## Caution

Published **March 2025**. The wearable described was a 2026 *plan* announced by the company's own chief executive; do not write as though the product exists or has been evaluated. Hulls' claim that competing devices "all suck" is a competitor's marketing, quoted as such.

The article is in the SMH's technology section and reads partly as product coverage: the two families interviewed are both satisfied users, and the piece contains no interview with a tracked person who objected, no legal practitioner, and no older-persons advocacy body. The missing-persons, dementia-wandering and falls statistics are quoted without attribution to a named source — verify them against AIHW and Australian Federal Police missing-persons data before using the numbers in an answer.

Finally, note the framing: the headline treats this as a market opportunity. Nothing in the article tests whether the people being tracked were asked in a way that let them say no.
