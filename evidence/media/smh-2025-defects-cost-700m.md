The *Herald*'s **Shoddy Sydney** series asks how far NSW has come since Opal Tower and Mascot
Towers, at the moment the state is trying to build a great many more apartments.

## The numbers

| | |
|---|---|
| Cost of defective work to NSW home owners (government estimate) | **$700 million a year** |
| Apartment complexes in which defects are found | close to **25%** |
| Work rectification orders for stand-alone homes **not** complied with in the mandated period | **40%** |
| Apartment buildings nationally with water ingress problems (2024 study) | **20–40%**, costing **$235–610 million a year** to fix |
| Building Commission NSW | moved out of Fair Trading in 2023, grown from **40 to 400 staff**, **$59 million a year** to run |
| Investigations into defective homes and units in the past year | **1,100** |

**Waterproofing** is the single biggest problem in NSW construction, and defective or missing
waterproofing causes structural damage, mould and concrete cancer. A 2024 report to the Australian
Building Codes Board attributes it to builders and designers lacking training, weak oversight by
licensing bodies and regulators, and the "squeezing" of compliance checking — especially the rise of
**Design and Construct contracts**, where the client signs a single contract at the start. Its best
line: "over time, the previous construction practice of relying on gravity has been forgotten."

## The argument about whether there is still a crisis

- Former Building Commissioner **David Chandler** said before retiring there was "a deep denial about
  the quality of home construction".
- His successor **James Sherrard**: "No building is built defect-free… If that door handle is set
  50 millimetres higher it's a defect, but it's not an irrecoverable defect." For serious structural
  defects, the Commission injuncts the developer so they cannot get an occupation certificate.
- Regulation minister **Anoulack Chanthivong** does not think there is a crisis — more inspections
  means more notices: "I'm pleased that we are finding defects… it means our enforcement regime is
  actually working."
- **Bronwyn Weir**, who wrote the landmark 2018 report and helped draft the **Design and Building
  Practitioners Act**, calls it "nation-leading" but "also, in some respects, a hopeful way of
  improving the quality of design."

## Use it for

- **shel.2.4 (securing other types of shelter)** — the current state of the problem, with figures.
  The **40% non-compliance with rectification orders** is the sharpest enforceability fact available:
  the order is the remedy, and two in five are ignored within the period.
- **Evaluating a reform you already have records for** — the DBP Act and the RAB Act are in the
  library as the response to Opal and Mascot. This is the evidence of what they achieved: real
  regulatory capacity (40 → 400 staff), and defects still in a quarter of complexes.
- **shel.2.10 / chg.2 (the media as an agency of reform)** — a newspaper investigation naming
  fraudulent construction qualifications as a concern and forcing three officials to respond. Rare
  and usable.
- **The collision (shel.3.1 / shel.3.4)** — two housing problems pulling against each other: not
  enough homes, and defects in the ones being built. Good for the argument that supply targets and
  quality regulation are in tension.
- **Resource efficiency** — $59 million a year to regulate against $700 million a year in defects.

## Caution

**Journalism with a campaign behind it**: the series is arguing that the government is downplaying a
crisis, and the April 11 piece says so in its headline. Treat the officials' quotes as the other
side of a contested question rather than as concessions. The **$700 million** is a government
estimate the article does not source precisely; the 20–40% water-ingress range is from "a major
2024 study" that is not named. "Defects found in close to 25% of apartment complexes" counts
complexes where any defect was found, and Sherrard's door-handle point is that a defect is not
necessarily serious — both figures need that qualification. From **April 2025**: check Building
Commission NSW's current data, and whether the legislative changes the minister referred to have
been introduced.
