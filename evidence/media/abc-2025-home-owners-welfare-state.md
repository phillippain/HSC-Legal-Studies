Professors **Peter Siminski** (UTS) and **Roger Wilkins** (Melbourne Institute) argue that standard
income measures ignore the income owner-occupiers derive from their homes — **imputed rent** (the
value of living rent-free) and **accrued capital gains** — and that Australia's tax system, which
taxes neither, is "a major driver of inequality, undermining the redistributive role of government".

## The figures

- Average disposable income of **outright owners is 34% above renters'**; count housing income and
  it is **86%** above.
- Treasury forgoes about **$50 billion a year** by exempting the family home from capital gains tax;
  imputed rent was taxed in Australia between 1915 and 1923 and never since.
- Over 23 years, including housing income cuts the **redistributive impact of income tax by 40.5%**
  and of welfare transfers by **18.9%** (the home is excluded from the Age Pension assets test).
- Including housing income moves Australia from **16th to 10th** highest inequality in the OECD.
- The concessions for owner-occupied housing are of similar size to those for superannuation and
  far larger than those for investment property — which get most of the public attention.
- "Australia's tax and transfer system could be labelled a 'home owners' welfare state'" (Siminski)
  — a play on the "wage earners' welfare state" of the twentieth century.

## Use it for

- **shel.3.1 (affordability)** — the demand-side argument: tax-free housing draws money into housing
  and lifts prices, "which makes it harder for younger people to break into home ownership".
- **shel.3.2 / shel.1.3** — discrimination by wealth rather than by attribute: a legal system that
  rewards those who already own, and an obligation to provide shelter met unevenly.
- **ls.4.1 / shel.t.reform** — the reform nobody proposes: "owner-occupied housing is the elephant in
  the room in current debates" (Siminski). Set against the 2026 Budget, which touched negative
  gearing and the CGT discount but not the family home.
- **Media as agenda-setter (shel.2.10)** — academic research reaching the public through the ABC on
  the day the Conversation piece appeared.

## Caution

A **news report of one research paper**, not the paper; the OECD ranking is the authors' own
exercise, not repeated for other countries. The $50 billion is a Treasury tax-expenditure estimate.
Nothing here proposes a specific reform, so use it for the problem, not the response. The class
application sheets built on it ("Application in Action" and the "Income Inequality & Affordability"
table) are student exercises, not evidence.
