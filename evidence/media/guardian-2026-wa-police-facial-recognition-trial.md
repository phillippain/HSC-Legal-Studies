In a trial that ended on **31 October 2026**, WA Police scanned **1,380,882** faces from marked vans over **100 deployments at 43 locations** (including three operations at the Perth royal show) and matched them against a police alert list. The result: **122 arrests**, **146** engagements with registered sex offenders, **10** welfare checks and **nine incorrect alerts**. Vans are off the road while police run an internal evaluation.

## The claims and the checks

| Claim | What the checking found |
|---|---|
| Error rate was **0.00065%** | The arithmetic is right (9 ÷ 1,380,882) but it divides false alerts by *scans*, not by *alerts*. At the three-month update 8 of 209 alerts (about 3.8%) were wrong. The final number of alerts is not published |
| Non-matches are pixelated and deleted at once | A police claim. The privacy impact assessment says non-matching data is deleted at once but does not mention pixelation. No independent audit was found |
| Scans were done "with no reasonable suspicion" (Civil Liberties Australia) | The scans are indiscriminate and the assessment names no specific statutory power. Reasonable suspicion (s 4, *Criminal Investigation Act 2006* (WA)) is the test for powers such as move-on orders (s 27) |
| Higher false-match rates for darker skin tones | NIST (2019) found higher false positives for Asian and African American faces in many algorithms, results varying by algorithm. The vendor's own bias results for this trial were not found |
| An officer reviews every alert before approaching anyone | The assessment says alerts are reviewed by trained officers before engagement. An alert alone is not grounds to arrest |

## The legal frame

- **No specific statute** authorises the scans. WA Police rely on the *Privacy and Responsible Information Sharing Act 2024* (WA), which commenced on 1 July 2026, after the trial began; the Information Commissioner (OIC WA) said it was not part of the design and warned of "collective privacy harms".
- The Commonwealth *Privacy Act 1988* does not cover State police. When Human Rights Watch last wrote (2021) WA had no human rights act; its status in November 2026 was not checked.
- Comparators: the OAIC's *Clearview AI* determination (2021), *Bridges v South Wales Police* (UK Court of Appeal, 2020) and the EU AI Act's limits on real-time police biometric identification.
