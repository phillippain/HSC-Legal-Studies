The counter-argument record. Every other item in this part of the library is about renters; this one
is about what landlords did when holding costs rose and tenancy law tightened — and it matters
because, if investors sell, the **rental supply** falls.

## The CoreLogic numbers (December 2024)

- **About 8,300** investor-owned properties listed for sale across **Melbourne**, up **6.6%** year on
  year and **14%** above the five-year average. In **Sydney**, **more than 6,000** ex-rental
  properties, up **1.7%** year on year and **2.8%** above the five-year average.
- **Capital growth for apartments** last year: **Sydney +1.8%**, **Melbourne −3%** — against
  **Brisbane +16.6%**, **Adelaide +16.9%**, **Perth +22.7%**.
- Rents actually **fell**: Melbourne **−0.5%**, Sydney **−0.2%** year on year, as demand eased with
  lower overseas migration and rising household size.
- **The cash-flow gap.** In Melbourne, median weekly mortgage repayments of **$896** (at a 6.45%
  investor rate) against rental income of **$604** — a **$292-a-week shortfall**. In **Sydney**,
  **$1,384** in repayments against **$773** in rent — a **$611-a-week** gap.
- Investor-owned properties were **31.2%** of all Melbourne listings (five-year average 29.1%), and
  **54.8%** of stock in the Melbourne CBD (five-year average 49.7%). In **Parramatta**, nearly 400
  investor-owned apartments hit the market in December, **54% of all listings**; in central Sydney,
  536 apartments took the ex-rental share to **51.6%** (five-year average 47.8%).
- The table of areas with the highest share of investor-owned listings: **Melbourne city 54.8%**
  (from 52.3%), **Darebin–North 45.5%** (38.5%), **Chatswood–Lane Cove 44.9%** (39.1%),
  **Darebin–South 44.7%** (41.2%), **Moreland–North 43.3%** (34.0%), **Ryde–Hunters Hill 41.7%**
  (37.4%), **Manly 37.0%** (32.3%), **Blacktown–North 20.6%** (16.8%).

## The attribution

**Eliza Owen**, CoreLogic's head of research: "Melbourne is relatively unfavourable for investors,
not just because of the new tax policies, but also very sluggish returns… Property holding costs have
increased for investors because of the **reduction in the threshold to pay land tax** at the start of
2024. Some people may be cutting their losses and are trying to exit that market now." The article
adds that **Victoria's higher taxes on property investors and rental laws requiring minimum rental
standards have added extra costs and depleted landlords' cash flows**.

**Arjun Paliwal** (InvestorKit): some investors are selling **at a loss**, having had "no capital
gains now for almost a decade, if not sometimes for the past 15 years". A Sydney agent: "Unfortunately,
investors are still looking to sell than buy."

One silver lining, and it is in the article: "As the door closes on some investors, the opportunity
could beckon **aspiring home buyers** as affordability improves in Sydney and Melbourne."

## Use it for

- **shel.2.2 — the unintended-consequence argument, with a source.** The strongest objection to
  stronger tenancy law is that it reduces rental supply, and here **CoreLogic's own head of research**
  links the exodus partly to **minimum rental standards and land tax**. Use it to make an evaluation
  genuinely two-sided instead of asserting that reform is costless.
- **But read it carefully, because it cuts both ways** — the same article says apartment **capital
  growth was 1.8% in Sydney and −3% in Melbourne** while Perth returned 22.7%, and that **rents fell**
  in both cities. The dominant cause looks like **poor returns and interest rates**, not tenancy law.
  A student who notices that is arguing at band 6.
- **Resource efficiency and who bears the cost** — a **$611-a-week** gap in Sydney between rent and
  mortgage. Negative gearing and capital gains concessions exist precisely because of that gap, which
  links this record to the tax records in the library.
- **Where a landlord sells, a renter moves (shel.2.2)** — pair it directly with the eviction research
  record: "end of a fixed term because the owner is selling" is now a **valid ground** for
  termination under the 2024 amendments, so an investor exit produces lawful evictions.
- **Markets are not uniform (shel.3.1)** — Brisbane, Adelaide and Perth were booming while Sydney and
  Melbourne apartments stalled. Useful against any claim that "the housing crisis" is one thing.
- **Supply as a policy lever** — the article's own suggestion that investor selling improves
  **first-home buyer** affordability is the trade-off at the centre of housing policy: the same stock
  cannot be both an affordable purchase and a secure rental.

## Caution

The **AFR** reporting property data, for an investor readership — the framing is sympathetic to
landlords and there is **no tenant or tenant advocate quoted**. The land tax and minimum-standards
point is **Victorian**, not NSW: Victoria cut its land tax threshold from the start of 2024 and has
minimum rental standards, so **do not apply this explanation to NSW's 2024 amendments**, which are a
different reform in a different state. The causal claim is **correlation plus expert opinion**, not
analysis — CoreLogic and a buyer's agency both have commercial interests in the market they comment
on. All figures are **December 2024** on a **one-month** snapshot of listings, which is volatile;
investor listings are also not the same as properties **leaving** the rental pool, since a buyer may
be another investor. The source PDF is a **full newspaper page** that also carries unrelated property
coverage. Interest rates, rents and capital growth have all moved since February 2025 — check current
CoreLogic data before quoting any of it as the present position.
