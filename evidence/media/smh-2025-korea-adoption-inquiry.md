A **landmark South Korean inquiry** found systemic fraud and abuse in that country's adoption
programme, including **falsified orphan registrations**, producing a profit-driven **"mass
exportation of children"** with minimal procedural oversight. The Seoul agency at the centre of it
facilitated thousands of adoptions to Australia.

Labor pledged that, if re-elected, it would ask the **Department of Social Services** — which is
responsible for intercountry adoption — to investigate and report to the minister. Adoptees, through
the Australian-Korean Rights Group, had campaigned for the Korean inquiry to finish its work and for
Australia to examine its own practices.

The article notes the shape of that promise precisely: an **internal departmental investigation**,
rather than a parliamentary inquiry conducted at arm's length with the power to hold **public
hearings**.

## Use it for

- **Adoption** as a contemporary issue with an international dimension, and the one that shows what
  intercountry adoption can go wrong at scale.
- **The choice of mechanism is the argument.** An internal review and a parliamentary inquiry are
  both "responses"; they differ in independence, in transparency, and in whether anyone can be
  compelled to answer. Naming that difference is a stronger evaluation than saying the government
  "responded".
- **CROC art 21** on adoption and art 7 on identity, and the limits of international law where the
  wrong was done in another state decades ago.
- **NGOs and affected people as agents of reform** — the inquiry happened because adoptees organised
  and the media carried it.

## Caution

Reported in April 2025 as an **election pledge**. Check whether the investigation was commissioned,
what form it took and what it found before citing it as a completed response — the difference
between announced and delivered is the point of the piece.
