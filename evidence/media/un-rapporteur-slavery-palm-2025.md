**Tomoya Obokata**, the UN Special Rapporteur on contemporary forms of slavery, reported after an
in-country visit that Australia's legal approach to protecting human rights is **"piecemeal"**, and
that migrant workers face **"alarming and sometimes severe patterns of exploitation"** by employers,
labour hire companies and migration agents — deceptive recruitment, excessive wage deductions,
hazardous conditions, harassment, threats, violence and sexual assault.

The **PALM scheme** (Pacific Australia Labour Mobility) is a temporary visa program for workers from
the Pacific Islands and Timor-Leste, with about **31,500** employed, mainly in agriculture and meat
processing.

## What the reporting found

- A former workplace supervisor described a Tongan worker docked an hour's pay for leaning instead
  of kneeling while budding mandarin trees — the employer **watching through a gun scope**. "They
  would work until they couldn't walk." A worker who had a dehydration-induced heart attack was
  **flown home rather than placed on WorkCover**.
- A NSW parliamentary inquiry into the risk of slavery for temporary migrant workers (June 2025)
  heard from Wagga GP **Dr Trudi Beck** that about **95%** of visa workers she saw chose an abortion
  "not because of personal choice" but to keep their visa — because pregnancy would breach the work
  conditions their visa depends on.
- A former Leeton mayor described a woman whose income, accommodation and health cover were all tied
  to her employer; she left after becoming pregnant, gave birth two months premature with no access
  to health services, and the baby was taken into state care. It was the **second child** of hers
  removed in Australia.
- Advocates described a **coerced marriage** on a farm in 2020, the employer allegedly telling a
  couple they would lose their visas if they did not marry.

## Use it for

- **hr.3.1** — the contemporary issue with the strongest current evidence, and the version of modern
  slavery that is happening now in Australia rather than offshore in a supply chain.
- **hr.2.2 / inv.6.3 (the United Nations, obligations of nation-states)** — a **special rapporteur
  conducting a country visit** is the UN mechanism students most often get wrong. It is scrutiny and
  publicity, not enforcement: the report binds nothing, which is the point to make about state
  sovereignty.
- **"Piecemeal" is the quotable finding** — it is the UN saying what the charter-of-rights debate is
  about. Pair with the *Modern Slavery Act 2018* record: Australia legislated about **corporate
  supply chains** while this was happening under a government-run visa scheme.
- **Where the exploitation comes from** — tying income, housing and health cover to a single
  employer, with a visa that depends on continuing to work. That is the mechanism, and it is a
  policy choice rather than a failure of enforcement. Connects straight to the family-violence visa
  records.
- **Non-legal responses (hr.2.13, chg.2.2)** — a newspaper investigation and a parliamentary inquiry
  surfacing what the regulator had not.

## Caution

A **newspaper investigation**, not the report: the Special Rapporteur's report is public and should
be read before quoting his findings, and the article does not give its title or date precisely. The
**95% figure is one GP's estimate** of her own patients given in evidence to an inquiry, not a
statistic — it should always be attributed to Dr Beck and described as her clinical impression. The
supervisor is **anonymous**, the farm is under new management, and the coerced-marriage account is
an allegation. Individual accounts given to an inquiry are not findings. The material is distressing
and involves identifiable vulnerable people; use it carefully and do not add detail beyond what is
published.
