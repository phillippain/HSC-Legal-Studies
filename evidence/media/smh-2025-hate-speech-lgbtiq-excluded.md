After a spate of antisemitic violence in Sydney, Premier **Chris Minns** announced in February 2025
that hate speech would be criminalised — **against the recommendation of the state's expert legal
body**. The package also banned protest outside places of worship and made graffitiing a Nazi symbol
on or near a synagogue a jailable offence.

But only speech that **intentionally incited race-based hate** was covered. Minns conceded that
**faith groups and the LGBTIQ+ community would not be protected**, because wholesale changes to the
anti-vilification legislation "would be too difficult to achieve quickly".

> "We were faced with a situation where we're looking at naked racism on our streets today… I made a
> decision that I wanted to progress the hate speech laws as it applies to race immediately."

Independent MP **Alex Greenwich** called the decision **"cruel"** — and disclosed that the AFP had
notified him the previous month of a series of alleged homophobic threats against him.

## Use it for

- **hr.3.1 / hr.2.9 (statute law)** — a precise, recent example of **how far a legal response
  reaches and who it leaves out**. The gap is not an oversight; the Premier explained it.
- **chg.1.1 / chg.3.2 (changing values, executive methods)** — reform driven by a crisis and by
  political will rather than by review. The expert legal body advised against it and was overridden;
  the thing that moved was an outbreak of violence. One of the clearest illustrations available that
  **speed and scope trade off**.
- **chg.4.2 (limitations of reform)** — a reform that protects one group from vilification while
  leaving another exposed is a readymade evaluation paragraph. The spokesperson for the excluded
  group being a sitting MP with police-notified threats against him makes the point without
  rhetoric.
- **Equality before the law (inv.1.4, inv.7.1)** — if the wrong is incitement to hatred, why does
  the attribute of the victim decide whether it is a crime?
- **Pair with *Wertheim v Haddad*** — the same conduct reached through the **Commonwealth civil**
  route under s 18C while NSW built a **state criminal** one, each with different coverage, remedies
  and standards of proof. Together they answer "how are rights protected in Australia?" far better
  than either alone.

## Caution

**February 2025, written the day the announcement was made** — before the bill was drafted, debated
or passed. The NSW legislation as enacted may differ in scope, and later amendments may have
extended protection to other attributes: **check the Act before saying who is or is not covered
now**, and use the `nsw-hate-crime-laws-2025-26` record for the law itself. The article is built
around one critic and is written as political conflict; the government's reasoning is given in a
single quote. "Against a recommendation from the state's expert legal body" is the journalist's
characterisation — find the NSW Law Reform Commission's actual advice before relying on it.
Greenwich's account of threats against him concerns allegations that were before the courts.
