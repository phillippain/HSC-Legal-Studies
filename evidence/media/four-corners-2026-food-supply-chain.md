The episode tracks **one violation across two jurisdictions**. Ghana gives the offshore supply-chain
problem — state sovereignty, transnational corporations, the limits of international law. Australia
removes the distance: the same model, operating domestically, under laws described as world-leading.

| Ghana — cocoa | Australia — horticulture |
|---|---|
| Child labour (CROC art 32, ILO C182) | Visa-tied and undocumented workers |
| Below-living-income poverty — **fewer than 5%** of cocoa farmers earn a living income | **Under $7–12 an hour**; wage theft |
| Certification and "mass balance" | Untraceable labour-hire syndicates |
| **Treaties bind states, not corporations** | **Rights exist but cannot safely be invoked** |

Children aged **9–13** were filmed during school hours with machetes and spraying pesticide without
protection. Workers described 40°C heat, denied bathroom breaks, no PPE. A PNG worker calls it
21st-century slavery: no voice, no power.

## The organising argument

**Recognition — effective.** Eight treaties and conventions ratified by both states; ILO C182 is
near-universal. **Promotion — partially effective.** Reporting duties, commissioners, certification,
media, NGOs. **Enforcement — failing.** No penalties under the Commonwealth Act, no wage-theft
prosecutions, no traceable employer, no judicial remedy for offshore victims.

## Use it for

- **The contemporary human rights issue**, argued structurally: the failure is not of *recognition*
  but of **enforcement architecture** — obligations attach to states while the harm is generated by
  transnational corporations.
- **State sovereignty** (`hr.2.1`) made concrete rather than definitional.
- **The role of the media** (`hr.2.6`) — and of NGOs — where no legal mechanism reaches.
