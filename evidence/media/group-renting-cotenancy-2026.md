A 7.30 report on extended families and friends applying for rentals **as a group**. Domain's
September 2026 quarter put the capital-city median house rent at **$700** a week (unchanged over
the quarter) and vacancy rates at **0.3% (Hobart, Darwin) to 1.5% (Canberra)**, near historic lows
(SQM measured **1.4%** nationally for September, up from 1.0% in March; Cotality 2.0% — the methods
differ); **30.6%** of occupied private dwellings were rented at the 2021 Census; social housing has
fallen to **4.0%** of households in 2025, from 4.7% in 2013. One Brisbane agent puts group
applications at **one in five** and applicants per home up from 10 to 15–20; one Perth renter's rent
rose from **$315 to $715** a week since 2019 (**127%**). On 14 October 2026 both Labor and the
Coalition rejected rent caps.

## What the 2024–25 NSW reforms changed, and what they did not

| | Before | After (Oct 2024 / May 2025) |
|---|---|---|
| Ending a lease | 'No grounds' permitted | Valid reason with evidence — sale, renovation, owner moving in |
| Notice (no breach) | Shorter for some leases | 90 days periodic or fixed term over six months; 60 days if six months or less |
| Rent increases | No single rule | Once in any 12 months, none in the first year, from 31 Oct 2024 |
| Application costs | Background check fees charged | Banned; one fee-free rent payment option required |
| Pets | Landlord could simply refuse | Valid reason needed; no reply in 21 days is approval |
| Bonds | New bond each move | Smart Rental Bonds statewide, same tenants, $25 transfer fee; from 21 Sep 2026 new agreements record each co-tenant's bond contribution |
| Domestic abuse | Departing victim had to notify co-tenants | From 21 Sep 2026 the landlord tells co-tenants within 7 days; under **s 79** a co-tenant barred by a final AVO loses the tenancy and NCAT can recognise a remaining occupant as tenant |
| **Co-tenant liability** | Landlord can pursue **any one** co-tenant for the full debt | **Unchanged** |

**No reform caps the amount of a rent increase** — only the frequency. A large one-off rise remains
lawful, and researchers say the June 2025 change letting landlords evict for renovations on a
written statement alone is a loophole in the no-grounds reform. The first mandatory End of Tenancy Survey (8 Oct 2026) found about **85%** of NSW leases
were ended by renters, not landlords.

## How a group tenancy actually works in NSW

Everyone named on the lease is a **co-tenant**; others are sub-tenants (who rent from a head-tenant)
or occupants; boarders and lodgers are outside the Act. Co-tenants are **jointly and severally**
liable, so the landlord can recover all the rent or damage from one of them. A periodic co-tenant
leaving gives **21 days' notice** to the landlord and each co-tenant (**s 101**); adding a co-tenant
needs the landlord's written consent, refusable for overcrowding (**ss 74–75**); a leaving co-tenant's
bond share must be repaid within 14 days (**s 174**). Under **s 102**, **NCAT** may end a co-tenant's
tenancy or the whole agreement in special circumstances — but NCAT generally **cannot** resolve
money disputes between co-tenants apart from bond payouts, so unpaid bills go to Community Justice
Centres or the Local Court. NCAT payment and compensation orders are each capped at **$15,000**
(s 187); matters are listed in about 4–6 weeks.

## Use it for

- **shel.2.2 / shel.2.3 (rights and obligations, protection for tenants)** — joint and several
  liability is the specific legal content here, and the article's silence on it is itself the teaching
  point: the market pushes people into the arrangement that carries the most risk.
- **shel.2.5 / shel.2.6 (dispute resolution, tribunals)** — a precise example of NCAT's **limits**,
  not just its role: s 102 can end a co-tenancy, but a co-tenant chasing a housemate for rent is in
  the Local Court.
- **shel.3.1 / shel.3.4 (affordability, social housing)** — a Perth median house rent of $750 against
  one renter's rise from $315 a week since 2019; social housing has shrunk as a share of stock, so
  private renting has no alternative.
- **chg.3 (mechanisms of reform)** — National Cabinet's **A Better Deal for Renters** (August 2023)
  set shared standards that **each state then legislates itself**, which NSW did in 2024–25. A clean
  example of cooperative federalism producing uneven law. NSW ruled out ACT-style rent caps in 2023.
- **Competing responses** — the Tenants' Union wants increases above 3% to need tenant agreement or
  Tribunal review, five-year minimum fixed terms, and protections extended to boarders; the ACTU
  wants two-year minimum lease offers and public housing at 6% of stock.

## Caution

A **class-made summary** of a **human-interest report, not data**. The headline "one in five
applications" is **one Brisbane agency's own experience**, and the Perth rent rise is one renter's
account (the first class summary gave it as $760/141%; the two later ones as $715/127%, which the
arithmetic supports — check the article before quoting). The "31% of Australians rent" line is a **2021 household**
measure, not people, and is five years old; the 1% national vacancy rate and the 3–5% social housing
share were not verified. The claim that the May 2026 Budget tax changes are driving investors out is
**disputed**: the changes start **1 July 2027**, grandfather existing holdings and exempt new builds,
and Domain's own report described rents as **stalling**. Only an agent, an investor-landlord and an
academic are quoted — no tenant advocate, no minister. Tenancy law is **state-based**: the cases are
in WA and Queensland while the law set out here is NSW, checked against the Act as made (2010) —
confirm against the current consolidation.
