The quiet counterpart to every offshore surrogacy story: **altruistic surrogacy inside the family**,
and it is becoming more common.

- **Louise Davis**, 52, a Victorian midwife with four adult children, carried her granddaughter
  **Riley**, born in April 2025, for her son **Joshua Wilson**, 30, and his husband **Dean
  Tattersall**, who used a friend's eggs to create embryos. She offered after they told her they were
  looking at **commercial options abroad**. Being pregnant in her fifties was "very difficult" but
  "definitely worth it".
- **Maree Arnold**, 58, from Tasmania, has carried **two** grandchildren for her daughter Meagan
  White, who was born **without a uterus but still has eggs** — Winston in 2022 and Phoebe in May
  2025. They first explored a **uterus transplant**, with Ms Arnold donating hers, before she
  suggested "maybe I should just carry".
- Actor **Olympia Valance** revealed her mother repeatedly offered — "My mum continually was like,
  'I'll carry the baby for you'" — though she and her husband did not take it up: "I don't think my
  husband would have been able to handle it. I don't think I would have been able to handle it,
  actually."

## The legal and medical frame

- Commercial surrogacy is illegal in Australia; **altruistic surrogacy, where expenses are covered
  but the surrogate is not paid**, is permitted.
- In **Victoria** a surrogate must be **over 25 and have already had at least one baby** — criteria
  Ms Davis met.
- **Fertility Society of Australia and New Zealand** board member **Dr Manuela Toledo**, who has
  treated several "surrogate grandmas", called it "an incredible gift" but "quite complex": the issue
  is whether the surrogate is **fit enough**, with pregnancy in a woman's sixties **not advisable**
  and "too many health concerns" past 60.
- Surrogacy lawyer **Sarah Jefford** had advised **five groups with a surrogate grandmother in the
  last 18 months**, and notes that when the babies arrive the **grandmothers are initially listed as
  the mother on the birth certificate**. "They all have a bit of a laugh about it … But it's pretty
  weird."

## Use it for

- **fam.3.3 — the altruistic model actually working.** Every other record in this part of the library
  is about offshore arrangements going wrong. This is the lawful pathway, and it shows what makes it
  work: a willing relative, a pre-existing relationship, no money.
- **The birth certificate point is the best thing here (fam.1.1 / fam.3.2)** — the **grandmother is
  registered as the mother** until a parentage order transfers status. It demonstrates in one fact
  that **legal parentage follows the birth, not the intention or the genetics**, and that the
  parentage order is what closes the gap. Use it to introduce how the *Surrogacy Act* works.
- **What "altruistic" costs (accessibility)** — Louise Davis offered because her son was looking at
  **commercial options abroad**, and Maree Arnold's family had already lost a baby carried by a
  surrogate in Canada. The lawful route depends on **having a relative willing and able to do this**,
  which most people do not. A precise equality-of-access argument.
- **Eligibility rules as protection (fam.3.1 / inv.1.5)** — over 25, a prior birth, and a clinical
  judgement that pregnancy past 60 is unsafe. Good for whether regulation protects the surrogate or
  restricts her autonomy, with a real clinician quoted on both sides.
- **Changing family structures (fam.1.4)** — two men and a friend's eggs; a daughter born without a
  uterus; a grandmother as gestational carrier. The definition of "family" doing real work.
- **Ethics without villains (fam.t.values)** — Olympia Valance **declined** her mother's offer. Worth
  a sentence: consent within a family is complicated by exactly the closeness that makes the offer
  possible.
- **Non-legal responses (fam.2.6)** — the Fertility Society's guidance and clinic screening are doing
  work no statute does.

## Caution

A **tabloid fertility feature**, not research: the claim that surrogate grandmothers are "becoming
more common" rests on **one lawyer's five matters in 18 months** and one clinician's impression, so
do not imply a trend with numbers behind it. The participants **consented to be named and
photographed**; even so, keep to what they said. The rules quoted are **Victorian** (over 25, prior
birth) — NSW's criteria under the *Surrogacy Act 2010* (NSW) differ, including the surrogate being at
least 25 and the intended parents at least 18, so check the Act for the jurisdiction you are writing
about rather than reusing these. "Expenses are covered but they are not paid" is the right summary of
altruistic surrogacy, but what counts as a **reimbursable expense** is precisely what is contested
and is before the **ALRC**, whose final report went to the Attorney-General by **11 September 2026**
and was not yet public in October 2026. Published **August 2025**; the births described are recent
and the children are identified by first name.
