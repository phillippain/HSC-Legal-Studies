Vincent Hurley, Lecturer in Criminology (police and policing) at Macquarie University, writing after
**four domestic violence-related deaths in one weekend in NSW** — women aged 23, 90 and 70 in
separate incidents, and a five-year-old child in the Blue Mountains, where the children's mother is
a suspect. Two years after the Prime Minister called domestic and gender-based violence a "national
crisis".

## The argument: the money follows the crisis, not its causes

| Point | Figure as reported |
|---|---|
| Domestic violence cases, 2024–25 | up **8%** (ABS) — the highest rate since national reporting began |
| Who is proceeded against | about **78%** of 97,800 DFV offenders were men |
| Commonwealth DFV spending | began in 2010–11 at **$1.8 million**; $643.9 million in 2023–24 — about **0.025% of GDP** |
| 2015–16 federal allocation | $117.3 million, of which **$21.7 million** for prevention |
| 2021–22 federal allocation | $151.8 million, of which **$92.5 million** for prevention |
| NSW, June 2026 | $184.1 million more for DFV, of which **$19.3 million** for men's behaviour change |
| Queensland, July 2026 | **$40 million cut** from DFV prevention, including men's programs |

NSW Police Deputy Commissioner Peter McKenna said domestic violence "doesn't know postcodes";
Premier Chris Minns said Australians could not "become numb"; the Opposition Leader said "something
is broken". Hurley's point is that the men who commit this violence "are there, in plain sight", and
that until funding challenges their behaviour **before the fact**, the problem will continue.

## Caution

Commentary by an academic arguing a case, not a government report: the funding comparisons come from
a MinterEllison analysis he cites rather than from budget papers, and he notes that DFV data is
itself unreliable because most victims never report. Reporting on unresolved criminal matters —
nothing here is proven against any accused person.
