Residents of **Opal Tower** at Sydney Olympic Park were evacuated on **Christmas Eve 2018** after
cracks appeared in precast concrete panels. The developers argued the structural failures were "an
isolated incident". This piece, written days later, tested that.

## What the reporting established

- A **2012 UNSW City Futures Research Centre** study found **85% of strata title owners in NSW** said
  there were defects in the construction of their building.
- An evacuation on this scale was unusual — the AFR reached back to a 2009 gas explosion at Bondi
  Junction for a comparison — but **defects themselves are not unusual at all**.
- Even counting the affected buildings is hard: owners are "often subject to **private settlements
  that demand confidentiality** of them."
- **Karen Stiles**, Owners Corporation Network: "This is a David & Goliath fight no owner expects, or
  should be expected, to enter into… they are left by a broken system to fend for themselves."
- **Louise Stewart**, Australian Subcontractors Association: subcontractors complete about **90% of
  the work** on a building and frequently suffer delayed or incomplete payment, and "the financial
  setup of the industry can encourage shoddy work."
- The AFR's own framing: the failure revealed the vulnerability of builders and subcontractors **and
  the lack of protections for consumers who buy apartments** — one of its companion pieces was headed
  "fridge buyers have better consumer protection than apartment buyers".

## Use it for

- **shel.2.4** — the origin point of the whole NSW defects story, and the record that stops a student
  treating Opal Tower as a one-off. **85% of strata owners reporting defects** is the number that
  reframes it as systemic.
- **Why the problem is invisible (shel.2.5)** — confidentiality clauses in private settlements mean
  the dispute resolution that does work **suppresses the evidence that it was needed**. An unusually
  good point about the limits of private remedies.
- **Consumer protection (dis.2.3)** — the fridge comparison is a serious argument: statutory
  guarantees for a $900 appliance are stronger than for a $900,000 apartment. Pairs with
  *Brookfield Multiplex*, where the High Court held the builder owed no duty of care in negligence to
  the owners corporation for pure economic loss.
- **chg.1 (conditions giving rise to reform)** — this is the event. The **Design and Building
  Practitioners Act 2020** and the **Residential Apartment Buildings (Compliance and Enforcement
  Powers) Act 2020** follow from it, as does the appointment of the first **Building Commissioner**
  in 2019. Read the sequence as Opal (Dec 2018) → Mascot (June 2019) → Commissioner → two Acts →
  *Pafburn* (2024) → the 2025 *Shoddy Sydney* findings.
- **shel.2.6 / shel.2.9 (industry bodies and NGOs)** — the Owners Corporation Network and the
  Subcontractors Association both quoted as the non-legal response.

## Caution

**January 2019**, days after the evacuation and long before any investigation reported — so the
causes described are early reporting, not findings, and the Opal Tower investigations that followed
should be read for what actually happened. The **85% figure is from 2012** and is **self-reported by
owners** about any defect, so it is not a measure of serious or structural defect; the 2025 SMH
figure of defects in about a quarter of complexes is the more current and more careful number. The
industry voices quoted each represent an interest. The AFR is a **business paper** writing for
investors, and the piece is partly about risk to developers.
