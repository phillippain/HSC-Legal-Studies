Two boys — John, 14, and William, 11 — have lived continuously with a non-Aboriginal couple since
2011 and 2013. At placement, their Indigenous heritage was not considered.

Before the NSW government can support their adoption, it must make **"reasonable inquiries"** as to
whether they are Aboriginal children, because NSW law sets an order of preference: an Aboriginal
child should be adopted, **first**, by someone of the Aboriginal community their birth parent
belongs to; if that is not practicable, by an adoptive parent from another Aboriginal community; and
only if neither is feasible, by non-Aboriginal parents.

Since about 2011, maternal and paternal family members had made **dozens of conflicting
representations** to Department of Communities and Justice caseworkers about the boys' possible
Aboriginal descent. Their mother did not previously identify as Aboriginal and now "strongly
identifies" as Indigenous after discovering the heritage of a great-grandmother she said was part of
the Stolen Generation.

A NSW judge determined they are **not** Aboriginal children under the law, clearing the likely
barrier to the adoption.

## Use it for

- **Adoption**, and the only dot point in the Family option where the *Aboriginal Child Placement
  Principle* does real work. That principle exists because of the Stolen Generations; here it was
  argued in a case where the children's connection was discovered after fourteen years of placement.
- **Law reflecting history.** The rule is a legal response to a past wrong, and applying it to these
  children would have separated them from the only family they have known. Neither outcome is
  obviously just, which is what makes it worth writing about.
- **Who decides identity, and on what evidence** — a court, on the balance of probabilities, from
  conflicting family representations.
