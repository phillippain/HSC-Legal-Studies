Released **14 October 2026** and covering 2024–25. Of 4,247 people detained, **30 (0.7%)** were later
released as wrongfully detained — up from **11** the year before. The Ombudsman found **80%** would
have been avoided had existing policies been followed.

## Mx B

Born in Australia in 1989 to New Zealand citizen parents, Mx B became a citizen **by operation of
law** on their tenth birthday in 1999, under the ten-year rule — but departmental records did not
show it. Three separate citizenship assessments were wrong, partly because officers did not consider
the case law on childhood absences. Mx B was detained on release from prison in September 2022 and
held **945 days**. Release came in April 2025 on a bridging visa as an outcome of ***NZYQ***
— **13 days before** a fourth assessment found them a citizen. Mx B was **missing from the
Department's six-monthly reports** because the release was recorded as an NZYQ case rather than a
wrongful detention.

## Findings and recommendations

| Finding | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| 19 cases (63%) from slow communication of ministerial decisions, 18 from a single one-day delay | Formal procedures and same-day communication |
| Mx A held 304 days after an **invalid** visa cancellation | Finish visa checks two months before prison release |
| That pre-release check had been recommended before and never implemented | Recommended again |
| None of the 30 has made a compensation claim | Apology stating they were wrongfully detained, plus a cause summary |

All three were accepted. **Ombudsman recommendations are not binding**; the Office monitors
implementation in later reports.

## Use it for

- **hr.2.4 / dis.3.5 / dis.3.6 (independent statutory authorities)** — the best current illustration of what an
  Ombudsman can and cannot do: it found what the Department's own reporting hid, but it cannot order
  release or compensation. Contrast with a court.
- **Wrongful is not unlawful** — the distinction that makes this evidence sharp. Under ***Ruddock v
  Taylor*** [2005] HCA 48, detention under s 189 of the *Migration Act 1958* on a reasonable
  suspicion is **lawful even if the suspicion is later shown to be mistaken**. So most of the 30 may
  have had no legal remedy at all; only Mx B's detention was conceded unlawful, from about November
  2022 when officers were on notice of the citizenship claim.
- **hr.3.1 / ICCPR art 9** — arbitrary detention. The AHRC has found detention that is not necessary
  or proportionate breaches art 9(1), and the Department rejected its compensation recommendation.
- **inv.1.6, inv.1.7 (procedural fairness, rule of law)** — executive error about the single most basic
  right, and a watchdog whose oversight depends on how the executive classifies its own cases.
- **Law reform that did not happen** — the pre-release check was agreed in principle and never
  implemented. A long-running pattern: the Ombudsman took on 201 such cases in 2005 after the Rau and
  Alvarez scandals, and a citizen was also wrongly detained in 2023–24.

## Caution

A **class-made summary** of a **news report**, not the report. The ABC calls all 30 cases
"unlawfully" detained; the Ombudsman says **"wrongfully"**, and only Mx B's was found unlawful —
using the ABC's word in an answer would overstate it. Ombudsman findings are not court rulings and
the recommendations are not enforceable. The Department's own written response and the figures should
be checked against the full report before quoting.
