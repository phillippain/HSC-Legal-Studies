Australia's **first federal Anti-Slavery Commissioner, Chris Evans** (a former Labor immigration and
workplace minister), said he wanted greater scrutiny of the visas of domestic workers brought in to
work for diplomats, and more accountability through the courts.

## The two cases

**Danaratna v Arunatilaka.** Priyanka Danaratna worked for the Sri Lankan deputy high commissioner in
Canberra: **65 cents an hour**, seven days a week, 6am to 10pm, and restricted from leaving the
premises except for short walks. In **August 2024** Raper J found her entitlements unpaid
(*Danaratna v Arunatilaka* [2024] FCA 918 — **$374,151.90**, against **$11,212.70** actually
received over the whole period), and in **December 2024** imposed a pecuniary penalty of
**$117,028.80** (*Danaratna v Arunatilaka (Penalty)* [2024] FCA 1431). With interest the article puts
the total above **$500,000**. The claim was brought under the **Fair Work Act 2009 (Cth)**, not
modern slavery law. **Sri Lanka refused to pay**, saying it was satisfied the salary was "mutually
agreed".

**Shergill.** Seema Shergill worked for the then Indian high commissioner from 2015 and was found by
the Federal Court in **March 2024** to have been paid about **$9 a day**. She was owed **$189,000**
in wages and interest; the former high commissioner was fined **$97,000**. **India refused to pay.**

The mechanism Evans described: embassies can avoid Australian workplace standards by putting domestic
workers on **diplomatic visas, for which they are not eligible**. The visa actually intended for
domestic workers requires an employment contract meeting Australian workplace law.

## Evans on prosecutions

> "We have very few prosecutions, and much fewer than should be occurring given the size of our
> population if you compare us internationally."

He said he would work with the Australian Government Solicitor and the AFP on why cases are not
reaching trial, and pointed to the **May 2023 review of the Modern Slavery Act 2018**, which
recommended creating his own role, revisiting reporting requirements, and **penalties for businesses
that fail to report** — with the Attorney-General's Department consulting on further legislation.

## Use it for

- **hr.3.1 with hr.2.1 — state sovereignty as an answer, not a word.** This is the best example in
  the library of the limit sovereignty places on enforcement: an Australian court can determine the
  debt, and a foreign state can decline to pay it. The victim wins and gets nothing. Use it whenever
  a question asks how sovereignty affects the enforcement of human rights.
- **Enforceability, with numbers** — $374,151.90 ordered, $11,212.70 received, $117,028.80 penalty,
  and **two governments refusing**. Far stronger than "enforcement is difficult".
- **A gap in the law, precisely located** — the problem is a **visa category**, not the absence of a
  prohibition. Excellent for "what reform is needed?", because the answer is administrative and
  specific: monitor who is granted a diplomatic visa.
- **Where modern slavery meets employment law** — these were run as **Fair Work Act underpayment
  claims**. Useful for showing that a human rights abuse is often litigated through whatever cause of
  action actually works, and for arguing that the modern slavery regime adds reporting, not remedies.
- **hr.2.4 — a statutory authority with a voice but no power to prosecute.** Evans can press, publish
  and advise; he cannot bring the cases. Good on the strengths and limits of independent statutory
  authorities.
- **Law reform in train (hr.t.reform)** — the 2023 statutory review and its recommendations, including
  mandatory action rather than mandatory reporting.

## Caution

Published **3 January 2025**, so check what has happened since: whether either government has paid,
whether further legislation followed the Attorney-General's consultation, and who now holds the
Commonwealth and NSW anti-slavery roles. The article's **$500,000** is wages plus interest; the
precise figures above come from the judgments and should be used in preference. Neither diplomat was
**criminally** convicted of a slavery offence — these were **civil underpayment proceedings** under
the Fair Work Act, so do not describe either as a modern slavery conviction. **Diplomatic immunity**
is not discussed in the penalty judgment and the article does not explain why it did not bar the
claims; do not assert anything about immunity. The Sri Lankan government's position (that the salary
was mutually agreed) is its stated response and should be reported as such.
