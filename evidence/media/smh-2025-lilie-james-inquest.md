**Lilie James**, a 21-year-old sports coach, was murdered at St Andrew's Cathedral School in October
2023 by **Paul Thijssen**, who then took his own life. This article reports the **coronial inquest**,
and it is the inquest — not the crime — that makes it evidence.

## What the inquest named

Thijssen had behaved the same way in an earlier relationship: he asked his then girlfriend to
**share her iPhone location** and would "blow up" if she turned it off; he spent time at her house
when she was not there, sat outside her workplace, and became upset if she did not answer quickly.
When she removed him from her profile picture he flew to Sydney, turned up at her church, and
**punched a tree next to her head**, saying "I can't punch the one thing I want to". He was twice
seen looking into her house; her father told him to stay away.

The article's central observation:

> "It's only now, under the unflinching gaze of a coronial inquest, that his actions were given the
> labels they deserve; **coercive control, technology-facilitated abuse, and stalking**."

## What the experts said

- **Associate Professor Kate Fitz-Gibbon** (Monash), an expert on intimate partner homicide, on
  friends reading the controlling behaviour as care: "It's assuming that [behaviour] was coming from
  a place of kindness, a place of love. It points to the **significant need for educational awareness
  among young people** that this can be … sinister."
- **Anna Butler**, chair of the **Australian Domestic and Family Violence Death Review Network**,
  identified the pattern of **re-exerting control** as James tried to extricate herself, and a "real
  tipping point, a significant loss of control" when she made clear she wanted to end it.
- **Dr Katie Seidler**, clinical and forensic psychologist, on the public performance: she suspected
  he was "papering over a sense of profound inadequacy by projecting an image of perfection… driven by
  the desire to **control the public narrative**".
- On the night James was at a party, a friend recalled Thijssen "stressing and **checking Lilie's
  location on Snapchat**" — the friend's reading was "you like her more than what you say if you are
  worried and checking on her".
- **The missed intervention.** Days before the murder Thijssen **asked a mutual friend whether there
  were any services that gave relationship advice**. Domestic violence experts told the inquest that
  if such a service had existed and been known to his friends, it "may have represented the **one
  opportunity for intervention** that could have stopped his deadly plan".

## Use it for

- **fam.2.4 — why criminalising coercive control is only half a response.** Every element of the
  offence was present in the earlier relationship, and nobody — including the woman's worried parents
  — had the **vocabulary** to call it that at the time. The law existed; the recognition did not.
  Read it directly with `smh-2025-tracking-apps-coercive-control`, which supplies the research behind
  the same misreading, and `bocsar-coercive-control-june-2026` for how rarely the offence is charged.
- **"Given the labels they deserve" is the quote.** Use it for the gap between conduct and legal
  characterisation, and for what an inquest is **for**.
- **Coronial inquests as a legal mechanism (dis.3.6 / fam.2.5)** — a court with no one to convict,
  examining a death to find how it happened and how to prevent the next one. The best example in the
  Family option of a **non-adversarial, preventive** process, and worth comparing with a criminal
  trial, which this could never have been.
- **The Australian Domestic and Family Violence Death Review Network (fam.2.7)** — a standing
  institution whose entire function is to learn from homicides. Name it when a question asks about
  non-legal or institutional responses.
- **The one intervention point** — a perpetrator looking for help and finding nothing. A precise,
  non-obvious argument for **perpetrator intervention services** as a legal-system response, rather
  than only victim support and punishment.
- **Prevention through education (exp.1.2 / exp.3.2)** — Fitz-Gibbon's recommendation is an education
  one. Young people in first relationships, learning to read control as love, is a problem the
  criminal law reaches last.

## Caution

**Handle with real care.** This concerns the **murder of a named 21-year-old woman** and the
perpetrator's **suicide**, both recent, with family and friends still identifiable, and the source
article recounts the **planning of the killing** in detail — including purchases, rehearsals and
surveillance — which this record deliberately does not reproduce. **Do not use that detail in class
or in a resource.** Flag the content before use and pair it with support information
(**1800RESPECT 1800 737 732**, Lifeline **13 11 14**).

On the law: this is **reporting of inquest proceedings**, not findings. The expert opinions quoted —
including the psychological account of Thijssen's motivation — are **evidence given to the coroner**,
and the article predates the coroner's **findings and recommendations**, which should be checked and
cited in preference. Thijssen was **never tried or convicted**; he died, so there is no criminal
finding of guilt, and the facts are as presented to an inquest. The headline framing, "a man who
couldn't cope", is the kind of explanation domestic violence researchers **warn against** because it
locates the cause in the perpetrator's distress rather than in his choice to control; the article
itself contains the better analysis, and a good answer uses that instead.
