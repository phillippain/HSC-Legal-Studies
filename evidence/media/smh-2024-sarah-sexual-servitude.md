"Sarah" left a religious cult at 21, then a controlling relationship, and was isolated and in debt.
A contact at the club where she was working introduced her to a man who said he could help her run
her own business as a sex worker. He became controlling, demanded to know where she was, and
influenced when and how she worked. She worked **interstate and overseas**, was pressured to provide
services she had not consented to, and she and the other women were increasingly isolated and
monitored. She escaped in **2020**.

The moment that makes this record worth having: when she tried to leave, she was told she would have
to pay **$25,000** to cover the cost of her no longer working and to "buy out" control of her
profile, including intimate images. She contacted a lawyer about the debt, and that is when she
**first heard the words "sexual servitude", "debt bondage" and "modern slavery"**. "They seemed like
very extreme words and I thought, 'That couldn't possibly happen to me'."

> "If you can build a prison in somebody's mind and make them think that they can't leave, then you
> don't even have to lock them up."

Finding help was its own problem: "The services I contacted didn't seem to understand my situation …
I didn't fit the picture of what they thought I should." She is now the **lived experience advisor**
to the Office of the Anti-Slavery Commissioner and to the advocacy group Survivor Connections.

## The surrounding evidence in the article

- Walk Free's *Global Slavery Index* estimate of **41,000 people in modern slavery in Australia**.
- The AFP received **340 reports** of modern slavery in **2022–23**, the highest on record, and for
  every recorded victim-survivor there are an estimated **four undetected** ones.
- UN Special Rapporteur **Tomoya Obokata**, concluding an in-country visit, raised **low prosecution
  and conviction rates**, "underreporting by victims" driven by fear of reprisals or of law
  enforcement, and specific concerns about **temporary migrant workers**, people with disability in
  specialised employment workshops, and asylum seekers forced to work illegally.
- NSW Anti-slavery Commissioner **James Cockayne**: "This is a wake-up call to governments at all
  levels in Australia."

## Use it for

- **hr.3.1 — the human case at the centre of the issue.** Every modern slavery answer needs one, and
  this is the strongest in the library because it is **Australian, recent, and consensual-looking on
  the surface**. No chains, no locked door; a debt and a controlled profile.
- **Why the law under-performs (enforceability)** — the "four undetected victims for every recorded
  one" ratio and the low prosecution rate give you a measurable gap between a criminal prohibition
  and its enforcement. The *Criminal Code* offences exist; what fails is detection and reporting.
- **Awareness as a legal problem (hr.1.1)** — a victim who could not name what was happening to her,
  and services that did not recognise her because she "didn't fit the picture". Use it to argue that
  a right is only as real as the ability to identify its breach.
- **Non-legal responses (hr.2.12 / dis.3.3)** — a survivor becoming the **lived experience advisor**
  to a statutory commissioner, and media reporting as the mechanism that made this public. Both are
  responses the law did not provide.
- **Pair with the embassy and school cleaner records** for three completely different faces of the
  same issue: sexual servitude, diplomatic domestic work, and government-contracted cleaning.
- **Debt bondage (crime.1.4)** — the $25,000 "buy-out" is a textbook illustration of a defined form
  of modern slavery, and more memorable than the definition.

## Caution

**"Sarah" is a pseudonym** and the account is **her own, as told to a journalist** — no court has
tested it and nobody has been charged or convicted in connection with it, so write it as her account,
not as findings. The subject matter is **sexual exploitation and coercive control**; flag it before
use. The 41,000 figure is a **Walk Free estimate** with a wide margin of error, not a count; the
"four undetected victims" ratio is also an estimate. The article was published in **November 2024**:
the AFP data is 2022–23, Obokata's visit findings were preliminary at the time, and the Anti-Slavery
Commissioner roles (Commonwealth and NSW) have changed hands — check who holds them before naming
anyone. "Sarah" works in the sex industry; the issue here is **coercion and debt bondage**, not sex
work itself, and the distinction matters.
