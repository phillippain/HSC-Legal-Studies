A child was **born in Brisbane in 2024** after an embryo belonging to one couple was transferred to
another patient. The error surfaced in **February 2025**, only when the birth parents asked to move
their remaining frozen embryos to another provider and **an extra embryo was found in storage**.
Monash IVF called it **human error**, apologised, notified the ART regulators including the
**Reproductive Technology Accreditation Committee**, and commissioned Victorian Senior Counsel
**Fiona McLeod** to investigate. Both families were considering legal action.

It followed a **$56 million settlement** the previous year — the largest class action against an IVF
provider — involving **more than 700 patients** whose healthy embryos may have been destroyed after
inaccurate genetic screening.

## Use it for

- **Birth technologies** (`fam.3.3`) where the question is not "should this be permitted" but **who
  is accountable when it goes wrong**, which is where the law is actually thin.
- **Whose child is it?** Parentage under the *Status of Children Act* and the *Family Law Act*
  presumptions was built for a birth mother and her partner, not for this. The article does not say
  how it was resolved — which is the point worth making: there is no clear statutory answer.
- **Regulation by accreditation rather than statute.** The response was an industry accreditation
  committee and a privately commissioned review, with damages the only route for the families.
  Compare that to how any other medical error of this magnitude would be handled.
- **Law reform trigger** — a visible failure, a national story, and a contemporary issue with no
  settled legal response yet.
