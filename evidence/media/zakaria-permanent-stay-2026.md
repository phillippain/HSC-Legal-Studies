Masood Zakaria, 31, charged over the **20 October 2021** Guildford murders of Salim and Toufik
Hamze, asks the Supreme Court to **permanently stay** his prosecution as an abuse of process, arguing
the AFP's role in his return from Türkiye was unlawful. The hearing opened before **Justice Edward
Muston on 19 October 2026**. **Everything here is an allegation; nothing has been tested at trial,
and the stay has not been decided.**

## Formal extradition versus what is alleged

| | Formal extradition | Zakaria's return (as alleged) |
|---|---|---|
| Legal basis | *Extradition Act 1988* (Cth) and the Australia–Turkey treaty | Turkish **deportation** of a foreigner allegedly on a false passport |
| Safeguards | Double criminality and speciality | Bypassed |
| Who acts | Government to government, AGD the central authority | AFP told Türkiye of the charges and supplied his passport |
| Challenge | The person can contest surrender | Removed **before his Turkish appeal was decided**; he says he asked for Iraq or a third country |
| Custody in transit | Officials of the two states | **Six Hart International contractors**, allegedly **$1.1 million** paid by the AFP to Skytraders and Hart; no Australian official aboard |
| Lawfulness | Lawful if the Act is satisfied | Ierace J (Sept 2025) found **credible prima facie evidence** it breached Turkish law, but was **not satisfied** the AFP knew |

He was detained in Bodrum in January 2023, held in immigration detention about 11 months, and
landed in Darwin on **3 December 2023**; on return he was first charged with 11 other offences
including conspiracy to murder, the murder charges following later.

## Day one of the hearing (19 October 2026)

- **Mark Conroy**, who led a 2021 investigation into the Alameddine group, was shown emails from
  the Attorney-General's office warning that deportation could **jeopardise the prosecution**.
- **Robert Scheeres**, one of the contractors, said Zakaria was never handcuffed and free to go; the
  defence then showed images of him in **flexi cuffs** on the flight.
- The first take-off from Istanbul was aborted after a **bird strike**; the party sat on the tarmac for
  **four days**. The defence calls it a kidnapping and says the AFP kept NSW Police out of the loop.
- The AFP maintains its actions were lawful; its case had not yet been put. The 18 October report
  was that a key witness, an ex-soldier involved in the escort, was uncontactable; the defence
  expects to call up to 35 witnesses, including two AFP Commissioners.

## Week one (to 24 October 2026)

- **Crown concession (22 October)**: the prosecutor accepted that Zakaria was **prohibited** from being
  deported while his Turkish appeal was pending. That concerns **Turkish law** — it does not concede
  what Australian officials **knew**, which is the question *Moti* makes decisive.
- The ABC named the two companies paid the **$1.1 million** as **Skytraders** (charter) and **Hart
  International** (escort); Hart's CEO said it has held AFP contracts for 26 years, including two
  other fugitive transfers (unverified).
- NSW Police said they only ever considered **lawful extradition** and learned of the contractor flight
  when Zakaria landed in Darwin.
- AFP Commissioner Krissy Barrett and former Commissioner Reece Kershaw were expected to give evidence
  the following week. A stay would be the first in NSW for a murder accused over alleged unlawful law
  enforcement conduct, and could affect at least two other prosecutions.

| | *Moti* (2011) | Zakaria (2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Extradition tried? | Two requests refused by Solomon Islands | NSW Police say only lawful extradition was considered |
| Removal lawful locally? | No — before his review window ended | Crown concedes prohibited while his appeal was pending |
| Officials' knowledge | Authorised travel documents knowing it was unlawful | AFP told it was legal; knowledge not proven in 2025 |
| Outcome | Stayed 6–1 (Heydon J dissenting) | Undecided |

## Week two (to 31 October 2026)

- Evidence from **three AFP officers** was partly **suppressed** under the *Court Suppression and Non-publication
  Orders Act 2010* (NSW) s 8 — so reporting of the AFP's side is necessarily incomplete: open justice against
  protecting operational material.
- The defence calls the contractor payments a "sham"; the AFP maintains it acted lawfully. Arrest warrants issued in
  December 2021 preceded the December 2023 deportation.
- In *Moti* the High Court said the focus is on what Australian officials **did or did not do**, not only collusion;
  whether the AFP **knew** the removal was unlawful may be decisive. *Moti* also held that AFP payments to the
  complainant and family were not an abuse.
- Either side may seek appellate review, as in *Moti*, where the Court of Appeal and the High Court reached different
  results.

## Extradition v what happened (3 November summary)

| | Extradition (the lawful route) | Zakaria's return |
|---|---|---|
| Legal basis | *Extradition Act 1988* (Cth) and the Australia–Türkiye treaty; AGD the central authority | Turkish deportation; AFP-funded private escort |
| Safeguards | Double criminality and **speciality** (trial limited to the extradited offences) | Avoided — murder charges were laid after his return and were not on the December 2023 warrant list |
| Right to challenge | Contest in the requested country | Removed while his Turkish appeal was pending; the Crown concedes removal was prohibited |
| Transparency | Formal request on the record | Much AFP evidence suppressed; officers testify with protection against self-incrimination (*Evidence Act* s 128) |

The live ABC headline now reads that AFP officers are "protected from prosecution" as their evidence is suppressed. An
unlawful removal alone does not require a stay: under *Moti*, the focus is on what Australian officials did or knew.

## Week three (to 5 November 2026)

- **AFP Ankara officer (4 November, AAP)** denied using any "leverage" on Turkish authorities, but accepted that AFP
  material may have encouraged Turkish police to remove him, and said Türkiye had threatened to send him to Lebanon.
- **Lead contractor (5 November, ABC)** testified that **no contractor zip-tied** Zakaria on the flight. He said Turkish
  authorities tied him at boarding and the ties were later removed; photographs of him in flexi cuffs on the flight had
  been shown to the court, so the point is **disputed**.
- The 5 November article gives the payment as **$1 million**; earlier ABC reports say **$1.1 million** to two firms.
  An AFP officer said Skytraders invoiced the AFP, which paid, but denied promising Türkiye it would pay.
- Hart's CEO said two AFP contracts moved **two other accused men**, who are subject to non-publication orders.
- Commissioner Barrett and former Commissioner Kershaw were reported as **no longer needing to testify**; no reason has
  been reported. AFP officers' evidence was reported as protected from use to prosecute them under Australian or Turkish
  law; the provision used was not reported.
- On his return he also faced warrants for directing a criminal group, cocaine supply and proceeds of crime offences.
- Two senior NSW Police officers gave similar evidence that they wanted extradition; one learned of the contractor flight
  only on landing in Darwin.

## Rule of law reading (Year 11 summary)

The same hearing works as a **rule of law** example: government bound by law at home and abroad, set against a court's
duty to try serious charges. A stay would follow *Moti* only if Australian officials' **knowledge and conduct** are
established; Ierace J's 2025 finding of prima facie unlawfulness under Turkish law did not find that the AFP knew.

## The test, and why the 2025 ruling matters

A permanent stay rests on the court's **inherent power** to prevent its process being abused: would
continuing bring the administration of justice into disrepute? The applicant carries a **heavy
onus**; stays are granted only in exceptional cases, weighed against the strong public interest in
trying serious charges.

- ***Williams v Spautz*** (1992) — a stay where a prosecution is brought for an improper purpose.
- ***Ex parte Bennett*** (1993, HL) and ***R v Hartley*** (1978, NZCA) — courts may stay where
  extradition procedures were bypassed.
- ***Moti v The Queen*** [2011] HCA 50 — the closest case: the High Court stayed a prosecution
  because Australian officials **knowingly facilitated** a deportation they knew was unlawful.
  **Official knowledge was central** — exactly the finding Ierace J did not make, and exactly what
  the Conroy emails are being used to establish.
- ***Ridgeway v The Queen*** (1995) — the lesser remedy: excluding evidence procured unlawfully.

A stay is **not an acquittal**: the accused keeps the presumption of innocence either way. AAP
reported a stay could affect other accused people deported to Australia.

## Caution

A **class-made summary** of **court reports drawing mainly on the defence case**, with the AFP's
argument still to come and no Crown response. The $1.1 million figure, the emails' content and the
"kidnapping" characterisation are defence allegations; the September 2025 judgment was **not read**,
only reporting of it, and "credible prima facie evidence" is not a final finding. The year he left
Australia (2021 or 2022), his age and the contractors' names are reported inconsistently; the
extradition treaty is confirmed only from law-firm sources. His alleged Alameddine role is unproven.
Payment figures differ between reports ($1m v $1.1m). 'Kidnapping' is the defence's word. Treaty status comes from a law-firm list, not DFAT. Check the eventual Muston J judgment before relying on any finding.
