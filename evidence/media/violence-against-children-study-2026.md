A University of Sydney study published in **JAMA Network Open** on **14 October 2026** compared three
national administrative datasets over **2014–2024**: police records, hospital data and child
protection data.

## What rose, and what did not

| | 2014–2024 |
|---|---|
| Police-recorded sexual assault, boys 10–17 | **+60%** (~4.8% a year; ~15.7% a year from 2021) |
| Police-recorded sexual assault, girls 10–17 | **+51%** (~4.2% a year) |
| Police-recorded family violence assault, boys 0–9 | **+57%** (~4.6% a year) |
| Other groups, family violence assault | Stable early, then ~8–20% a year from about 2020 |
| Assault **hospitalisations** | No significant change |
| **Substantiated** maltreatment | No increase |
| Share of reports investigated | Fell from about **half to about a third** |

## Why the gap matters

The divergence is the point. Hospitalisations flat may mean severe physical harm is not rising;
substantiations flat, against a falling investigation rate, points at **capacity** rather than less
abuse. In NSW, the Ombudsman found about **65%** of screened-in risk-of-significant-harm reports were
closed **without a caseworker allocated** in 2024–25, and that closing reports solely for lack of
capacity is **inconsistent with DCJ's duties** under the *Children and Young Persons (Care and
Protection) Act 1998*. Only about a quarter of children reported at ROSH were seen face to face in
2022–23; of those who were, 48% were found at ROSH.

## Use it for

- **fam.3.4 (care and protection of children)** — the statutory machinery against its own output.
  Anyone may report (s 24), mandatory reporters must (s 27), the Helpline screens against the risk of
  significant harm test (s 23), DCJ investigates "as it considers necessary" (s 30), the Secretary
  must take appropriate action (s 34) and the Children's Court may make care orders (s 71). The
  discretion in ss 30 and 34 is where capacity bites.
- **Effectiveness with a built-in counter-argument** — part of the rise may be greater willingness to
  report, as one expert in the article notes. The 2023 *Australian Child Maltreatment Study* found
  **28.5%** of Australians experienced child sexual abuse and **39.6%** exposure to domestic
  violence, and **younger** adults reported *less* childhood sexual abuse than older cohorts, so
  police data and prevalence can move in opposite directions.
- **hr.3.1 / CROC** — children as rights-holders, and the *National Plan to End Violence against
  Women and Children 2022–32* recognising children as **victim-survivors in their own right**.
- **ls.4.2 (agencies of reform)** — the researchers, the Ombudsman, the 2024 Rapid Review (21
  recommendations, being implemented progressively through National Cabinet), and former National
  Children's Commissioner Anne Hollonds saying it is time for action, not more inquiries. The
  researchers also call for nationally consistent corporal punishment laws, parenting programs,
  respectful relationships education and economic support for families.

## Caution

A **class-made summary** of a **news report of a study**, not the paper. The figures are
**police-recorded victimisations**, so they mix changes in abuse with changes in reporting and
policing — never present them as prevalence. The suggested drivers (inequality, COVID-19, cost of
living, online gender norms) are the researchers' **hypotheses**; the study did not examine
perpetrators. The study is national; the NSW Ombudsman figures are added context and are not from the
study. Check the JAMA Network Open paper before relying on exact rates.
