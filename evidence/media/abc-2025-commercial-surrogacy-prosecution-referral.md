A Brisbane couple who had been trying to have a child for **more than 15 years** paid a company
"purportedly registered under the law of Country E" at least **€84,000 (about A$140,000)** to find a
surrogate, source anonymous donor eggs and arrange an embryo transfer. The surrogate — 37, living in
"country G", **speaking no English** — gave birth in 2024. The overseas birth certificate named her
as the mother and the Australian man as the father. The child came to Australia with the couple in
late 2024.

They then asked the Federal Circuit and Family Court for a **parenting order**, and in a judgment of
**28 January 2025** Carew J:

- **dismissed the application** — "I am concerned that to make the order sought by the applicants
  would act to circumvent the law in Queensland, where commercial surrogacy is a criminal offence",
  and in any case there was no evidence of the child's or the couple's current circumstances, so she
  could not be satisfied the order was in the child's best interests;
- **referred the documents to the Queensland DPP** to consider prosecution — "It is not in contention
  that the applicants have entered into a commercial surrogacy arrangement … and as such are liable
  to prosecution." Maximum penalty **three years' imprisonment**;
- **referred their lawyer to the NSW Legal Services Commissioner**, to consider whether she had
  complied with her obligations "by procuring and filing affidavits … in which they admit to an
  offence under [s] 56 of the Surrogacy Act".

Her Honour called it "curious, to say the least" that the couple had filed an application leaving
themselves open to prosecution. There was **no evidence of what the surrogate was paid**. The matter
was referred on to Queensland Police, who said they were making inquiries.

## The surrounding evidence

Surrogacy lawyer **Stephen Page**: more than **3,300 Australian children** have been born to
surrogates abroad, with hundreds of uncertain parentage; Australia has **eight systems of surrogacy
regulation**, one per state and territory; "There should be a uniform law … as long as it's one
version not eight versions"; and state bans on overseas commercial surrogacy fail "because they
cannot be enforced and therefore should be repealed". He knew of **three other** referrals to
prosecutors and, to his knowledge, **no one had ever been prosecuted**. Some parents are "too scared
to go to the courts". Surrogacy Australia president **Sarah Bevan**, whose practice acted for the
surrogate: "Many children born through surrogacy internationally do not have their reality recognised
in a legal sense."

## Use it for

- **fam.3.3 — the sharpest available illustration of a law that punishes the people it governs and
  protects nobody.** The couple broke a Queensland law; the surrogate's payment is unknown; and the
  child is the one left without recognised parents. Use the three outcomes together.
- **Enforceability, demonstrated twice over** — an extraterritorial criminal prohibition that
  produces **referrals and no prosecutions**, and that only comes to light **when parents ask a court
  to help their child**. A law enforced only against those who volunteer is the best enforceability
  example in the Family option.
- **The chilling effect (inv.1.4 / accessibility)** — parents "too scared to go to the courts", so
  children stay unrecognised. A concrete access-to-justice consequence of a criminal prohibition.
- **fam.1.2 — state and federal law pulling against each other.** A **Commonwealth** court asked for
  a parenting order declined to make one because of a **state** offence, while **Commonwealth**
  citizenship and passport law had already let the child in. Outstanding for jurisdictional conflict.
- **Law reform with a live process (chg.1.3 / fam.t.reform)** — eight regimes, a decade of calls for
  uniformity, and the **ALRC Review of Surrogacy Laws** running now. See the Caution for its timing.
- **The best interests of the child as a limit, not a trump card** — the order was refused partly for
  **want of evidence** about the child. A good corrective to the assumption that "best interests"
  always produces the outcome the adults want.
- **Professional obligations (dis.2.5 / inv.1.6)** — a solicitor referred to the regulator for filing
  affidavits in which her clients admitted an offence. A rare, examinable example of a lawyer's duty
  to the court cutting against the client's instructions.

## Caution

The parties, the company's country and the surrogate's country are **anonymised** in the judgment;
Pt XIVB applies, so do not speculate about identities or countries. This is **news reporting of a
first-instance judgment** — read the judgment before quoting anything beyond the passages the ABC
quotes directly. A **referral is not a charge**: as at the article's date nobody had been prosecuted,
and the outcome of the DPP, police and Legal Services Commissioner referrals is **unknown** — do not
say the couple or the lawyer were penalised. The law firm's statement that it has handled "countless
similar" cases "without any issues" is its own account. The offence is a **Queensland** one (s 56,
*Surrogacy Act 2010* (Qld)); the extraterritorial bans apply in **NSW, Qld and the ACT** only, and
NSW's equivalent is s 11 of the *Surrogacy Act 2010* (NSW) — check the provision for the state you
are writing about. The **ALRC final report** went to the Attorney-General by **11 September 2026**
(extended from 29 July) and must be tabled within 15 sitting days, so it was **not yet public** in
October 2026; do not attribute recommendations to it until it is released.
