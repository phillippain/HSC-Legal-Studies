Marie Segrave, Professor of Criminology at the University of Melbourne, on the migration changes
Immigration Minister **Tony Burke** announced at the National Press Club on **17 September 2026** —
among them a crackdown on visa overstayers backed by the threat of detention, and a backpacker
ballot. Her argument is not about the policy's migration merits but about who else it catches.

## The mechanism

- Migration law already offers a safety net in one place only: the **family violence provisions**,
  which give a woman on a **partner visa** a pathway to permanency if the relationship ends because
  of violence.
- Many women experiencing violence are not on partner visas — student visa holders who form a
  relationship here, asylum seekers who do not report violence because the family breadwinner's
  protection visa is at stake, and women promised a partner visa who arrive on a visitor visa, the
  promise itself used as control.
- Perpetrators lie about visa status, so women believe they hold a partner visa when they do not,
  and withhold Home Affairs correspondence so the perpetrator holds all the information.
- The result: women overstay **without knowing they have**, and a policy that threatens overstayers
  with detention becomes one more thing an abuser can threaten them with — "dobbing them in" to have
  them deported, which Segrave says already happens at high rates.

## The figures

| Finding | Source as cited |
|---|---|
| One in three migrant and refugee women had experienced domestic and family violence | the largest national study of migrant and refugee women |
| Temporary migrants were **more likely** to experience violence, including violence tied to visa status | same study |
| Only **22%** were confident they knew what visa they held and the rights attached to it | survey of temporary visa holders reporting migration-related controlling behaviour |

## Use it for

- **fam.2.4 (domestic violence)** — the sharpest available example that a legal response can be
  undone by a law made elsewhere for another purpose. Coercive control provisions criminalise
  threats to a partner's status; migration settings make that threat credible.
- **hr.3.1** — a contemporary issue where the rights at stake (security of the person, access to
  protection) belong to non-citizens, whose enforcement depends on the very status being used
  against them.
- **fam.2.6 / hr.2.5** — non-government advocacy as a reform driver: the national advocacy group for
  women on temporary visas experiencing DFV was about to launch its **third Blueprint for Reform**,
  a measure of how long the same recommendations have gone unanswered.
- **Evaluation** — good evidence that "responsiveness" is not just speed. The changes were announced
  after a six-week delay and still, Segrave writes, showed no sign that victim-survivors had been
  considered.

## Caution

This is **advocacy scholarship in a media outlet**, not a court, a statute or a dataset: Segrave
writes as a researcher arguing for a visa solution, and the piece predicts harm rather than
measuring it. Use it for the mechanism and the figures, attribute the prediction to her, and pair it
with the legal source if the answer needs one — the family violence provisions themselves, or the
*National Plan to End Violence against Women and Children*, which she says commits little on this
point. The studies are named in the article by link rather than by title, so cite them as "the
national study of migrant and refugee women reported in *The Conversation*" unless you can check the
original.
