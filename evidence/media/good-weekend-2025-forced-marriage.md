The AFP dealt with **91 forced-marriage cases** in a year, thought to be a small fraction of the real
number. Forced marriage has been an offence under **Division 270 of the *Criminal Code Act 1995*
(Cth)** since 2013.

## What happened

**Ruqia Haidari** was pressured by her mother, **Sakina Muhammad Jan**, into marrying Mohammad Ali
Halimi. The AFP had met Haidari in Shepparton in August 2019 after her school reported she did not
want to marry; she declined their offer to speak to her mother. The couple moved to Perth in November
2019. The next day, during a fight, Halimi cut her throat and killed her. He pleaded guilty to murder
and was sentenced in 2021 to **life with a 19-year minimum**.

The AFP — which **had never secured a forced-marriage conviction** — then charged the mother. In the
**Victorian County Court**, Muhammad Jan was found guilty of **causing a person to enter a forced
marriage** and sentenced to **three years with a one-year minimum**. She had herself been married at
**12 or 13**, widowed by the Taliban, and came to Australia as a refugee in 2013, uneducated and
without English. Investigators had hoped a conviction would set a precedent and deter others.

## The argument about whether it worked

- Criminologist **Laura Vidal**: the system "scapegoated one person within a complex dynamic" —
  "nothing happened to the matchmaker, or the brothers, or the community or the 500 people who
  attended the wedding". **"We've got one prosecution in 12 years that was only possible because the
  victim died. Are we really happy with that?"**
- **Nesreen Bottriell**, Australian Muslim Women's Centre for Human Rights: the outcome has damaged
  trust and "is going to deter victim-survivors from seeking support for fear of their mother or
  family members being prosecuted"; forced marriage is about "power and control over women's
  decision-making", not religion.
- A Danish model — working with families to shift behaviour rather than the **victim–perpetrator
  binary** — is being trialled at Life Without Barriers.

## Use it for

- **Enforceability against reporting**: an offence that produced one conviction in twelve years, and
  that conviction discourages the very people it protects from coming forward. This is the best
  available example in the course of a criminal law response that may be **counter-productive**.
- **Consent and the legal requirements of marriage** (`fam.1.3`) — real consent, not formal consent.
- **Non-legal responses** — the alternative reporting pathways that now exist precisely because
  reporting to police carries this cost.
