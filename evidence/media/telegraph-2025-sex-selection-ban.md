Selecting a baby's sex for non-medical reasons is **banned in Australia on ethical grounds**. The ban
is not in an Act: it sits in the **National Health and Medical Research Council's ethical guidelines
on assisted reproductive technology**, which clinics must follow to stay accredited.

**Caitlin Bailey**, 31, a Melbourne content creator and single mother of two boys and a girl, used a
**California doctor, Daniel Potter**, to select the sex of her fourth child, and spent about
**$45,000** including travel to conceive a girl. She had testing in Melbourne, chose a US sperm donor
in a process she described as like "**online shopping**", and made two trips to the US — one to have
eggs retrieved and one for the embryo transfer. "Everybody gets a brother, everybody gets a sister."

Dr Potter — who estimates his sex selection services have produced the birth of **thousands of
Australian babies over more than 20 years**, and who was due to run seminars in Brisbane, Sydney and
Melbourne — called it "**absolutely ridiculous**" that Australian parents cannot do this at home,
comparing it with IVF, which was "initially controversial but now widely accepted". Sex selection is
done by **biopsying embryos**, which also screens for chromosomal abnormalities.

## Where the law stands, and the argument for moving it

A **Fertility Society of Australia and New Zealand** spokeswoman said national consultation on
**non-medical sex selection** formed part of an extensive review of the NHMRC guidelines about **ten
years ago**, that "**a majority of submissions supported the option**" of it, and that in **2017, at
the conclusion of that review, the NHMRC upheld its moratorium**. Given that the consultation is now
a decade old, "amid a falling Australian birthrate and an evolving ethical landscape, it may be time
to revisit the discussion".

**Dr Raelia Lew**, Melbourne IVF medical director, made the hardest argument in the piece: she has
had patients who **conceived naturally and terminated a pregnancy based on the sex** of the baby
because they wanted a "balanced" family. "It's a very upsetting scenario… I personally feel that if a
family felt so strongly about sex selection that they were willing to terminate a pregnancy in the
first trimester based on that, it would actually be **less harmful to that family to allow them to do
an IVF cycle with sex selection**."

## Use it for

- **fam.3.3 — regulation without legislation.** The most interesting feature of this record is
  **where the rule lives**: NHMRC ethical guidelines enforced through clinic accreditation, not a
  statute passed by a parliament. Excellent for classifying law (inv.1.3), for delegated and
  non-legislative regulation, and for asking who should decide a question like this.
- **Enforceability of a domestic ban in a global market** — "thousands of Australian babies over more
  than 20 years" through one US clinic, and a doctor running seminars in three Australian cities.
  Pair it with the Colombian surrogacy record: the same pattern of a prohibition that redirects
  conduct offshore rather than preventing it.
- **Responsiveness, with a date attached (chg.1.2)** — the consultation was about **ten years ago**,
  a **majority of submissions supported** allowing it, and the moratorium was **upheld in 2017** and
  has not been revisited. A measurable lag between public submissions and the rule.
- **The strongest argument you would not have thought of (fam.t.values / inv.1.5)** — Dr Lew's point
  that the alternative to regulated sex selection is sometimes **termination**. Use it to show that a
  prohibition has consequences, and that "ethical grounds" cuts both ways.
- **Access and money (inv.1.4)** — $45,000 and two trips to California. The ban does not stop sex
  selection; it limits it to people who can afford to leave.
- **Both sides of the ethical case** — against: sex ratio distortion, treating children as designed
  products, the slide toward other traits, and the "online shopping" language in the article itself.
  For: autonomy, family balancing, the termination argument, and the embryo biopsy also screening for
  chromosomal abnormalities.

## Caution

A **tabloid exclusive** that is largely **advocacy for liberalising the ban**: the two medical voices
quoted both favour revisiting it, the overseas provider is given an unchallenged platform for his
own commercial seminars, and **no ethicist, no disability advocate and no opponent is quoted** —
students must supply the other side themselves. Dr Potter's "thousands of Australian babies" is his
own **estimate**, not verified data. The NHMRC guidelines are the authority here: **check the current
edition** and the exact terms of the moratorium rather than relying on this summary, and note the
guidelines also permit sex selection to avoid a **serious sex-linked genetic condition**, which the
article does not explain. "Banned in Australia" is a simplification — the mechanism is clinical
accreditation and the ART legislation that incorporates the guidelines, and it varies between states.
Caitlin Bailey consented to be named and photographed; the discussion in class should stay on the
legal and ethical question, not on her choices. Published **May 2025**, so the NHMRC's position may
have moved.
