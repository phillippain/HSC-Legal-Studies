Written after Treasurer **Jim Chalmers'** economic roundtable declared a consensus on
**intergenerational fairness**. Superannuation, capital gains and trusts had all been named as
concessions favouring older, higher-income people. This article is about the one nobody names.

## The concession

The **tax-free principal place of residence** generates **as much as $50 billion a year** in tax
breaks on realised capital gains. It is exempt from **capital gains tax** and excluded from the
**age pension asset test** — which, as the article notes, amplifies the incentive for retirees to
funnel money into the home in order to qualify for a payment.

- **Peter Siminski** (UTS): "If you're interested in tackling intergenerational equity, housing
  affordability and productivity, the **elephant in the room** from the economic roundtable is the
  favourable tax treatment of owner-occupied housing."
- **Ben Phillips** (ANU): "It's one way of making a fair bit of wealth **without paying a cent of
  tax**."
- **Tim Helm** (Prosper Australia): the exemption skews investment into housing "instead of putting
  it into other more productive investments like investing in companies."

Buyers bid prices up knowing future gains will be **entirely untaxed**. And it is "considered
untouchable – arguably a bigger sacred cow than taxing superannuation".

## What affordability now looks like

From the **PropTrack–CommBank First Home Buyer Report** released that week: affordability is at its
**lowest level since records began in 1995**. A first home buyer household aged **25–39** renting on
a typical income of **$129,000** could afford just **17% of homes** sold in the past year. Saving a
**20% deposit** on a median-priced home takes the equivalent of **5.9 years** — against **3 years in
1995** and **4.7 in 2005**.

## Stamp duty versus land tax

- The **ACT** is the only jurisdiction applying a material **annual tax on unimproved land value**
  to the principal residence, through its rates system; owners typically pay about **$2,000 to
  $6,000** a year, multiples of ordinary council rates elsewhere. Stamp duty has been wound back in
  favour of land rates since **2012**, though the planned **20-year transition** has been delayed and
  criticised for **double taxation**.
- **Victoria University Centre of Policy Studies** research: in the ACT, a **10% reduction in stamp
  duty** was associated with a **6% rise in property transactions**.
- **Nassios and Giesecke** (Victoria University CoPS) model swapping stamp duty for land tax: market
  prices of low-density houses **4.7% lower**, and the **total price paid by a purchaser 9.4% less**
  once the upfront duty is gone; apartment prices rise slightly relative to houses because holding
  periods are shorter.
- **NSW** under **Perrottet** let first buyers choose between upfront stamp duty and an annual levy.
  The **Minns government axed it**.
- Modelling for the **Productivity Commission** goes further: abolish **$38 billion** of state
  conveyancing duty and **$18 billion** of land tax on investment properties, and replace them with a
  **broad $47 billion annual land tax on all properties, including the principal residence**. Rents
  would fall because the bias against investment properties disappears, and "given that the average
  renter has a lower income than the average owner-occupier, it seems likely that the property tax
  reform package **reduces inequality**" — while cutting the main property taxes **from three to
  one**.
- The article also carries a ranking of the **economic cost of different taxes**, with **residential
  stamp duty near the very top (74)** and **council land rates negative (−4)** — i.e. stamp duty is
  among the most economically damaging taxes and land rates among the least.

## Use it for

- **shel.3.1 / shel.2.1 — the tax law that sets the price.** Most answers treat affordability as a
  supply problem. This is the demand side written into the *Income Tax Assessment Act*, and the
  figures let you argue it rather than assert it.
- **"The elephant in the room"** and **"a fair bit of wealth without paying a cent of tax"** are the
  quotes, and they come from named academics rather than advocates.
- **Affordability with a benchmark (inv.1.4)** — **17% of homes** affordable, **5.9 years** for a
  deposit against **3 years in 1995**. A thirty-year trend line in two numbers.
- **Stamp duty as the law students never question** — it is the tax they will actually pay, it
  discourages moving, and the ACT evidence (**10% cut → 6% more transactions**) shows the effect.
  Perfect for resource efficiency and for whether a law meets society's needs.
- **A reform that failed, with names and dates (chg.1.3)** — **Perrottet proposed it, Minns axed
  it**, while the ACT has been transitioning since 2012 and is still behind schedule. Use it for the
  conditions that give rise to law reform and, more usefully, for why **good policy does not become
  law**: political cost, transitional unfairness and double taxation.
- **Intergenerational conflict made concrete (shel.t.values)** — a concession that benefits the
  asset-rich and prices out the young, defended because the family home is "sacrosanct". Then the
  honest counter: for many households the home **is** their entire retirement saving, and taxing it
  would hit people who are not wealthy in income terms.
- **Australia versus New Zealand** — NZ's housing minister **Chris Bishop** said prices "are too
  expensive" and needed to fall, and NZ prices did fall **13%** from their 2021 peak. Australia's
  **Clare O'Neil** targets "sustainable house price growth, not price falls". A sharp comparison on
  whether a government can even say what the goal is.

## Caution

The **AFR's economics editor** writing **analysis**, not reporting — it argues a case, and almost
every voice quoted (Siminski, Phillips, Prosper Australia, the CoPS modellers) supports taxing land
more and transactions less. **No defender of the exemption is quoted**, so a balanced answer must
supply one: the family home is the main asset of many low-income retirees, a land tax is payable
whether or not you have income, and the exemption keeps the tax system out of the family home. The
**$50 billion** is an estimate of forgone revenue on **realised** gains and depends heavily on
assumptions; the **$38bn / $18bn / $47bn** figures are **modelling for the Productivity Commission**,
not government policy, and nothing in this article has been legislated. The tax-cost ranking
(stamp duty 74, land rates −4) is reproduced from a chart whose **units and source are not legible in
the library copy** — describe it as a ranking, do not quote the numbers as a measure. Published
**5 September 2025**, before the 2026 Budget changes to negative gearing and the CGT discount
recorded at `federal-budget-2026-housing-tax-changes`, so read the two together and do not present
this as the current tax position.
