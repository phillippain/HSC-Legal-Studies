The record that explains why a criminal offence is not enough: the behaviour the law now calls
coercive control is, among young people, **read as affection**.

## The eSafety research

A survey of **2,000 adults** by the **eSafety Commission**:

- **Men (one in five)** were more likely than **women (one in ten)** to agree that **constantly
  texting** to ask who a partner was with or what they were doing is usually a **sign of care**.
- **26.3% of men** against **11.8% of women** thought wanting a partner to be **constantly available**
  to respond to texts, calls or video chat was a sign of care.
- Almost **14%** of all participants said using a **location-sharing app to track an intimate partner
  whenever they wanted** would be reasonable — rising to almost **19% of 18-to-24-year-olds**.

**eSafety Commissioner Julie Inman Grant** traces it to parents: around COVID the commission noticed
tracking apps being used by parents as a "well-intentioned safety measure", and "we think, over time,
that has **anaesthetised young people** to this whole idea of being monitored or surveilled". Her
conclusion: "When this is happening to young people in, say, their first relationships, I don't think
they would **self-identify** as being a victim survivor of coercive control or domestic violence."

**Life360** monthly active users in Australia grew from **1.9 million** in the June quarter of 2023
to **2.7 million** a year later.

**Maria Atienzar-Prieto** (Griffith University), whose PhD research ran focus groups with young people
who had used location-sharing apps in relationships, found the same misreading — and the mechanism:
"One of the findings that really highlighted how this behaviour was normalised, was that the behaviour
**starts in the family home**… 'I felt very comfortable using this app with my friends and partners
because my parents tracked me while I was growing up.'" Location sharing is treated as a
**demonstration of commitment**, so stopping it reads as distrust or a breach of dating etiquette: "A
lot willingly opt in because everyone around them does it… **But when a young person doesn't want to
share their location, opting out is very difficult.**"

The article also notes that tracking offers a **false sense of safety** — Audrey Griffin's friends
were following her walk home on the night she was killed by a stranger, and lost track of her.

## Use it for

- **fam.2.4 — the gap between the law and the culture.** NSW has criminalised coercive control
  (s 54D), and the conduct the offence targets is being **consented to and welcomed** by the people it
  protects. That is the most interesting enforceability problem in the topic: an offence cannot be
  reported by someone who does not think anything is wrong.
- **"Surveillance is love" is the thesis**, and the research gives it a cause: a generation tracked by
  their parents. Pair Inman Grant's "anaesthetised" with Atienzar-Prieto's "it starts in the family
  home".
- **The numbers are the evidence (fam.t.values)** — **19% of 18–24s** think tracking a partner at will
  is reasonable, against **14%** overall. A **younger** cohort holding **more** permissive views is the
  opposite of the usual assumption about changing values, and it predicts the problem getting worse.
- **The gender split** — 26.3% of men against 11.8% of women on constant availability. Useful, careful
  evidence that the behaviour and its interpretation are gendered.
- **Non-legal responses are the only ones that reach this (fam.2.6 / fam.2.7)** — education, parental
  conversations about boundaries, and the eSafety Commissioner's own public advocacy. Inman Grant's
  advice is to **never track a child without their knowledge** and to say why you are doing it. A
  statutory officer whose main tool is persuasion.
- **Consent that is not really a choice (inv.1.5)** — "opting in is easy, opting out is very
  difficult". Links to the broader legal problem of consent to surveillance, and to the privacy and
  online safety records.
- **Technology-facilitated abuse (crime.1.8 / chg.6.2)** — ordinary consumer apps, designed for
  safety, used for control. The clearest example of law reform struggling to keep pace with
  technology, and it needs no new statute to explain: Snap Map, Find My Friends and Life360 are all
  lawful.

## Caution

Journalism reporting **unpublished-in-detail research**: the survey of **2,000 adults** is summarised
by the paper, so go to the **eSafety Commission's** own publication for the methodology, question
wording and margins before quoting a percentage. Atienzar-Prieto's work was **PhD research using
focus groups** — qualitative, small, and not generalisable to all young people. The **Life360 growth
figures** are commercial usage data and say nothing about misuse. Most importantly, the article's
framing links tracking apps to the murders of **Lilie James** and **Audrey Griffin**: the apps did not
cause either death, and in Griffin's case the killer was a **stranger** and the tracking was a safety
measure that failed. Do not write that tracking apps cause homicide; the evidence is that **location
monitoring is a documented feature of coercive control** and that young people misread it. Both cases
involve named, recent victims — handle them with care, and note the article carries
**1800RESPECT (1800 737 732)**, Lifeline and Beyond Blue details for a reason. Published **May 2025**;
app usage, eSafety's position and the law have all moved since.
