On **5 November 2026** the Foreign Minister, Senator Penny Wong, announced sanctions on **four individuals and one entity** over alleged serious human rights violations in Sudan's war, which began on **15 April 2023** between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF). The individuals named are **Burhan, Dagalo, Al-Tijani Al-Tahir Karshoum and Hussein Barsham**; the entity is the **Al-Baraa Bin Malik Brigade**, an SAF-aligned Islamist militia. The individuals face financial sanctions and travel bans; the entity faces financial sanctions only. **These are executive listings, not findings of guilt.**

## International responses v Australia's autonomous sanctions

| | UN / ICC | Australian autonomous sanctions |
|---|---|---|
| Legal basis | UNSC Res 1556 (2004) and 1591 (2005), binding on UN members | Autonomous Sanctions Regulations 2011 reg 6A(4); Australian law only |
| Who decides | Security Council committee, advised by an expert panel | Foreign Minister, with the Attorney-General's written agreement |
| Targets | Two UN listings since the 2023 war began | Both top commanders plus three others |
| Reach | Arms embargo, asset freezes, travel bans | Assets and entry connected with Australia only |
| Accountability | ICC convicted Ali Kushayb (Oct 2025) for 2003–04 Darfur crimes; 20 years (Dec 2025) | No trial; a listing can be revoked on written application |
| Measured effect | Embargo poorly enforced | An October 2026 assessment found sanctions disrupted some finance but did not reduce violence |

## How a thematic human rights sanction works

1. The Minister must be satisfied the target was responsible for or complicit in serious violations of the right to life, freedom from torture or freedom from slavery.
2. Since the 2021 amending Act the Minister must obtain the **Attorney-General's written agreement** (reg 6A).
3. The Minister designates for financial sanctions and declares individuals for travel bans; names go on DFAT's Consolidated List.
4. Dealing with a designated person's assets without authorisation is an offence; declared persons cannot enter or remain in Australia.
5. Listed persons may apply in writing for revocation; a **class-based humanitarian permit** lets aid work continue.

The listing instrument itself was not retrieved; the steps follow the regulation and the release.

## Key facts and what the release leaves out

- The US had already sanctioned both Dagalo and Burhan in January 2025, so Australia **follows rather than leads**; the UK sanctioned four RSF commanders in December 2025.
- Government figures: 14 million+ displaced, about 34 million (over 70%) needing aid, $67 million+ in Australian humanitarian assistance. An October 2026 assessment cites OCHA's 13.4 million displaced; the death toll is not independently verified.
- The release does not say whether any listed person holds assets in or travels to Australia, so the practical bite may be **mainly symbolic**. Sudanese-Australian advocates have asked mainly for more aid and visa support, not sanctions.
- Australia says it had already sanctioned 13 individuals under the UN framework; an undated DFAT snapshot lists six UN designations (unverified).

## Competing perspectives

- **Supporters**: accountability signal; the UN Fact-Finding Mission recommended targeted sanctions with ICC prosecution; listing both sides signals even-handedness; the humanitarian permit protects aid.
- **Critics**: targets move business to Egypt and the Gulf; sanctions need a wider political strategy; unilateral measures cannot replace trials (ICC justice for Darfur took two decades).
