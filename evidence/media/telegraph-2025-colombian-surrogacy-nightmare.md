Charles and Emmanuel, a Melbourne couple in their thirties, signed with **Tammuz Family**, an
Israel-headquartered surrogacy agency, in **May 2022**. Commercial surrogacy is illegal in Australia
and their search for an **altruistic** surrogate "never happened", so they chose **Colombia** — on
cost, and because it allows same-sex couples to access commercial surrogacy.

They paid **more than A$64,900**, with a further **$45,000+** payable during a pregnancy. They flew
to Bogotá in January 2023 to donate sperm at the Celagem clinic. They ended up with **five viable
embryos**; none produced a birth. "Given the amount of embryos that we had, the likelihood of every
single one of them failing was very, very low, and yet, here we are." After running out of embryos
they had to start again with a new egg donor, and by then had "lost all confidence". Almost three
years on they remain childless.

The contract says the fees are **non-refundable**, but they bought a "**guarantee**" plan — "as many
donor cycles and subsequent embryo transfers as needed until a live birth". The agency's Australian
head of operations said embryo transfer failure "is a recognised part of the IVF process" and that
the guarantee exists "to offer certainty in an uncertain process".

> "If you make noise and complain, no baby and no refund — that's the really hard part."

## The expert framing

- **Sarah Jefford** (surrogacy lawyer): "guarantee" plans are not unusual, and "there aren't really
  any 'guarantees' when it comes to reproduction and fertility". She knew of other intended parents
  who paid **$80,000** to a different provider and three years later had nothing, with their
  **embryos stuck in another country**. "We need to make surrogacy more accessible in Australia, so
  that intended parents do not feel compelled to go overseas."
- **Professor Paula Gerber** (Monash): "It is shameful that if people need surrogacy to start their
  family, we force them to travel to poorer countries with inferior health care services and
  regulation." Australia's prohibition on compensated surrogacy is "outdated and in urgent need of
  reform".
- **Dr Ju Attawet** (Swinburne): "once money is transferred offshore … there is little to no legal
  protection available"; there is **no international legal framework** regulating cross-border
  surrogacy and global reform is unlikely soon, so "meaningful regulation must come from destination
  countries themselves … Australia cannot legislate internationally, [but] it can strengthen
  protections for its citizens."

## Use it for

- **fam.3.3 — the consequence of a prohibition, stated as a causal chain.** Commercial surrogacy is
  banned here → no altruistic surrogate available → an overseas agency → $65,000 gone, no child, no
  remedy. One paragraph, four links, all sourced. This is the strongest "the law pushed them
  offshore" evidence in the library.
- **Enforceability across borders** — "once money is transferred offshore … little to no legal
  protection". Australian consumer and contract law reaches an Israel-headquartered agency operating
  in Colombia barely at all. Pair it with the Caution's point about **eight domestic regimes and no
  international framework**.
- **A contract that cannot be enforced (dis.2.3 / dis.2.7)** — non-refundable fees plus an open-ended
  "guarantee". Ask what remedy a court could realistically give, and the answer is the point.
- **Same-sex couples and access (fam.1.4)** — they chose Colombia partly because it permits same-sex
  commercial surrogacy. Useful for how the law's treatment of family forms shapes real choices.
- **Two sides of the reform argument (fam.t.reform)** — Gerber and Jefford both argue for
  **compensated or more accessible domestic surrogacy**, and the exploitation concern that justifies
  the ban is the counter-argument. Set them against each other rather than asserting one.
- **The exploitation question turned around** — the usual concern is harm to surrogates in poorer
  countries. Here the people without protection are the **intended parents**. A genuinely useful
  complication for an evaluation.

## Caution

A **tabloid exclusive** built on the couple's own account, with the agency given a right of reply
that it used: Tammuz says timelines run from receipt of sperm, that biological failure is expected,
and that it "remain[s] fully committed" to them. **No court has found anything** — there is no
judgment, no regulatory finding and no allegation proved, so write it as a dispute, not as
wrongdoing, and do not say the agency defrauded them. The couple's **surnames are withheld** at their
request. Published **8 April 2025**: their situation may have resolved either way. The $80,000 example
Jefford gives is a **different provider**. The experts quoted are **advocates for reform** — Gerber
and Jefford both argue for compensated surrogacy in Australia — so their quotes are positions, not
neutral findings. The **ALRC Review of Surrogacy Laws** final report went to the Attorney-General by
**11 September 2026** and must be tabled within 15 sitting days; it was **not public** in October
2026, so do not state its recommendations.
