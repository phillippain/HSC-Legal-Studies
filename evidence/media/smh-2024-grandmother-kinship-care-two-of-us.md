Statistics on kinship care tell you how many children are in it. This piece tells you what the legal process felt like from inside one of those arrangements, in the two voices that lived it.

## What happened

In 1995 Marita, then 50, got a phone call: her granddaughter **Ashlee, aged three**, had been found in the dark outside a house in Robe, South Australia. Marita's daughter Kim — a heroin user since her teens — was not home. Marita's husband John took a week off work, drove over and brought the child back to Melbourne.

Then came the law:

> "It was a rigmarole getting custody; it took us until Ashlee was 13."

**Ten years.** Through all of it, Ashlee "never changed her story: she was too scared to go back to her mum's."

Before the orders were made, the arrangement had no legal standing at all. Marita describes enrolling Ashlee in kindergarten and "explaining that we weren't her legal guardians at that point, but we knew her mother wasn't going to come and pick her up." The care was real; the authority was not.

Ashlee's account of the proceedings themselves is the detail worth keeping:

> "In custody disputes, the courts get you to match a fictional character to a family member. Mum would ask me to pick Cruella de Vil for Grandma."

A child, in a court-ordered assessment exercise, being coached by one party against the other. Kim died of an overdose in 2017.

Non-legal support does the other half of the work in this story. The **Mirabel Foundation**, which supports children of addicts, is credited by both of them — "Grandma was able to talk and heal with other carers. It changed both our lives." Ashlee, now 32 and a technology consultant with three scholarships behind her, speaks publicly for Mirabel.

## Use it for

- **fam.1.4 — alternative family relationships.** Grandparent-headed kinship care, with the legal gap named precisely: a decade of de facto care without guardianship.
- **fam.3.4 — care and protection of children.** The arrangement worked and the child was safe long before any order existed. Use it to argue that the law's **responsiveness** is the weak point, not the family's willingness.
- **A delay argument with a number attached.** Ten years from the removal to the final orders, in a case where the child's evidence never wavered. Far more persuasive in an essay than "court processes can be slow".
- **fam.2.6 — the role of non-government organisations.** Mirabel supported the *carer*, not only the child — the gap in statutory support that NGOs routinely fill.
- **The child's voice.** Pair it with s 60CC(3) considerations and with research on children's participation in family law proceedings: here the "fictional character" exercise became another front in the dispute.

## Caution

This is a **first-person interview column** (Good Weekend's "Two of Us"), not a case report. There is no citation, no judgment, no court named and no legal reasoning — the events run from 1995 to 2024 across **South Australia and Victoria**, and we are told nothing about which jurisdiction or which Act produced the orders. Treat "custody" as the family's word, not a current legal term: parenting orders replaced custody and access language in 1996, part-way through this very story.

The ten-year figure is Marita's recollection and is not verifiable from the article. Quote it as her account, which is how it is offered.

Written carefully, that limitation is itself the point: the article is evidence of lived experience and of the gap between care and legal recognition, and should be used alongside statutory and statistical sources rather than in place of them.
