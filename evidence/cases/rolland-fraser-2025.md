Both parents enrolled a child at a private school and signed the enrolment forms; the father said he
would pay the full fees to Year 12. He did, until his employment was **terminated for reasons beyond
his control**; he took a lower-paid role in the same industry and stopped paying the fees. The
mother applied to depart from the administrative assessment.

## How child support actually works

Child support is normally set by a **formula** — each parent's adjusted taxable income and
percentage of care. The formula is fast and cheap and, as the Family Court said in *Gyselman*, "will
also produce cases of unfairness and inequity". So the *Child Support (Assessment) Act 1989* (Cth)
allows a **departure** on eleven statutory grounds, each prefaced by **"special circumstances"** —
facts "peculiar to the particular case which set it apart from other cases" (*Philippe*;
*Gyselman*). A departure also has to be **just and equitable** and **otherwise proper**: s 98C(1)(b).

The mother relied on two grounds: the children were being educated in the way both parents expected,
and the assessment did not reflect the father's **earning capacity** (s 117(7)).

## Held — decision set aside and substituted

Increases of **+$8,363, +$8,898 and +$8,738** across the relevant years.

## Use it for

- **Rights and obligations of parents** with the machinery visible: a formula for speed, a departure
  process for fairness, and a tribunal to hold the balance.
- **Earning capacity** is the question worth arguing: a parent who loses a top-tier job involuntarily
  is not automatically treated as still able to earn that salary — and is not automatically
  released from what both parents planned for their children either.
- The counterpoint to *Devine and Kitson*, where the rules produced a harsh result and the Tribunal
  affirmed it. Here the rules produced a harsh result and the Act had a way out. Same scheme,
  opposite outcomes: that contrast is what an evaluation needs.
