Mr Hallmann, an employee with **serious chronic-illness disabilities**, complained to the **NSW
Anti-Discrimination Board** that **Southern Cross University** had discriminated against him: by
failing to provide an **ergonomic chair** to accommodate his disability, by requiring medical
documentation he said he could not comply with, and by **victimising** him after he complained to the
Vice-Chancellor.

In the substantive decision (**No 2**, December 2025) NCAT **dismissed all of his claims**. This
decision deals only with what happened next: the University asked for **about $129,000** in costs for
the final year of the case, against the self-represented applicant who had lost.

## Held — costs refused

Under **s 60 of the Civil and Administrative Tribunal Act 2013 (NSW)** each party bears its own costs
unless there are **"special circumstances"** (s 60(2)) — which need only be "out of the ordinary"
(*Cripps v Dawson*). The Tribunal was **not satisfied** there were:

- his **non-compliance was reasonably explained by his disability**;
- most of the delay **pre-dated the year claimed**;
- his **non-attendance actually shortened the hearing**; and
- his original claim was **not misconceived**.

Section 36(3) imposes the **same duty on every litigant**, so being self-represented does not by
itself justify costs — but his impairment supplied a reasonable explanation that distinguished the
cases relied on against him. **Each party bears its own costs.**

## Use it for

- **hr.2.11 / hr.2.9 — how human rights are actually enforced in Australia.** No charter: a **state
  statute** (*Anti-Discrimination Act 1977*) makes disability discrimination unlawful, the
  **Anti-Discrimination Board** takes the complaint, and **NCAT** determines it. Name all three and
  the mechanism is complete.
- **Costs as the real barrier to enforcing a right (inv.1.4).** The most valuable thing here is that
  it is a **costs** decision. A $129,000 exposure would deter anyone from testing a discrimination
  claim, and the "each party bears its own costs" rule exists so that it does not. Argue access to
  justice through the costs rule rather than through filing fees.
- **Enforcement without a remedy (hr.t.effectiveness)** — he **lost every discrimination claim** and
  the proceedings ran about **four years**. Use it honestly: the system gave him a hearing, not an
  outcome. That is a stronger evaluation than either "rights are protected" or "rights are not
  protected".
- **Disability as context in procedure, not just in substance** — his impairment explained his
  non-compliance. A good example of a tribunal adjusting **how** it judges conduct, which is what
  substantive equality looks like in practice (and links to the CRPD).
- **Competing rights (hr.1.1)** — his right to test a complaint against the University's interest in
  not bearing unreasonable expense, mediated by a statutory presumption. Neat, small, and
  examinable.
- **Tribunals vs courts (dis.2.5)** — "just, quick and cheap" (s 36 CAT Act) weighed against fairness
  to a vulnerable litigant, with the Tribunal choosing fairness.

## Caution

The library document is a **case study card**, not the judgment — check the decision itself before
relying on any detail. This is a **costs ruling only**: the applicant **lost the substantive case**
(No 2, December 2025), so it is **not a win for the rights-holder** and must not be written up as
one. A **first-instance tribunal** decision, discretionary and on its own facts; it does not hold
that costs are never awarded against unsuccessful applicants. There were **no findings that the
University discriminated** — the ergonomic chair, the medical documentation and the victimisation
claims all failed, and nothing here says the University acted wrongly. Mr Hallmann is named in the
decision; he is also described as having serious chronic illness, so keep the discussion to the legal
issue. Numbered "(No 3)", so there is earlier history that this record does not cover.
