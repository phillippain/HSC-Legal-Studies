A husband of 69, permanently incapacitated since 2012, with **$886,337 of injury payouts** on one
side of the ledger; a wife of 54 who ran the case herself, with thirty years of care and homemaking
on the other. Married 1991, never divorced. The home was rented from 1996 and bought in 2012. Four
trial days across five months; judgment more than six months after final submissions.

## Held — 55.5 / 44.5 of a $947,735 pool

- The four-step approach: identify the pool, assess contributions, adjust for future circumstances,
  then **stand back** and ask whether the result is just and equitable. No presumption of equality.
- Homemaker and carer contributions recognised "not in a token way" — the value shift traceable from
  *Mallet* (1984).
- Cross-allegations of family violence, neither established to the **Briginshaw** standard, and
  neither shown to have affected capacity to contribute (ss 79(4)(ca), 79(5)(a), introduced 2024).
- A **superannuation splitting order** of $155,066.29 from her interest to him, binding on the
  trustee (Pt VIIIB).
- The home: first option to the husband if he pays $133,307.07 within 60 days; second option to the
  wife to refinance and pay $497,692.93; failing both, a detailed sale regime preserving the split.
  Until then she has sole occupancy and pays the outgoings. A registrar may sign for a party who
  refuses (s 106A).
- Both parties had sought 70/30 in their own favour; both were refused.

## Use it for

- **fam.2.3** — the clearest step-by-step property judgment in the collection, with reform doing
  visible work (superannuation splitting; the 2024 family violence provisions).
- **Access to justice** — three days of cross-examination conducted by a litigant in person, a
  $2.45m allegation lost for want of source documents, and a judge protecting a self-represented
  party from her own agreement.
- **Shelter** — renting to owning to fighting over who keeps the home: "I am worried I will be
  homeless", a $294,000 mortgage at about $2,440 a month against a pension of $548 a week, stamp
  duty as a barrier to buying again, and Centrelink's homeowner thresholds shaping the orders.
