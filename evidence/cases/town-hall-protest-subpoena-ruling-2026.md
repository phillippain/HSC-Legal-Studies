**Sixteen protesters**, led by Adam Adelpour, face trial over the **9 February 2026** Town Hall rally
on charges including assaulting police, resisting arrest and throwing missiles; the ABC reports
police have withdrawn **10 of about 30** original prosecutions (police counted **27 arrests** and 10
officers hurt on the night; how 30 relates to 16 people is unexplained). On **16 October** the defence
played a **32-minute** compilation of CCTV and body-worn footage in court — protesters grabbed by the
head or neck during take-downs, and an officer heard urging a colleague to walk a handcuffed man
through faeces. On **19 October 2026 Judge Scott
Nash** rejected the Police Commissioner's objection and found the defence subpoena had a
**legitimate forensic purpose**. The matter returns on **30 October**; trials are expected in
**July 2027**. (NSW magistrates have been titled "judge" since 1 April 2026, with no change in powers.)

## Held (as reported)

- The documents sought — standard operating procedures such as those for capsicum spray, and
  **communications between senior police and the Premier, two ministers and their staff** — had a
  legitimate forensic purpose. The defence wants to show what directions police were given, arguing
  the policing was coordinated "from the top down".
- Police, through barrister Bradley Dean, had argued the subpoena was a "fishing expedition"; they
  now claim **public interest immunity** over some categories, so what is actually produced is still
  to be decided.

## How the two questions are decided

| Step | Test | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Legitimate forensic purpose | More than relevance: it must be "on the cards" the documents will materially assist the defence | *AG (NSW) v Chidgey* [2008] NSWCCA 65 |
| Public interest immunity | Court may exclude documents of state where secrecy outweighs disclosure, weighing the offence and that the **defendant** seeks them; may inspect privately; may make a direction conditional on a stay. A claim needs senior sign-off, usually a confidential affidavit, and alternatives such as suppression orders should be considered | *Evidence Act 1995* (NSW) s 130; Crown Solicitor's PII fact sheet |
| Appeal | A third party (the Commissioner) generally cannot appeal a Local Court subpoena ruling | 2023 Supreme Court ruling, s 53(3)(a) |

## Why lawfulness is the whole case

Assaulting a police officer under *Crimes Act 1900* **s 60** requires the officer to be **in the
execution of duty**. Force is lawful only when reasonably necessary to arrest (**LEPRA s 231**). If
the arrests or force were unlawful — the defence's characterisation of the footage, which police
dispute — the charges may fail at the element, not merely in mitigation. The defence also notes one
officer's statement says the men began praying *after* being told to disperse; CCTV, it says, shows
them already praying.

## Use it for

- **crime.3.7 (use of evidence)** — disclosure, subpoenas and public interest immunity as the
  machinery of a fair trial, with CCTV and body-worn footage cutting both ways.
- **crime.3.6 / crime.3.2** — the Crown must prove every element; the adversary system lets the
  accused compel the state's documents to contest one.
- **crime.2.1** — read with *Lees and Jarrett* (the laws) and *LECC Operation Makalu* (the
  oversight): three forums testing the same policing operation at once.
- **dis.3.2 (individuals in conflict with the state)** — a precise example of legal responses by the
  accused (subpoena) and by NGOs (an open letter from **80 organisations** including NSWCCL and the
  HRLC, 2 June 2026, calling for all charges to be dropped after *Jarrett*).
- **Competing perspectives** — Premier Minns: a lawful dispersal direction was largely ignored and
  police lines repeatedly charged; police: videos taken out of context, officers threatened and
  assaulted; the Supreme Court found the major event declaration was not aimed at suppressing
  protest. Defence: unlawful arrests, excessive force, degrading treatment, likely directed from the
  top down.

## Caution

A **class-made summary** of a **report of an interlocutory ruling with no published reasons**. The
footage was a defence selection and not every clip shows what led to each clash; the arrests, the
force and the officer's account are all in dispute, and every accused is presumed innocent. Charge
and withdrawal counts differ between reports and the "10 of 30" figure is unexplained. The
*Chidgey* and *Sparos* points rest on case notes, not the judgments — check NSW Caselaw before
quoting. The two class summaries cite the force limit as LEPRA s 230 (general) and s 231 (arrest);
both apply. Documents may still be withheld after 30 October. Do not name the accused beyond the
spokesperson the ABC names.
