Ms Silberman **lives with significant disabilities and is not in paid employment**. Interim orders
had been made for the husband to pay her **periodic spousal maintenance**, and — by consent —
**"dollar-for-dollar" costs orders**, under which he funds her legal costs to match his own.

He had been **in default of both for some time**. By the time of her May affidavit she was owed
about **$69,000 plus interest** under the costs orders and **$6,500** in spousal maintenance. She
moved to enforce; that was resolved. He then applied to **vary the maintenance order and discharge
the costs orders**, claiming his financial circumstances had changed.

## Held — application dismissed

The discretion to vary or discharge spousal maintenance is in **s 83**, and it is **not enlivened
unless there is a relevant change**. Behrens J was **not satisfied** that the husband's evidence
about his income and borrowing capacity established any change — his expectation that he could
borrow "ad hoc" sums from a relative had not materialised, and taken individually or together none
of the matters relied on amounted to a sufficient change in circumstances.

On the costs orders, the power to discharge was taken to arise with the power to make them, now in
**s 114UB(2)**. The Court was **not persuaded it was just to discharge** them.

## Use it for

- **fam.2.3 (consequences of separation — property)** — the half of the dot point students forget.
  Property division is one thing; **spousal maintenance** under ss 72 and 75 is an ongoing
  obligation to support a former spouse who cannot support themselves, and this case shows it being
  enforced against resistance.
- **Disability and economic dependence** — maintenance here is doing exactly what the Act designed
  it for: a spouse who cannot work and cannot earn. Good for arguing that the family law system
  responds to **need**, not only to contribution.
- **"Dollar-for-dollar" costs orders (inv.1.4)** — a genuinely useful mechanism to know about. Where
  one party controls the money, these orders fund the other's legal representation so the litigation
  is not decided by who can afford to keep going. A concrete answer to "how does the system address
  inequality between the parties?", and it sits beside legal aid rather than inside it.
- **fam.2.5 / compliance** — the most quotable feature is the sequence: **default, then an
  application to be relieved of the obligation**. The Court's refusal shows that non-compliance is
  not itself a change in circumstances, and that an order remains an order until a court says
  otherwise.
- **Enforcement as a measure of effectiveness** — $69,000 owed under consent orders, and the remedy
  is another application. Useful for the gap between having a right and realising it.

## Caution

**Pseudonyms**; Pt XIVB applies. These are **interim** orders in continuing proceedings, so nothing
here is a final determination of either party's entitlements, and the outcome could change at final
hearing. A **short, first-instance, discretionary** decision: it turns on the husband failing to
discharge an evidentiary burden, not on any new principle. The s 114UB(2) point is expressed
tentatively in the judgment ("presumably arises") — check the provision before stating the power
confidently. The wife's disabilities are relevant to need and should be described no further than
the judgment does.
