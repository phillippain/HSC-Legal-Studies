Two children, **X born 2021** and **Y born 2023**. Proceedings had run since 2024, an **Independent
Children's Lawyer** was appointed, and **two family reports** were obtained (November 2024 and August
2026). The matter was listed for a three-day final hearing from 5 August 2026 — and settled on
4 August, **before any party was cross-examined**. The remaining hearing dates were vacated.

## What the consent orders actually say

This is the value of the record: it is a **complete, real set of final parenting orders**, and most
students have never seen one.

- **Sole decision-making to the mother**, carved out for the children's names and for any change of
  living arrangements that would make time with either parent significantly harder. Before any
  long-term decision she must email her intention, allow the father **14 days** to reply, consider
  his views, and tell him the decision within **7 days**.
- **Seven stages** of increasing time with the father, each measured in **visits, not months**: six
  visits of 9am–1pm fortnightly (the first two **in a public place**), then 9am–3pm, then 9am–5pm,
  then Saturday plus Sunday, then an **overnight weekend — but not before Y turns four**, then Friday
  5pm to Sunday, and finally alternate weekends Friday after school to Monday before school, **not
  before Y turns five**.
- **Supervised changeover** at a community contact service for 12 months, cost shared equally, then
  at a recreation centre or at school or daycare.
- **Communication by email only**, except for a text in an emergency. Children's calls via an app,
  with a make-up call required if a call is missed.
- A long list of conduct restraints: no denigrating the other parent, including on social media; no
  **physical discipline**; remove the children from anyone under the influence; no public social
  media photos of the children.
- Specific restraints: the father may not leave the children with the **paternal grandparents** until
  stage 5, may not let the children be with the paternal grandmother while she is affected by alcohol
  or drugs, and may not let the grandparents **express religious views** to the children or take them
  to church. The mother must refer to her partner as "stepdad" or by name. **Neither parent may take
  the children out of Australia.**
- A built-in **dispute resolution clause**: three FDR practitioners nominated, the other parent picks
  one, costs shared.
- Both parents must **continue with their psychologists** for as long as recommended.

## The one order the judge nearly refused

Order 26 restrains **both** parents from bringing the children into contact with **the mother's
sister**. Hogan J said that but for the Independent Children's Lawyer's submissions she would
probably have declined it: she made it because of the aunt's geographic distance, her health, the
near-absence of past contact, no evidence of real benefit beyond a theoretical one, and both parents'
agreement — and because removing it might later be blamed for undermining the mother's ability to
carry out orders she herself had sought. The father's agreement was recorded as **reluctant**: he
"does not see the necessity of such Order, given that Ms B is the mother's sister".

## Use it for

- **fam.2.5 (legal and non-legal responses) — the real statistic made concrete.** The overwhelming
  majority of family law matters resolve without judgment, and this is what that looks like: two
  years, two family reports, an ICL, and settlement on day two of the trial. Better evidence of the
  **Court as a pressure point for negotiated outcomes** than any figure.
- **fam.2.2 (consequences of separation — children)** — a staged rebuild of a relationship. Quote a
  stage: "not before Y turns four". Time is tied to the child's development, not the parent's claim.
- **Enforceability** — ask which of these orders a court could actually police. Dates and places,
  easily; "speak of the other parent respectfully", much less so. An excellent exercise, and a strong
  paragraph on the limits of enforceability in parenting matters.
- **The Independent Children's Lawyer (inv.1.4)** — the ICL's submissions changed the judge's mind on
  Order 26, and the Court **thanked the ICL for offering to stay on after funding ceased** and
  discharged her after four visits. Funding running out mid-case, with the lawyer working unpaid, is
  a sharp, specific access-to-justice point.
- **dis.1 (dispute resolution)** — orders that contain their **own FDR mechanism** so the next
  disagreement does not come back to court. Good evidence for ADR being built into legal outcomes
  rather than sitting beside them.
- **Religion, grandparents and new partners (fam.1.4)** — three restraints on people who are not
  parties. Useful for how far parenting orders reach into an extended family.

## Caution

**Pseudonyms**; Pt XIVB applies. These are **consent orders**: the Court made them because both
parents agreed, so apart from Order 26 they are **not findings** and there is **no reasoning** about
either parent's conduct. Eleven paragraphs of **ex tempore** reasons. Do not infer why the father's
time was restricted or why changeover is supervised — the judgment does not say, and the family
reports are not public. The detail about the **paternal grandmother and alcohol** and the religious
restraint are terms the parties agreed to, not things the Court found proved. The staged schedule is
fitted to a 5-year-old and a 3-year-old and is not a template.
