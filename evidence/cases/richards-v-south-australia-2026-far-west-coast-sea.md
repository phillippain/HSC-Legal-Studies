This is the most useful recent native title judgment for showing students what recognition costs and what it delivers. Not because the law changed — it did not — but because of the arithmetic of the case.

## The shape of the claim

On **5 December 2013** Mansfield J made a **consent determination** of native title over about **75,249 km²** of land for the Far West Coast People — descendants of Kokatha, Mirning and Wirangu people — from the coast north of Streaky Bay to the Western Australian border (*Far West Coast Native Title Claim v South Australia (No 7)* [2013] FCA 1285). The determination stopped at the **low water mark**. Sea country beyond it was dismissed from that application, expressly without deciding that no native title existed there.

So in **March 2016** the same people filed a sea claim: the waters abutting that boundary, extending **three nautical miles** seaward.

By trial the claim had shrunk to a strip between **30 and 300 metres** wide. The primary judge (*Miller v South Australia (Far West Coast Sea Claim) (No 4)* [2025] FCA 388) then found native title existed only in an "Accessible Area" — **30 metres** along most of the coast, **50 metres** at the eastern end, with the stretch along the Bunda Cliffs excluded entirely because it was not physically accessible at sovereignty. The recognised rights were to speak for the area, to access and remain in it, to take resources **"for purposes limited to domestic consumption and sustenance of family members"**, and to care for and protect significant sites.

Three nautical miles, claimed in 2016, had become a 30-metre ribbon by 2025.

## What the Full Court decided

| Ground | Issue | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Competency objection | Were the answers to separate questions, stated in the reasons at [1505] but never put in a formal order, an appellable "judgment"? | **Rejected.** The judge "intended to pronounce a judgment… and has 'in reality' done so" [64]. Interlocutory, so leave required. |
| Leave | *Decor Corporation* test | **Granted** |
| 1 and 2 | Should native title extend to all claimed waters as sea country, based on spiritual connection rather than physical access at sovereignty? | **Dismissed.** The evidence on spiritual belief "did not rise above generalised assertions as to the extent of sea country". |
| 3(a), (c) | Did the judge misconstrue the 2013 land determination as not recognising a **communal** title held by all the Far West Coast People? | **Allowed.** She based the conclusion on "a part of the land determination that does not purport to say anything about how native title rights and interests are held. To do so was in error" [192]. |
| 3(b) | Refusal to receive the land determination's **reasons** into evidence under s 86(1)(c) NTA | Not made out |
| 4 | Should the right to take resources extend to trade and ceremony, not only domestic consumption? | **Dismissed.** No witness evidence that resources were taken from the sea claim area for trade or ceremony; contemporary evidence emphasised "family gatherings and recreational fishing" [241]–[242]. |

Outcome: leave granted, appeal allowed **in part**; the answer to separate question (b)(i) — *who holds the native title* — set aside and **remitted to a single judge** for re-hearing on the whole of the lay and expert evidence. **No order as to costs**, consistent with s 85A of the NTA. 247 paragraphs; heard 6–7 November 2025; delivered 29 July 2026.

## The legal point worth knowing

Native title under s 223(1) means the **"communal, group or individual"** rights of Aboriginal peoples. That three-way distinction, drawn from Brennan J in *Mabo (No 2)* (1992) 175 CLR 1 at 61–62, decides **who** holds what. A communal determination means every member of the holding group has rights across the whole determination area; a group determination can confine rights to subgroups and sub-areas.

The primary judge had read the 2013 determination as not establishing communal title, and so allowed evidence that native title in the adjacent sea was held only by descendants of **eighteen named antecedents** rather than by the Far West Coast People as a whole. The Full Court held the 2013 determination's Order 9 — rights derived from "the relevant traditional laws and customs of the Kokatha, Mirning **and** Wirangu who comprise the Far West Coast People" — supports "a single communal title", the conjunctive "and" treating them as one holding group [2345 of the text]. Reading it otherwise let in evidence inconsistent with a determination that was already final.

The Court was careful about the limit: a determination over one area "is not determinative" of a claim over another [194]. Connection must still be proved for the new area. But what the earlier determination settled about **how** the title is held cannot be re-litigated next door.

## Use it for

- **hr.2.10 — the role of common law.** The through-line from *Mabo (No 2)* is explicit: a 1992 common law recognition, codified in s 223(1), still deciding in 2026 who holds what. Native title is the standing Australian example of judge-made rights, and this shows the doctrine being applied, not merely cited.
- **hr.2.11 — the role of courts and tribunals.** A textbook illustration of an appellate court's function: correct the legal error, refuse to make the findings itself, send it back. The applicant asked the Full Court to answer the question; it declined — "most appropriately undertaken by a single judge of this Court upon remittal" [246].
- **Delay, as an effectiveness argument with hard dates.** Consent determination 2013 → sea claim filed 2016 → trial judgment April 2025 → appeal July 2026 → remitted for re-hearing, with extinguishment and s 225(c)–(e) still untried. Ten years in, the claim group is undetermined. That is a far stronger accessibility point than any general statement about native title being slow.
- **The gap between recognition and content.** Rights recognised over 30 metres of water, exercisable only to feed family members. Use it against any claim that native title delivers substantive economic benefit, and alongside *Akiba v Commonwealth* [2013] HCA 33, where a commercial fishing right **was** recognised — the difference was the evidence, not the law.
- **Procedural fairness and cost.** No order as to costs under s 85A reflects the NTA's own recognition that claimants should not be deterred by costs risk. Note too that the applicant's first notice of appeal was filed in the wrong form and had to be re-done — an access-to-justice detail, in a case run by a native title representative body with senior counsel.

## Caution

**South Australia, Federal Court, not NSW.** Useful for the Human Rights topic and for native title generally, but it is not a NSW land rights case; the *Aboriginal Land Rights Act 1983* (NSW) scheme is different in kind and should not be conflated with it.

The decision is **interlocutory and partly unresolved**: question (b)(i) was remitted, the parties were still filing submissions on the form of remittal orders within 14 and 28 days of 29 July 2026, and extinguishment had not been decided at all. Do not write that the Far West Coast People "won" or "lost" the sea claim. The accurate statement is that the area and content of the rights stand, and the identity of the holders must be decided again.

Much of the judgment is practice and procedure — whether answers to separate questions are an appellable judgment under s 24(1) of the *Federal Court of Australia Act 1976* (Cth). Interesting, but not an HSC dot point; use it only if the question invites an argument about procedural barriers to review.

The judgment names eighteen apical ancestors and discusses contested family connection between Mirning, Kokatha and Wirangu people. Handle that material with care: the dispute about who holds the title was argued between Aboriginal parties as well as against the State and the Commonwealth, and summarising it carelessly misrepresents living people's identity.
