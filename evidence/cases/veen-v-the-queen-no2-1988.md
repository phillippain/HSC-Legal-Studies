Veen, an Aboriginal man with alcohol-related brain damage, killed a man in 1975 and was convicted of
manslaughter (diminished responsibility). His life sentence was quashed by the High Court in *Veen
(No 1)* (1979) and replaced with 12 years. Weeks after release on licence in 1983 he killed again;
the Crown accepted a plea to manslaughter, and Hunt J imposed **life imprisonment** because Veen was
likely to kill or injure again when affected by alcohol.

## Held — appeal dismissed, 4–3

- "The principle of **proportionality** is now firmly established in this country": a sentence may
  not be extended beyond what is proportionate to the crime merely as **preventive detention**.
- But "the distinction in principle is clear" between that and weighing **protection of society among
  other factors**, which is permissible.
- "The purposes of criminal punishment are various: protection of society, deterrence of the offender
  and of others who might be tempted to offend, retribution and reform." They overlap, and a mental
  abnormality can cut both ways — less culpable, more dangerous.
- On these facts the life sentence stood. Deane J (dissenting): "The common law of this country makes
  no provision for any system of imprisonment by way of preventive detention."

## Use it for

- **crime.4.2 (purposes of punishment)** — the classic High Court list, written before *Crimes
  (Sentencing Procedure) Act* s 3A codified them (1999).
- **crime.4.8 (post-sentencing)** — the reason preventive detention needs a statute: *Kable* (1996),
  *Fardon* (2004) and the *High Risk Offenders Act* are what Parliament did next.
- **Balancing offenders and society** — proportionality as the offender's protection, community
  safety as the counterweight.

## Caution

A 4–3 decision with no agreed alternative among the dissenters. Some printed versions interleave
extracts from later cases; only the 1988 reasons are the judgment.
