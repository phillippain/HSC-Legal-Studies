A Muslim woman from **Hyderabad** arrived in 2015 as her student husband's dependant; their three children
were born in Australia. In 2012 men carrying BJP flags pulled her off her scooter and police would not
register a complaint (the Tribunal accepted this). The family applied for **protection visas** in 2022,
fearing Hindu nationalist violence; a delegate refused in June 2025.

## The tests

| | Refugee (s 36(2)(a), ss 5H, 5J) | Complementary protection (s 36(2)(aa)) |
|---|---|---|
| Threshold | **Real chance** of **serious harm** for a Convention reason, throughout the country | **Real risk** of **significant harm** (death, torture, cruel or degrading treatment) |
| Finding | Discrimination likely, but "the threshold for serious harm is a high one"; harassment falls short | No real risk on removal |

Late claims of threats were found **not credible**. DFAT reports low-to-medium societal discrimination;
NGO data records hundreds of hate crimes a year — numbers the Tribunal weighed against India's population.
The refusal was **affirmed** for all five applicants.

## Use it for

- **hr.3.1 / hr.2.1** — how Australia decides who it owes protection: the Refugee Convention definition
  enacted in statute, applied by a tribunal, within state sovereignty over migration.
- **Evaluating effectiveness** — a high threshold protects the system's integrity but leaves people facing
  real discrimination without protection.

## Caution

A **class case summary** of a single-member decision, not binding. The Refugee Convention is not named in
the reasons. Treat the family's experience with care.
