Kelly Royall died after falling from the sixth-floor bathroom window of the flat she shared with the
accused, during a violent argument. The Crown case was that she fell or jumped **escaping his
attack**. He argued that whatever he had done, **she** caused her own death.

## Held

Conviction for murder upheld. Causation is a question of **fact for the jury**, guided by legal
principles rather than by a single formula:

- the **"but for"** test asks whether the death would have occurred anyway — necessary, but not
  sufficient on its own;
- the accused's act must be a **substantial or significant cause**, an **operating** cause at the time
  of death;
- a victim's act in **reasonably apprehending danger** and trying to escape it does **not** break the
  chain of causation — the "fright and flight" principle. Only a wholly disproportionate or
  extraordinary response, or an intervening act of a different kind (*novus actus interveniens*),
  will do that.

## Use it for

- **Causation** (`crime.1.4`), which the syllabus lists as an element concept and which is otherwise
  hard to evidence. This is the leading Australian authority and it comes with facts a student can
  actually recall.
- **Actus reus** more broadly — causation is the link between the act and the prohibited result, so it
  is where the element is tested in practice, particularly in homicide.
- **Domestic violence** — it is a case about a woman choosing a sixth-floor window over the man in the
  room, and the law's answer is that his conduct caused the death. Useful beside the coercive control
  material, where the same idea — the victim's response measured against the danger created — appears
  in a different form.
- The related principle worth naming: the **"eggshell skull" rule**, that an offender takes the victim
  as they find them, including their beliefs and physical condition.
