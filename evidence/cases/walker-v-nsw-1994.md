Denis Walker, charged in NSW, argued that the criminal law of the colony never applied to Aboriginal people, or that Aboriginal customary law survived as a parallel system after *Mabo (No 2)*. Mason CJ struck the argument out.

## Held

- English criminal law applied to everyone in the colony from settlement; "there is no analogy with native title". Native title survived because it was a right to land the Crown could recognise; a parallel criminal law would contradict the basic principle that all people stand equal before the criminal law.
- "Even if it be assumed that the customary criminal law of Aboriginal people survived British settlement, it was extinguished by the passage of criminal statutes of general application."

## Use it for

- **inv.2.3 / inv.2.1** — the limit of Customary Lore's recognition: it governs kinship, Country and obligation within Communities, but Australian courts recognise it only where a statute or doctrine lets it in (sentencing, native title), never as a competing criminal law.
- **inv.1.7** — the rule of law as equal application, used here against a claim for difference.

## Caution

A summary dismissal at first instance by a single judge, not a Full Court decision; it is authority for the equal-application point and nothing wider. It says nothing about how sentencing courts may consider Customary Lore (see Circle Sentencing and *Bugmy*).
