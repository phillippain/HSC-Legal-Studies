In a dispute with his neighbours over a shared right of way, Mr Campbell — not police or the DPP —
issued court attendance notices charging them under s 4(1) of the *Inclosed Lands Protection Act 1901*
(NSW) with entering or remaining on "inclosed lands" (maximum fine 5 penalty units, $550). The land's
northern boundary was marked by a yellow painted line and survey marks. The Local Court acquitted both
in September 2025; he appealed to the Supreme Court on questions of law, representing himself.

## Held — appeal dismissed, all five grounds rejected

- **"Inclosed" means inclosed.** The words after "including" in the definition explain its breadth;
  they do not create a separate category. The **whole** boundary must be marked by an erection, canal
  or natural feature so that it can be known — **paint is not an "erection"**, and partial inclosure is
  not enough.
- The magistrate's comment on easements was immaterial.
- **Discretion**: even if error had been shown, relief would have been refused — criminal exposure for
  more than two and a half years was out of proportion to a $550 fine.
- **Costs**: the costs shield in *Crimes (Appeal and Review) Act* s 70 covers public prosecutors only;
  the private prosecutor pays the defendants' costs.

## Use it for

- **crime.1.6 (summary offences)** and **crime.2.4 (charge)** — any person may institute a prosecution
  (*Criminal Procedure Act* s 14); the DPP may take one over. A rare real example.
- **crime.4.5 (appeals)** — a prosecutor's appeal against an acquittal lies only on a question of law.
- **Statutory interpretation** of a penal provision, and proportionality as a reason for restraint.
- **shel.1.4 / shel.2.4** — pair with the trespass and squatting card: what "inclosed" land actually
  requires.

## Caution

A **class evidence summary**. The underlying neighbour dispute about the right of way was not decided;
a civil trespass action remains open. Whether the DPP was asked to intervene is not stated.
