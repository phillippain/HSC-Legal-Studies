Anthony Markarian, a heroin addict on parole, drove for a dealer for six months in 2000 and was paid
in heroin; the supply totalled 415 g, above the 250 g commercial quantity. He pleaded guilty and was
sentenced to **2 years 6 months**. On a Crown appeal the NSW Court of Criminal Appeal started at a
15-year maximum, took off about a third, deducted 25% for the plea and added time for further
offences — arriving at **8 years, non-parole 4 years 6 months**. It had also used the wrong maximum
(15 years rather than the 20 for the offence charged).

## Held — appeal allowed and remitted

- A maximum penalty is a **yardstick** — a basis for comparing the case with the worst possible case —
  and it "will rarely be right" to look first to the maximum and deduct proportions.
- Courts "may not add and subtract item by item from some apparently subliminally derived figure".
- Whether a judge used "stages" or "instinctive synthesis" is not itself the test, but "the law
  strongly favours transparency. Accessible reasoning is necessary in the interests of victims, of the
  parties, appeal courts, and the public."
- McHugh J defended instinctive synthesis: "There is no magic number for any particular crime." Kirby J
  criticised it.

## Use it for

- **crime.4.1 (judicial guidelines)** — the High Court's method for sentencing, cited in almost every
  later sentence; it is the foundation for *Muldrock* (2011) on standard non-parole periods and *Hurt*
  (2024) on minimums.
- **crime.4.5 (Crown appeals)** — a sentence tripled on appeal, and then set aside for flawed method.
- **Discretion** — the theme in its clearest form: how much structure, and how much judgment?

## Caution

The High Court did not fix the sentence or say 2 years 6 months was right; it remitted the appeal.
It set no universal rule for or against "instinctive synthesis".
