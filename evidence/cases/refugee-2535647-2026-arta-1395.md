A Hindu woman from Haryana, India, arrived on a visitor visa in March 2023 and applied for a
protection visa. She said her father had forced her into a marriage, that she was repeatedly beaten
by her father and brothers when she tried to leave it, and that a local **Khap Panchayat** had
pressured her over it. A delegate refused the visa in June 2025.

## The credibility findings

| | |
|---|---|
| **No personal affidavit or statement was ever filed**, though affidavits were obtained from her mother, uncle and a friend | Unexplained |
| She told the Tribunal both brothers had tortured her; her mother's affidavit named only one | Unresolved |
| Her uncle, who she said helped prepare the affidavits, made **no mention of family violence at all** | Unexplained |
| Her original application made **no mention of the marriage**; she said she had "blocked it out" | Not credible |
| Her form said she could not relocate within India; her own evidence and her witnesses' contradicted it | Contradicted |

The Tribunal did not accept the events had occurred.

## Use it for

- **Onus and evidence in a human rights claim.** The right not to be forced into marriage is not in
  dispute anywhere; what decided this case was who had to prove what, and with what. Nothing in the
  case turns on whether forced marriage is wrong.
- **A worked example of why documents beat assertion**, and of the cost of self-representation or
  poor preparation — pair it with *Palgrave and Blackwall*, where documents and attendance won, and
  *Billings & Clowes (No 2)*, where non-attendance lost.
- **Family as well as Human Rights**: forced marriage is a defect in consent, and Australian law
  treats it both as a ground of nullity and as a criminal offence. The claim reaches the Family
  option's *legal requirements of marriage* dot point from an unexpected direction.
