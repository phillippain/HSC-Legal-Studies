An English case, included because it shows the cross-border problem more vividly than any Australian
decision in the library — and because the legal structure is close enough to compare.

In England and Wales, intended parents obtain a **parental order** under **s 54 of the Human
Fertilisation and Embryology Act 2008**, which the court considers alongside the child's **lifelong
welfare**. Where the criteria are met the intended parents become the child's only legal parents.

## What happened to Z

A **same-sex couple** engaged a surrogacy agency and were assured the procedures and embryo transfer
would happen in **Cyprus**. The agency was in fact based in a country that **does not permit
surrogacy for same-sex couples**, and advised the couple that **only one of them should sign** the
agreement "to avoid any issues" — so they **deliberately concealed the nature of their relationship
from the surrogate**. The embryo transfer took place in Cyprus; **Z was born in a different country,
where same-sex surrogacy is illegal**; the agency advised them **not to tell the embassy** about
their relationship or that the birth was by surrogacy. Z then had to travel to **a third country** to
obtain a visa to enter the UK.

Three jurisdictions. In two of them same-sex surrogacy was unlawful and the couple's relationship was
hidden. Had it been discovered where Z was born, the agreement would have been illegal and **Z's own
legal status and safety would have been at serious risk** — the authors ask what would have happened
had she needed long-term medical treatment.

The practical result: the intended parents had **little or no control over where their child was
born**, could not bring her to England until she was **eight months old**, and then faced a lengthy
process to have their status recognised.

## Held

The **High Court was critical** of the couple's failure to consider the issues before entering the
arrangement: "Lack of research, lack of legal advice and a naive pursuit of a desire to have a child
were no defence." But **the child's welfare would have been adversely affected** had the court refused
a parental order on public policy grounds, and so **the order was granted**. The judgment contains an
essential, non-exhaustive **checklist of issues anyone considering surrogacy should address first**.

## Use it for

- **fam.3.3 — the clearest illustration that the child's welfare rescues the arrangement.** The court
  condemned the conduct and made the order anyway. The same tension appears in the Australian cases
  (parenting orders refused, prosecutors notified) and this is the sharpest statement of it: **public
  policy loses to the welfare of the child who already exists**.
- **"No defence" (inv.1.4 / inv.1.5)** — judicial criticism of intended parents who did not take
  legal advice. Useful for the limits of good intentions, and for why specialist advice is a real
  access-to-justice issue rather than a formality.
- **fam.1.4 — discrimination designed around.** The whole arrangement was structured to hide a
  **same-sex relationship** from the surrogate and from an embassy, because two countries would not
  permit it. The strongest available evidence that unequal recognition of family forms produces
  deception and risk rather than compliance.
- **Conflict of laws, made concrete (fam.1.2 / inv.6.6)** — embryo transfer in one country, birth in
  another, a visa from a third, recognition in a fourth. Pair with the BBC record's argument for a
  **Hague Conference** instrument.
- **Compare the English and Australian routes (fam.3.2)** — a **parental order** under s 54 HFEA
  against a **parentage order** under the *Surrogacy Act 2010* (NSW), both welfare-based and both
  dealing with arrangements the home state disapproves of. A genuine comparative paragraph.
- **Reform that will not fix it (fam.t.reform)** — the **Law Commission's 2023 final report** on
  surrogacy aims to draw intended parents into UK arrangements rather than overseas ones, which the
  authors say means "little will change" for overseas arrangements and "certainly nothing that would
  prevent the issues that arose in Re Z".

## Caution

The library document is a **practitioner commentary in the Law Society Gazette**, not the judgment —
it is written by barristers to urge intended parents to take legal advice, and that is its angle.
**Read the judgment before quoting anything as a finding**; the authors do not quote the judge
directly except for the "no defence" formulation, and the countries are not named in their account.
This is **England and Wales law**: s 54 HFEA, parental orders and the Law Commission's 2023 report
have **no application in Australia**, so use it comparatively and never cite it as Australian
authority. The child is anonymised as "Z". Note the asymmetry in the Australian comparison — in NSW,
Queensland and the ACT, entering a **commercial** arrangement overseas is a criminal offence, which
was not the issue here. The Law Commission report (2023) had **not been implemented** at the time of
the commentary; check its status before saying what UK law now is.
