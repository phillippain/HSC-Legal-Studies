"FHST" is an **11-year-old Japanese citizen and Australian permanent resident**. Her father, an
Australian citizen, applied for her **citizenship by conferral**. A delegate of the Minister
**accepted she was eligible** but refused the application anyway, using the discretion in
**s 24(2) of the Australian Citizenship Act 2007 (Cth)**: on the delegate's view the family — then
living in the USA — did not intend to reside in Australia or maintain a close and continuing
association with it.

Her father sought **merits review** in the Administrative Review Tribunal, self-represented.

## Held — decision set aside

The Tribunal set the refusal aside and **remitted it with a direction that the s 24(2) discretion
should not be exercised**. On the evidence the family was likely to return:

**Pointing to residence** — a credible, specific plan with a hard deadline (the father's **US visa
expiring in July 2026**); an **NSW property** to move back into and Australian **superannuation**;
tax obligations met; the child lived in Australia **2014–22**, keeps friends here and **identifies as
Australian**; the father **declined a US Green Card sponsorship** and the family holds no US assets.

**Against** — "scant" evidence of a close and continuing association; limited community
participation; no family (only friends) in Australia; no booked flights; "negligible" tax; resident in
the USA since September 2022.

On balance the Tribunal was satisfied the family had "unconditional and credible plans" to return
within three months.

## The three principles

- **Merits review**: the Tribunal stands in the decision-maker's shoes to reach the **correct or
  preferable decision**, not merely to check for legal error.
- **"Likely" means probable, not merely possible** — ordinary-meaning statutory interpretation of the
  residence test (*Ho v Minister*).
- **Policy guides, it does not bind**: the Tribunal is not bound by departmental policy (the
  Citizenship Policy Instructions) and applies it only "in the absence of cogent reasons not to"
  (*Drake (No 2)*). Those instructions themselves require **hardship or disadvantage to a child** to
  be weighed before refusal.

## Use it for

- **hr.2.11 / hr.2.4 — courts and tribunals enforcing rights without a bill of rights.** Australia
  has no enforceable charter, so a child's claim to nationality is protected by a **statute, a
  discretion and a tribunal that can remake the decision**. That is the mechanism, and this is a
  current example of it working.
- **dis.3 (disputes with the state) — the model example.** An individual, a government delegate, an
  independent tribunal, and the decision remade. The ART replaced the AAT in 2024, so a 2026 ART
  decision is also the most current illustration available of the new body.
- **Accessibility, concretely (inv.1.4)** — a **self-represented parent** overturned a ministerial
  delegate's refusal. Low-cost merits review as a genuine check on executive power, and a far better
  answer than "tribunals are cheaper than courts".
- **Discretion is where rights are won and lost** — she was **eligible**; everything turned on a
  discretion in s 24(2). Excellent for arguing that the existence of a legal entitlement is not the
  end of the question.
- **The best interests of the child (croc-1989)** — the CRC principle reaching an Australian decision
  through **departmental policy**, not through directly enforceable law. A precise example for
  "incorporation of human rights into Australian domestic law" (hr.2.7).
- **Rule of law** — a delegate's view of the family's intentions replaced by a tribunal's assessment
  of the evidence, on the record. Use it where a question asks how executive power is constrained.

## Caution

The library document is a **case study card**, not the judgment — a teaching summary, so verify
anything load-bearing against the ART decision itself. The applicant is **anonymised ("FHST")**. A
**merits review decision by a single General Member**: persuasive as an illustration only, **not
binding precedent**, and the outcome is a **remittal with a direction**, so the Minister's delegate
still had to make the decision. Decided **March 2026** on a plan to return by **July 2026** — whether
the family in fact returned is unknown and unknowable from this document. The evidence against
residence was substantial and the Tribunal said so: present it as a **finely balanced** case, not a
clear one. Nothing here establishes a child's right to citizenship; s 24(2) remains a discretion to
refuse.
