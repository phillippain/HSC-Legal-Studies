Chamanjot Singh, about 22, and Manpreet Kaur, 27, married in India in 2008 in a match that let one
come to Australia on a study visa and the other as a dependent spouse. On 29 December 2009, after she
said she had never loved him and would have him put out of the house and out of the country, he
strangled her and cut her throat at least eight times with a box cutter. He offered a plea to
manslaughter; the **Crown refused it** and tried him for murder. The jury acquitted of murder and
convicted of manslaughter because the Crown **could not disprove provocation**.

## The sentence — McClellan CJ at CL, 7 June 2012

**Non-parole period of 6 years, balance of term 2 years.** The judge accepted the provocation was
real but noted "many other recorded instances" more serious, weighing the degree of provocation, the
time for reflection and the violence used; immaturity, his psychiatric condition and likely
deportation were mitigating.

## What happened next

The sentence produced community and media outrage — "Six years for killing sparks call for law
review", *SMH* (2012). A Legislative Council **Select Committee on the Partial Defence of
Provocation** reported in 2013, and the **Crimes Amendment (Provocation) Act 2014 (NSW)** replaced the
defence with **extreme provocation**: the deceased's conduct must be a serious indictable offence, and
infidelity, a non-violent sexual advance or words alone cannot qualify.

## Use it for

- **crime.3.8 (partial defences)** — a partial defence is not an acquittal but a re-labelling, and the
  label carries the sentence with it.
- **Law reform driven by public and media pressure**, with all four stages visible: a decision, an
  outrage, a review, an amendment.
- **Moral and ethical standards** — the objection was not that the law was misapplied, but that the
  law as applied did not match what the community thought murder was.
