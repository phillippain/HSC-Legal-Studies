A long relationship. The husband lives in **Australia**, the wife in **Country B**, and the property
is in both. The wife appeared by **Webex**, and the hearing was timed around the difference between
Hobart and her time zone.

There were **parallel foreign proceedings**: divorce commenced by the wife in Country B, property
proceedings commenced by the husband there. She **never disputed the Australian Court's
jurisdiction** or sought a stay, and the Court found Australia was the correct forum. The orders
required the husband to **discontinue his Country B property proceedings** within seven days.

## The s 79 method after the 2024 amendments

The judgment is useful because it walks the **amended Part VIII** sequence:

1. Identify the **existing legal and equitable interests** and liabilities — *Stanford*.
2. Decide whether it is **just and equitable** to make any order at all.
3. If so, consider **contributions** (s 79(3)(b)(i)) and the parties' **current and future
   circumstances** (s 79(5)).

The judgment notes expressly that what used to be "**added back**" under the pre-amendment *Omacini*
line is now to be **considered within the statutory categories** instead — a change students will
not find in older textbooks.

## Outcome

**77% to the husband, 23% to the wife**, with each retaining the property in the country where they
live, and each otherwise keeping what is in their own name.

## Use it for

- **fam.2.3 (property)** — a worked example of the **post-2024** s 79 process, which is now the law
  and differs from most published explanations. The "no more add-backs" point is the one to note.
- **fam.1.2 (state and federal jurisdiction), extended internationally** — two courts in two
  countries seized of the same marriage. The Australian Court proceeded because jurisdiction was
  never contested, and resolved the clash by **ordering a party to discontinue abroad** rather than
  by claiming power over the foreign court.
- **Enforcement across borders (fam.2.5)** — dividing assets so that **each party keeps what is in
  their own country** is a practical answer to a hard problem: an Australian order over foreign land
  is difficult to enforce. Good evidence that effectiveness sometimes means designing an order
  around the limits of the law's reach.
- **Pair with *Fry & Read (No 2)*** — there a foreign judgment conflicting with Australian orders was
  refused; here the clash is headed off before it happens. Two ways the system handles the same
  problem.
- **Access to justice (inv.1.4)** — a party overseas participating by audio-visual link, with the
  hearing scheduled around her time zone. A small, concrete example of technology widening access.

## Caution

**Pseudonyms**, and the foreign country is anonymised as "**Country B**" — so nothing can be said
about which legal system was involved. Pt XIVB applies. A **Division 2** first-instance decision
turning on its own facts: **77–23 is not a benchmark**, and the reasons for that split (contributions
and the s 79(5) factors) are specific to this couple. The record does not restate the asset pool. The
foreign proceedings were ongoing when the orders were made, so whether the discontinuance actually
happened is not recorded. Check the amended s 79 text directly — this summary follows the Court's
description of the new provisions rather than quoting them.
