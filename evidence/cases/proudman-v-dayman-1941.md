Annie Proudman was convicted under s 30 of the *Road Traffic Act 1934–1939* (SA) of **permitting a
person who did not hold a licence to drive her car**. She said she believed the driver, Hawke, was
licensed. A single judge set the conviction aside; the Full Court of South Australia restored it; she
sought special leave to appeal to the High Court.

## Held — special leave refused; the conviction stands

- **Knowledge is not an element.** The prosecution did not have to prove she knew the driver was
  unlicensed; the "permitting" in s 30 is directed to the driving, not to the lack of a licence.
- **But mistake can excuse** — Dixon J: "As a general rule an honest and reasonable belief in a state
  of facts which, if they existed, would make the defendant's act innocent affords an excuse for
  doing what would otherwise be an offence." The presumption that this applies to a new statutory
  offence "varies with the nature of the offence and the scope of the statute".
- **The burden** of establishing honest and reasonable mistake "is in the first place upon the
  defendant".
- **Her evidence failed**: she had no reasonable grounds. Rich ACJ — "If she did not know she did not
  inquire, and a fair inference is that she did not care."

## Use it for

- **crime.1.3 (strict liability)** — the leading statement of the honest-and-reasonable-mistake
  defence, the one answer available when *mens rea* need not be proved; pair with the *Road Transport
  Act* card for the everyday offences it governs.
- **crime.1.2** — what *mens rea* is for, seen by its absence; *He Kaw Teh* (1985) later set the test
  for when Parliament intends strict liability.
- **Moral and ethical standards** — a person indifferent to a legal duty cannot say "I didn't know".

## Caution

Three judges with three emphases (McTiernan J found *mens rea* in the intent to permit driving); the
mistake rule is Dixon J's statement. An application for special leave, not a full appeal. Absolute
liability offences allow no mistake defence at all.
