He migrated from the United Kingdom to Adelaide in his early teens in 2012 with his parents and four
siblings. He attended three schools, then an autism intervention program, then ceased schooling. He
was diagnosed with **autism spectrum disorder and ADHD**, began using alcohol and drugs, and was
convicted of several offences including crimes of violence. His visa was **cancelled under s 501(3A)
on character grounds** in April 2023.

His protection claim was **structural rather than personal**: he did not say any individual or group
in the UK had threatened him. He said that as an autistic man with limited education, no money, no
property and no contact with UK relatives since 2012, he would be unable to access support services,
would become **homeless**, and would be vulnerable to exploitation, institutional abuse, self-harm
and relapse.

## The inconsistency that sank the claim

In the **cancellation** proceedings his family swore they would sell their home and return to the UK
with him — evidence the earlier Member recorded as "stated and restated … at no time …
equivocally", and which the Tribunal found as a fact. Months later, in the **protection**
proceedings, they said the opposite. The Tribunal preferred the earlier evidence and found the later
claims **"opportunistic"** [39]. Decision affirmed.

## Use it for

- **The hardest question in the Human Rights topic**: persecution requires a persecutor. Destitution,
  disability and the absence of support are real harms with no author, and the refugee framework —
  built in 1951 around state persecution — has no place for them. That is a limitation of
  international law, not of this Tribunal.
- **Deportation as a post-sentencing consideration** (crime.4.8) reaching a person who arrived as a
  child, alongside *Samuelu*.
- **Disability and the CRPD**, arts 12–14, meeting migration law.
- **Shelter crosses in**: homelessness as the harm feared. Few pieces of evidence sit in Crime, Human
  Rights and Shelter at once.

## Caution

Two sets of proceedings with contradictory family evidence — the case is as much about how tribunals
weigh inconsistency across matters as it is about protection.
