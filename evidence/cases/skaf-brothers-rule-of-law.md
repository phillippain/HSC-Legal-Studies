> This case concerns serious sexual offences against teenage girls. The case note itself carries a
> support warning (Kids Helpline 1800 55 1800; Lifeline 13 11 14).

Bilal Skaf (18) and Mohammed Skaf (17) were convicted over a series of aggravated sexual assaults
committed in company on three dates in August 2000 at Greenacre, Bankstown and Chullora. Three
District Court trials before Judge Finnane (2001–2002) returned guilty verdicts; Bilal received an
aggregate **55 years** with a 40-year non-parole period, at the time the longest non-life term
imposed in Australia, and Mohammed **32 years** with 20.

## What happened next — six appeals in eight years

| Year | What changed |
|---|---|
| 2004 | The 12 August convictions **quashed for juror misconduct**; retrial ordered. Sentences recalculated — Bilal down nine years, Mohammed eight |
| 2005 | Sentences **manifestly excessive**: not the "worst category" for the offence. Bilal 28 years, Mohammed 19 |
| 2006 | Retrial in the Supreme Court before Matthews AJ; guilty verdicts; resentenced to 38 and 26 years |
| 2008 | Conviction appeal refused, **sentence appeal allowed** — totality not given adequate weight. Bilal **31 years** (28 non-parole), Mohammed **23 years** (18 non-parole) |

Mohammed Skaf was released on parole in October 2021. In June 2026 he was charged with drug and
proceeds-of-crime offences, refused police bail, and did not apply for bail in the Local Court.

## The jury point — why the retrial happened

Identification was the central issue in the second trial, and much of the evidence concerned how well
a person could be recognised in the park at night. After the verdict, a solicitor unconnected with
the case told the court that the foreman and one other juror had **gone to the park the night before
and tested the lighting themselves**. The foreman said he went "to clarify something for my own
mind… I wanted to be sure my decision was not in any doubt before the verdict."

The Court of Criminal Appeal held the trial had miscarried: it could not be satisfied the jury "would
have returned the same verdict if the irregularity had not occurred". The observations were not
evidence, were not the subject of directions, and could not be tested — "procedural unfairness (denial
of natural justice) as the accused were unable to test the material in any way."

## Use it for

- **crime.3.9 (juries)** — the strongest Australian example of what jury directions are *for*, and of
  a verdict falling because two jurors did their own research.
- **Law reform that followed** — the *Jury Amendment Act 2004* (NSW) made juror inquiries an offence
  carrying up to **two years**, and the *Evidence Act* was amended so a complainant's earlier evidence
  could be used at a retrial, which is the only reason the 2006 retrial could proceed after she
  declined to testify again.
- **crime.4.3 / crime.4.5 (sentencing and appeals)** — aggregate sentencing under s 53A, and
  **totality, proportionality and parity** worked through over four sentencing exercises. Bilal's
  longer term reflects his greater objective seriousness; Mohammed's youth was a subjective factor.
- **crime.4.8 (post-sentencing)** — parole as structured reintegration rather than mercy, and the
  2026 charges as the answer to "does parole shield an offender?"
- **The rule of law** — equality before the law and open justice applied to "the most horrific of
  crimes", which is the case note's whole argument.
