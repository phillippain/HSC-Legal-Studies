Mr and Ms Harlow married in 1980, separated in January 2023 and divorced in March 2024. The husband
started **s 79 property proceedings** in September 2024 and **died the day after making his last
Will**. His **legal personal representatives** were substituted as the applicant, so the property
case is now **the estate against the 77-year-old wife**.

Their adult son, **Mr B Harlow (born 1982)**, says a property is held on a **secret trust** for him
(*Blackwell v Blackwell*). He tried to get into the family law case, and when that failed he kept
going.

## The paper trail

- **Nov 2025** — his application to intervene or be joined is **dismissed**.
- **Dec 2025** — costs against him: **$133,763.87** to the estate and **$95,771.48** to the wife,
  payable out of his share of the estate.
- **Apr 2026** — the **Full Court** dismisses his leave application and his costs appeal. More costs:
  **$122,623.49** and **$125,888.13**. Total so far: **over $478,000**.
- He also runs, in the **Supreme Court of NSW**, a **family provision** claim under s 59 of the
  *Succession Act 2006*, and a **revocation suit** seeking to remove the executors, restrain a sale
  and allege *devastavit*; and he has drawn **$490,000** in interim distributions. A mediation failed.
- **June 2026** — three **special leave** applications to the High Court.
- **July 2026** — this application: **stay the s 79 trial**, vacate the September dates, or restrain
  the parties from settling.

## Why it failed

**He has no right of appeal.** Section 55(1)(b) of the FCFCOA Act bars special leave from a single
judge of Division 1 exercising original jurisdiction, and s 55(3)(c)(i) bars it from a Full Court
decision about joining a party. Both applications were therefore **prima facie incompetent**. His
argument that this "impermissibly destroys the constitutionally entrenched appeal" ran into **s 73 of
the Constitution**, which gives the High Court appellate jurisdiction "with such exceptions and
subject to such regulations as the Parliament prescribes" — and *Cockle v Isaksen* (1957) upheld
exactly that kind of exception.

A stay pending special leave is an "**extraordinary jurisdiction**" requiring **exceptional
circumstances** (*Burgundy Royale*). Campton J found: the prospects of special leave "cannot be
regarded as substantial"; he can still challenge the refusal when appealing any **final** s 79 order
(s 55(4)(a)); and he can bring the secret trust claim in the **Supreme Court**, which is the natural
forum. Against that, vacating the trial would push it to **mid-2027**, with the wife's health
declining, the estate's costs mounting, and the family provision claim and the whole administration
of the estate held up. The **balance of convenience** favoured the parties. Dismissed.

## Use it for

- **fam.2.3 (property) — the limits of s 79.** Property adjustment is a **matrimonial cause**: its
  reach is confined to rights that "have their basis in the marital relationship". A son's equitable
  claim, however genuine, is not a family law claim. The cleanest available illustration that family
  courts are not general-purpose courts.
- **fam.1.2 (state and federal jurisdiction) — one family, four courts.** Family law property,
  Supreme Court family provision, Supreme Court probate revocation, and the High Court. Outstanding
  for showing how **federal and state jurisdiction interlock**, and what it costs a family when a
  dispute is split across them.
- **Court hierarchy and appeals (inv.6 / dis.2)** — the whole appellate structure in one case: first
  instance, Full Court, special leave, and **statutory limits on appeal rights**. Pair it with
  *Cockle v Isaksen* and s 73 for the constitutional point that an appeal to the High Court is a
  right **Parliament may qualify**.
- **Cost as a barrier, from the other direction (inv.1.4)** — over **$478,000** in costs orders
  against one litigant, three sets of senior counsel, and a case that has consumed an estate. Most
  access-to-justice evidence is about people who cannot afford to litigate; this is about what happens
  when someone can.
- **Delay and the overarching purpose (fam.2.5)** — s 95 of the *Family Law Act* requires disputes to
  be resolved "as quickly, inexpensively and efficiently as possible", and the Court used it against
  a further 12-month delay. A quotable statutory standard for any effectiveness question.
- **The human cost** — the wife is **77**, her health is declining, and her solicitor's evidence is
  that the litigation has caused significant stress and anxiety. Use it rather than asserting that
  delay harms people.

## Caution

**Pseudonyms**; Pt XIVB applies. This is an **interlocutory, first-instance** procedural ruling, not
a decision about who owns anything: the **secret trust claim has never been tested**, and nothing
here says it is bad. **Two of the three special leave applications were still pending** when this was
decided, so the outcome is unrecorded — check before saying the High Court refused leave. The costs
figures are **orders made**, payable out of his share of the estate, not amounts shown to have been
paid. The file is a procedural judgment of 82 paragraphs and assumes the earlier rulings
(*Harlow & Harlow* [2025] FedCFamC1F 795, (No 2) [2025] FedCFamC1F 918, [2026] FedCFamC1A 67) — read
those before relying on any finding about the son's conduct. He is described as a litigant whose
submissions were rejected, which is not the same as a finding of bad faith.
