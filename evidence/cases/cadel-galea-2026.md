The mother appealed final parenting orders. It was **common ground** that the children should live
with the father and that he should have sole long-term decision-making responsibility; what she
challenged was the finding that **she** had perpetrated family violence.

The primary judge found the father had committed family violence against her on one occasion in
2023, rejected her other allegations, and found she had committed family violence against him —
**overdosing on Valium on multiple occasions**, pushing him, and **grabbing his arm and using it to
strike her own head** — and that this made him fearful.

## The argument

The mother said the conduct was **self-directed**, happened during a "genuine mental health crisis",
and was "a symptom of her illnesses… rather than conduct properly capable of constituting family
violence". She argued the primary judge had **conflated "fear for" her welfare with "fear of" her as
a perpetrator**.

## Held — appeal dismissed

Schonell J at **[42]–[43]**:

> "The definition of family violence in s 4AB of the Act does not, by words of limitation, prescribe
> the types of behaviours that may constitute family violence… **The definition is wide enough to
> encompass acts of self-harm as constituting family violence.** The precondition that the behaviour
> causes a family member to be fearful can be comfortably satisfied where the family member is
> 'fearful of' the behaviour as it impacts upon them **or 'fearful for' another person, including the
> perpetrator**. To narrowly construe the definition would be to imply words of limitation that do
> not appear in the section."

And the context point, squarely:

> "the fact that the acts of self-harm occurred during a mental health crisis and were symptomatic of
> the appellant's broader mental health condition **does not preclude** such behaviour from meeting
> the definition… **The definition focuses upon the state of mind of the affected family member
> rather than the intention of the perpetrator.**"

Applying the Full Court in ***Pickford & Pickford*** (2024). Evidence that the father was "afraid,
scared or did not feel safe" was enough to find he was fearful. No appealable error; one clerical
error corrected under the **slip rule** (r 10.13(1)(e)); no order as to costs.

## Use it for

- **fam.2.4 (dealing with domestic violence)** — the most important recent authority on **how wide
  s 4AB is**. Most students define family violence as what one person does *to* another; this holds
  that behaviour directed at **yourself** can qualify. A striking, memorable extension.
- **The test is the victim's state of mind** — "fearful of" or "fearful for". That single
  distinction is worth more in an answer than a paragraph of general description, and it is the
  reason the appeal failed.
- **fam.2.2 (consequences of separation — children)** — note what was *not* in dispute: both parents
  accepted the children should live with the father. The fight was over the **characterisation** of
  the mother's conduct, because that drives risk, and risk drives everything else in Pt VII.
- **The hard question for a 25-marker** — is it just to label conduct arising from mental illness as
  violence, when the person is unwell and the harm is to herself? The Court's answer is that the Act
  protects the family member who is frightened, not the label. Students can argue either way and
  cite this.
- **inv.4.5 / chg.3.3 (courts and parliament, judicial methods)** — a court reading a statutory
  definition **broadly by refusing to imply words into it**, and law developing case by case from
  *Pickford* to here.

## Caution

An **appeal on the evidence**, so most of it is about whether findings were open to the primary
judge, not about what the law is. Only the s 4AB discussion at [42]–[43] is the usable statement of
principle; the rest turns on *Edwards v Noble*, *Gronow* and *Lee v Lee* restraint. The names are
**pseudonyms** and **Pt XIVB of the Family Law Act makes it an offence to publish an account that
identifies the parties** — never add detail. The mother's mental illness is central and should be
written about with care: the Court did **not** find she was to blame for being unwell, only that the
effect of the conduct met the definition. The evidentiary rulings (treating psychiatrist excluded
under r 7.01(1)(a), video recordings under s 135 of the *Evidence Act*) are separate points and are
not summarised here.
