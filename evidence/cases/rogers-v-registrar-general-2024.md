Joseph Saric, a Croatian migrant and ship's engineer, died of cancer in July 1968 — divorced, out of
contact with his family, without a will — owning a one-bedroom fibro cottage at Gymea Bay. Ross
Paul, who lived nearby, notified authorities, organised the funeral two days later, and then "treated
Mr Saric's fibro cottage at Gymea Bay as his (Mr Paul's) for the next nearly 50 years until his own
death in 2018": he split the cost of a fence with a neighbour, paid rates and electricity, insured it,
and rented it out from at least 1995. After Paul died his executor asked the Court for orders so the
cottage could be sold as part of Paul's estate. It sold in 2024 for almost **$1.5 million**.

## Held — Kunc J, 16 May 2024

- **Adverse possession made out.** There was "no evidence that Mr Paul went into possession under a
  lease, licence or any other form of consensual relationship with Mr Saric's estate or heirs"; the
  Court inferred he took possession from **1 January 1969**, given the "substantial windfall or gift"
  the cottage represented.
- **Which limitation law applied.** Because time began running before 1971, the *Limitation Act
  1969* (NSW) did not apply (s 6(1)(c)); the *Real Estate (Limitation of Actions) Act 1837* did — a
  **20-year** period with a **40-year** long-stop. "Any cause of action to recover possession of the
  cottage by Mr Saric's legal personal representatives was completely extinguished from
  1 January 2009."
- A declaration of possessory title: the executor was entitled to be registered as proprietor. The
  Registrar General and the Chief Commissioner of State Revenue filed submitting appearances; the
  application was unopposed.

## The rule today

Under the *Limitation Act 1969* (NSW) a person who possesses another's land **continuously for
12 years**, without force, without secrecy and without the owner's permission, can defeat the
owner's claim; a **30-year** long-stop applies even where the 12 years cannot start running (owner
under a disability, or dispossession not reasonably discoverable). Doing what an owner does —
fencing, paying rates, keeping others out, renting it out — is what counts. Barrister Patricia Lane:
"the rights to land are based loosely on a concept of 'use it, or lose it'."

## Use it for

- **shel.2.4 / shel.1.4 (securing other types of shelter; squats)** — the syllabus's "squats" dot point
  with an actual NSW judgment behind it: the *only* right a trespasser starts with is that the owner
  use reasonable force to remove them, yet long enough possession ripens into title.
- **shel.2.1 (systems of registration)** — Torrens title is "indefeasible" but not absolute: possessory
  title is one of the exceptions, and the Registrar General was a party.
- **shel.t.values / shel.t.compliance** — the law rewarding a non-owner for fifty years of quiet
  non-compliance, because the heirs never asserted their rights: "like all claims to land, there's a
  time limit" (Prof Cathy Sherry).
- **Contrast with *Willis v Abraham (No 2)*** — the same doctrine failing where occupation was
  concealed and permission was claimed.

## Caution

A **class record built from the SMH court report** (Michaela Whitbourn, 4 August 2024) and a
secondary case note; the judgment itself was not read here. The case was **unopposed**, so no
competing claim was tested. The 1837 Act periods applied only because possession began before
1971 — quote the modern 12/30-year rule for current cases. Pseudonyms do not apply (a probate
matter), but treat the individuals as the report does.
