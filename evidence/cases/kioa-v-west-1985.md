Mr and Mrs Kioa, Tongan nationals who had overstayed, were ordered deported. The decision-maker
relied on a departmental report containing **adverse allegations they had never been told about** and
had no chance to answer.

## Held

The deportation order was invalid. The **rules of natural justice** — the **hearing rule** and the
rule against **bias** — are presumed to apply to the exercise of a statutory power that affects a
person's rights, interests or legitimate expectations, and can be excluded only by clear legislative
language. Fairness is not an all-or-nothing standard: **what it requires depends on the
circumstances**, but at a minimum a person must know the substance of what is said against them.

## Use it for

- **Procedural fairness** (`ls.1.6`), which the syllabus asks about directly and which most answers
  can only define. This is the case that makes it a legal rule rather than a principle.
- The **two rules stated separately**: no one may be a judge in their own cause (*nemo iudex*), and
  both sides must be heard (*audi alteram partem*). Naming them, then showing the hearing rule
  operating on an adverse report, is the difference between a definition and an application.
- **Administrative review** (`ind.2.5`, `ind.2.6`) — procedural fairness is the main ground on which
  a government decision is judicially reviewed, so this case sits underneath the whole external
  review topic.
- As a contrast to **custody safeguards** in Crime: the same idea — you must be told what is alleged
  — appears there as the s 122 LEPRA caution.
