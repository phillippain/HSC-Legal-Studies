Two children, born 2008 and 2011, with a child support case registered since 2009. For years the
parents ran a **private collection arrangement** — an assessment existed but Services Australia did
not enforce it. On 11 December 2023 the mother applied for the agency to collect, and for arrears
over the preceding three months. Arrears: **$7,445.39**.

The father accepted he had paid her nothing in that window, but said he had met house payments, car
payments, food, phones and wi-fi, and that those should be credited. He also disputed the recorded
care percentages: he said they had not separated until May 2023 and had gone on living in the same
home while he worked fly-in fly-out. The mother told Child Support they separated in 2008.

## Held

| Issue | Outcome |
|---|---|
| Should the collection application be accepted? | **Must be accepted** |
| Are the three months' arrears payable? | **Payable** |
| Can the third-party payments be credited? | **No credit** — ss 71, 71A, 71C all require that both parents intended the payment *in lieu of* the assessed liability |
| Were the care percentages wrong? | **No jurisdiction to review them** |

Set aside and remitted: the figure now stands expressly contingent on care percentages the Tribunal
could not review.

## Use it for

- **Private ordering and its limits.** A private arrangement is only as good as the goodwill behind
  it; the moment one parent asks the agency to collect, three months of arrears become an
  enforceable debt as of right, and up to nine months lies in the Registrar's discretion.
- **Jurisdiction as a limit on justice** — the cleanest example in the set. A tribunal that suspects
  a decision is wrong but has no power over the question is a real constraint on effectiveness, and
  it is not a criticism of the tribunal.
- Twenty-eight months from the father's objection to the decision, **twenty of them inside the
  agency's own internal objection stage**.
