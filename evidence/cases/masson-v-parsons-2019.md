## Held

A **known donor who intended to parent, and did**, can be a parent for the purposes of the
*Family Law Act 1975* (Cth). The donor was recorded on the birth certificate, supported the child
financially and was actively involved in her life.

The **state presumption did not survive the federal question.** Section 14 of the *Status of
Children Act 1996* (NSW) deems a man who provides semen not to be the father. The High Court held
that provision did not apply in proceedings under the *Family Law Act* — the Commonwealth Act's own
scheme governed, so the state deeming provision was not picked up.

**"Parent" is a question of fact, not of definition.** The Court held the word carries its
**ordinary, contemporary Australian meaning**, and that whether a person is a parent is answered by
the facts of the relationship rather than by a label like "sperm donor".

## Use it for

- **fam.3.3 (surrogacy and birth technologies)** — the case that decides *who counts as a parent*
  when a child is conceived artificially. Any answer that says a donor can never be a parent is
  wrong after 2019.
- **fam.1.5 (rights and obligations of parents and children)** — the practical stakes: parental
  responsibility, and in this case the mother and her partner could not simply relocate the child to
  New Zealand.
- **inv.5.1 / inv.5.2 / inv.4.1 (division of powers, court hierarchy)** — an unusually clean illustration of
  Commonwealth and state law meeting on the same question and the Commonwealth Act prevailing in
  federal proceedings. Pairs with the international surrogacy line, where the same relationship
  between ss 60H and 60HB and state surrogacy Acts produces the **opposite** result for intended
  parents.
- **fam.t.values (changing community values)** — "ordinary, contemporary Australian understanding"
  is the Court saying in terms that the meaning of parent moves with the society, which is a gift for
  a themes-and-challenges answer.
- **The limit, which students miss** — this does **not** make every donor a parent. It turns on a
  **known** donor with an ongoing role. A clinic donor with no involvement and no intention to
  parent is not covered, and the decision does not disturb that.

## Caution

The record in the library behind this is a **short explainer with the appearance of an AI-generated
summary** (numbered reference markers, no pinpoint citations), so **do not quote it**: read the
judgment or a law-firm case note before using any phrase as the Court's. In particular, check the
precise constitutional route before writing about it — the explainer describes it loosely as federal
law "overriding" state law, which is not quite how the Court put it. The parties are pseudonymised.
The case is about **artificial insemination between known parties**, not a surrogacy arrangement, so
do not use it to say anything directly about surrogacy parentage.
