After years of trying and **ten IVF cycles**, the Farnells engaged **commercial surrogacy in
Thailand** — unlawful for NSW, Queensland and ACT residents to arrange overseas, and unlawful in every
Australian state. In **December 2013** the surrogate, **Pattaramon Chanbua**, gave birth to twins,
Pipah and Gammy, from Mr Farnell's sperm and donor eggs. The Farnells returned to Australia with
**Pipah** and left **Gammy**, who has **Down syndrome**, in Thailand; Ms Chanbua said they had
rejected him. Mr Farnell was a **convicted child sex offender**.

Ms Chanbua applied for an order returning Pipah to Thailand.

## Held

The judge framed it as a choice between two unsatisfactory options: leave Pipah with the Farnells,
where she was well cared for and bonded, or return her to uncertain care and unguaranteed safety.
Staying was the **least unsatisfactory option**. Despite grave concern about leaving a child with a
convicted sex offender, the court accepted expert evidence of a **low risk of reoffending** and
imposed protective measures.

## Use it for

- **Surrogacy** (`fam.3.3`) — the case that made the issue national, and the one that shows the law's
  central problem: the prohibition on commercial surrogacy is **unenforceable across a border**, and
  once a child exists, the only question a court can ask is what is best for that child *now*.
- **The best interests principle producing an uncomfortable outcome.** Say that plainly; it is the
  strongest evidence that "best interests" is a comparison between available futures, not a reward for
  good conduct.
- **Gammy's position** — no Australian court could make any order about him. A child conceived for an
  Australian couple, left in another country, outside the reach of the law that was supposed to
  prevent the arrangement.
