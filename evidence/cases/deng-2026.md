A mother from China, in Australia since 2016 — student visa, English course, qualifications by 2020,
then work in Canberra and her own apartment — and her son, born here in 2024. The father **cannot be
named, served, or asked to consent to anything**; the birth certificate records him as unknown.
Home Affairs set a deadline, and only a court order could meet it. Self-represented, with a Mandarin
interpreter, heard and decided the same day.

## Held — three orders, ex tempore

- Under **r 2.34**, service of the initiating application is **dispensed with**.
- The child lives with his mother.
- The mother has **sole parental responsibility** and decision-making authority for all major
  long-term issues.

The reasons record why: the orders "are necessary to provide legal certainty for the child, noting
that the Mother is not a citizen or permanent resident of Australia" (at [73]) and give stability to
his care and living arrangements.

## The three steps that made it possible

- **Standing** — s 65C: a parent may apply, and the birth certificate records the other parent as
  unknown.
- **Service** — r 2.34: finding him would be "an almost impossible task for the Mother" (at [60]);
  she knew a first name, returned to the pub three times and asked the staff.
- **Jurisdiction** — s 4(1) defines a "proceeding" as one "whether between parties or not": "I infer
  from that definition that it is not necessary that there always be two parties in a proceeding, or
  an applicant and a respondent, before the matter can be determined" (at [47]). *Kenneally v
  Kenneally* (2012) 269 FLR 171, *Re J and M* (2004) 32 Fam LR 668 and *Re Yarnall* [2021] FCCA 241
  — a donor case, an unknown father and a deceased mother — show the jurisdiction is "sufficiently
  flexible to permit orders to be made where there is no respondent, no identifiable father or no
  surviving parent" (at [55]); there is no need for "exhaustive investigations merely to create a
  respondent". Not separately ordered — the express permissions she
sought to make all welfare decisions and to travel internationally — because the three orders carry
that authority on their face.

## Use it for

- **fam.1.1 (the concept of family law)** — the jurisdiction tested at its edge: a system built
  around two parents, applied where there is only one, on the strength of a definition in s 4(1).
  The authorities that make it work (*Re J and M*, *Re Yarnall*) come from **donor conception**, so
  birth-technology law supplies the rule for a child conceived by chance.
- **fam.3.2** — sole parental responsibility granted not to resolve a dispute but to **record one
  that never existed**, and to make it provable to another arm of government.
- **Access to justice** — a self-represented applicant, an interpreter, an ex tempore judgment on
  the day, and a deadline three weeks out. Effectiveness measured in days.
- **hr.3.1 / CROC** — a rare domestic judgment that names the **Convention on the Rights of the
  Child**, which reaches the child through **s 60B**, not on its own force. Without the order, "the
  Mother and child's immigration status and ability to remain in Australia may be affected".
- **fam.3.4** — the safety checklist in s 60CC(2A) was still worked through, and raised nothing.

## Caution

**Unopposed**: no respondent appeared, so nothing here was contested and no finding was tested by
another party. The Court did the testing itself, with questions from the bench. It is authority for
*how* a court proceeds in that situation, not for any contested proposition. Summary prepared for
class from the judgment; quotes checked against the judgment text. The six-page class summary of 22 October 2026 replaced the earlier
four-page version.
