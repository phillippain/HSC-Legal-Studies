A Tamil man who ran a business in Sri Lanka made three linked claims: **imputed political opinion**
(that agents targeted him over social media posts of business designs seen as promoting LTTE
motifs), **family association** (repeated enquiries about two brothers with an adverse profile), and
**particular social group** (targeted for extortion as an affluent Tamil business owner). His matter
had already been remitted once by the Federal Circuit and Family Court.

## Held — decision affirmed

The Tribunal concluded he "attempted to migrate via a visitor visa involving fraud, manufactured a
protection claim through social media posts when that failed, and fabricated accounts of past harm
and illegal departure" [119]. It found he had departed lawfully, by air, without questioning. Once
credibility collapsed, the factual foundation of every claim collapsed with it.

## The question worth teaching

**Even if he faced prosecution for the posts, would that be persecution?** Sri Lanka bans glorifying
the LTTE. Because the LTTE was an exclusively Tamil organisation, Tamils are statistically far more
likely to breach that law.

The Tribunal still had to decide it, because **a finding on credibility does not dispose of a claim
that the law itself is persecutory**. That is the discipline students most often skip: a witness
being disbelieved does not answer a question about the law.

## Use it for

- **Characteristics of a just law** (Preliminary ls.1.4) with a real dilemma: a law that is neutral
  on its face, directed at a genuine security purpose, and falls overwhelmingly on one ethnic group.
  Indirect discrimination in a form students can argue about.
- **A law and a lie in the same case** — a strong illustration that the two are decided separately.
- Links across to **international crime**: proscribing an organisation and its symbols is a domestic
  measure against a transnational problem, with a human rights cost.
