**Crimes Act 1900 (NSW) s 93X** (2012) makes it an offence to *habitually consort* with convicted offenders after an official police warning (maximum 3 years). Three people charged with consorting challenged the law in the High Court.

## Held

- The offence **burdens the implied freedom of political communication**, because consorting can include political association, but the burden is justified: the law's purpose, preventing or impeding organised crime, is legitimate, and the offence is reasonably appropriate and adapted to it. The law is **valid**.
- The Court rejected the argument that the Constitution contains a free-standing freedom of association.
- The warning requirement and the defences (family, work, education, legal advice, health) were part of what made the burden proportionate.

## What happened next

The NSW Ombudsman's review (2016) found the offence was used heavily against **Aboriginal people and young people**, often for minor offending, and recommended changes; Parliament added further defences. The 2026 organised crime reforms added interim serious crime prevention orders alongside consorting.

## Use it for

- **Police powers and the balance of rights:** a preventive power (no proven offence) upheld as proportionate. Contrast *Lees* and *Jarrett* (2025–26), where protest powers failed the same test.
- **Rule of law:** the courts test Parliament's laws against the Constitution; here the law survived.

## Caution

The case upholds the law; the criticism is about how police use the discretion to warn and charge, which is the Ombudsman's point, not the Court's.
