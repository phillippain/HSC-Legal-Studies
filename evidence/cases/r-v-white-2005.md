Phyllis Jane O'Brien was murdered at Katoomba in August 2003; police recovered a DNA profile from
material under her fingernails. Herman White was in custody on an unrelated matter in November 2003
when he **asked for a cigarette**. The butt he discarded was collected and analysed, and the profile
matched. He was charged with murder, and the defence challenged the DNA evidence.

## Held

The collection did not contravene the *Crimes (Forensic Procedures) Act 2000* (NSW) and was
**admissible**. The judge weighed the **gravity of the offence**, and the fact that the cigarette was
**requested by the accused rather than offered by police**. The desirability of admitting the evidence
outweighed the undesirability of admitting evidence obtained in that way.

## Use it for

- **Forensic evidence and the limits of the forensic procedures regime** — the Act governs procedures
  *carried out on* a person; it says much less about what a person leaves behind. That gap is the case.
- The **s 138 balancing exercise** made concrete: seriousness of the offence on one side, the manner of
  obtaining on the other. Contrast with *Rondo*, where the balance went the other way.
- **Rights of suspects in custody** — a suspect held on one matter was, in effect, sampled for another
  without an order. Good for arguing that custody safeguards protect the formal process and not the
  informal one.
