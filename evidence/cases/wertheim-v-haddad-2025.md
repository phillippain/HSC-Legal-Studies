Peter Wertheim of the **Executive Council of Australian Jewry** sued over a series of lectures by
Sydney preacher **Wissam Haddad**, delivered to about 400 people and recorded and published online.
**Stewart J held the speeches breached s 18C** of the *Racial Discrimination Act 1975* (Cth) and
ordered the material removed.

## The questions the case turned on

| | |
|---|---|
| **s 18C** | Was the conduct "reasonably likely to offend, insult, humiliate or intimidate", done **because of race or ethnicity**, and "otherwise than in private"? Jewish people are an **ethnic group** for the Act, which is why a religious audience did not take it outside the section |
| **Public or private** | Haddad initially argued the sermons were private, but conceded they were delivered to about 400 people and recorded and broadcast |
| **s 18D** | The exemption for anything said reasonably and **in good faith** for genuine religious, academic, artistic, scientific or public-interest purposes. Haddad relied on religious instruction rooted in scripture; the applicants argued the language was deliberately provocative and so not in good faith |
| **Constitutional limits** | The **implied freedom of political communication** (*Lange v ABC*) and s 116. The respondent argued s 18C must not be read to restrict religious teaching or political discourse |
| **Remedies** | The applicants sought **injunctions, removal of the material and a corrective notice — not damages** |

**The line the judgment drew matters as much as the result**: Stewart J was clear that **criticising
Israel is not in itself antisemitic and does not breach the Act**. What offended s 18C was the
racial characterisation of Jewish people.

## Use it for

- **hr.3.1 (a contemporary issue)** — a current Australian case where two rights collide directly:
  freedom from racial vilification against freedom of religion and political communication. Better
  than an abstract "rights sometimes conflict" paragraph because a court had to choose.
- **hr.2.11 / hr.2.9 (courts and statute law)** — Australia has **no general statutory right to free
  speech**, only the implied freedom, and the RDA is ordinary legislation. Shows how rights are
  actually protected here: by a statute, enforced by a court, with the Constitution as an outer
  limit rather than a source of positive rights.
- **Remedies as a measure of effectiveness** — removal and a corrective notice rather than damages.
  Good for asking what the point of a vilification action is: compensation, or stopping the speech?
- **Compare with the NSW response** — the Minns government criminalised incitement to racial hatred
  in early 2025 and excluded faith and LGBTIQ+ groups. *Wertheim* is the **civil federal** route;
  the NSW laws are the **criminal state** route. Set them side by side for the different ways a legal
  system answers the same problem.
- **hr.1.1 / hr.t.values** — the case asks whether protection from vilification extends to speech a
  speaker sincerely holds as religious doctrine.

## Caution

**The document in the library is not the judgment.** It is a pre-decision summary of the parties'
*arguments*, written while the case was still running — it says "the outcome will define the
boundaries", refers to "the prosecution" (this was a **civil** proceeding, so there was none), and
has the look of an AI-generated brief with no pinpoint citations. **Read the judgment**, or the
Human Rights Law Centre case summary, before quoting anything as the Court's reasoning. The
outcome stated here was confirmed from reporting of the decision, not from the library document.
Be careful and precise with the offensive language: quote it only where the point requires it, and
attribute it to the findings. There was a companion worksheet for students, now filed as a teaching
resource rather than evidence.
