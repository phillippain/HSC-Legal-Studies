The property trial finished on **18 February 2026** and Johns J **reserved judgment**. On that last
day, consent orders were made requiring the husband to give the wife's solicitors **at least 72
hours' written notice** before signing any contract or heads of agreement to sell either of two
businesses, or before changing office holdings, transferring shares or altering corporate
structures — and giving the wife **liberty to list the matter urgently** if she was notified.

On **25 February** he gave notice of his intention to sign heads of agreement to sell one of the
businesses. Her lawyers asked for a signed copy and proposed joint conduct of the sale with proceeds
held on trust. **He did not reply.** The matter was called on at **4.15pm on 3 March**. The signed
heads of agreement arrived by email shortly after the hearing began.

## The orders

The husband was **at liberty to sell** (by consent), but the broker must be authorised to **deal
directly with the wife's solicitors** on due diligence, the contract and the final price; and the
proceeds must go first to the costs of sale, second to the secured bank, and the **balance into the
wife's solicitor's interest-bearing trust account** until judgment is delivered.

He opposed both. His objection to the broker order was that the wife's involvement would "**spook**"
purchasers — for which "he adduced no evidence". The Court noted the **background of allegations that
he had been reluctant to disclose his financial interests**, and that "through the course of the
proceedings, there were many instances where the husband's adherence to his obligations around
disclosure was found wanting". On the proceeds, he claimed more secured creditors than the wife
accepted but had led no evidence of any at trial, and conceded as much; being told by his business
advisors not to agree was "not a basis for refusing the order".

## Use it for

- **fam.2.3 (property) — the gap between the hearing and the judgment.** Students assume the asset
  pool stays still. It does not: the husband was free to sell a significant asset while the judge was
  writing. Excellent for the **practical difficulty of dividing property that is still being traded**.
- **Disclosure (fam.2.5 / enforceability)** — the whole order is a response to a disclosure record
  "found wanting". **Full and frank disclosure** is the duty that makes property division work, and
  this is what a court does when it doubts a party will comply: not a penalty, but a mechanism that
  routes the information around him.
- **Self-representation (inv.1.4)** — the wife had counsel at trial and solicitors here; the husband
  was a **litigant in person**, and lost on the point where he led no evidence. A precise, non-emotive
  example of how representation affects outcomes: the law did not change, the evidence did.
- **Responsiveness, measured in hours** — notice on 25 February, listed and decided on 3 March at
  4.15pm, **23 paragraphs, ex tempore**. The best available answer to "can the legal system respond
  quickly?" — and worth contrasting with the same system's delays elsewhere.
- **"Liberty to apply" clauses (dis.1.8)** — the order worked because an earlier consent order built
  in both a **notice requirement** and a **fast route back to the same judge**. A concrete drafting
  technique, and a good argument that enforceability is designed in advance.

## Caution

**Pseudonyms**, with the businesses, the bank, the broker and the town all anonymised; Pt XIVB
applies. Extremely short, **ex tempore**, and **interim** — the final property judgment was still
reserved, so nothing here says how the pool was divided. Order 1 was made **by consent**. The remarks
about the husband's disclosure are the judge's assessment in the context of this application, drawn
from proceedings whose reasons are not in this document; report them as what the Court said, not as a
finding of dishonesty, and note the Court did **not** find he was hiding assets. The asset pool,
values and the parties' circumstances are not stated at all.
