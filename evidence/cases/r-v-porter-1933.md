Bertram Porter, separated from his wife and caring for their 11-month-old son, made "desperate but
unsuccessful efforts" at reconciliation, showed signs of a nervous breakdown and went three nights
without sleep. On 28 November 1932, after his wife's final refusal, he gave the child strychnine and
was about to take it himself when police arrived; the child died. Because the Territory had no
Supreme Court yet, he was tried for murder in the **High Court's original jurisdiction**, before
**Dixon J** and a jury.

## Dixon J's charge to the jury

1. **The Crown goes first** — every person is presumed sane; the Crown must prove the act, the intent
   and the death beyond reasonable doubt, or there is "a plain verdict of not guilty".
2. **A disease, not a mood** — the mind must be in a state of "disease, disorder or disturbance";
   "excitability, passion, stupidity, lack of self-control and impulsiveness" are "quite different
   things".
3. **Two heads** — the accused did not know the nature of the act, or did not know it was **wrong** —
   wrong "according to the everyday standards of reasonable people", about that act at that time.
4. **The prisoner proves it** — "on the balance of probability", not beyond reasonable doubt.

The jury returned **not guilty on the ground of insanity**. The High Court adopted the charge on the
burden of proof in *Sodeman* (1936) and on "wrong" in *Stapleton* (1952).

## Use it for

- **crime.3.8 (complete defences)** — the mental illness defence; NSW now states it in the *Mental
  Health and Cognitive Impairment Forensic Provisions Act 2020* s 28, with the special verdict "act
  proven but not criminally responsible" (s 30).
- **crime.3.6** — the one common-law exception to *Woolmington*: here the burden is on the accused.
- **Moral and ethical standards** — "wrong" is measured by the community's ordinary standards, not by
  whether the act was unlawful.
- **Why the law excuses** — Dixon J: punishment exists to deter, and it is useless to punish someone
  who cannot understand what they did.

## Caution

A charge to a jury, not an appellate judgment, and it cites no cases; the rules it explains are the
*M'Naghten* rules (1843). The order made after the special verdict is not in the report. Use modern
NSW terminology ("mental health impairment") when writing about current law.
