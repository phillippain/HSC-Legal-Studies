The couple cohabited from 1999, married in 2000, had **five children**, and divorced in 2015. From
the first birth Ms Cosgrove stopped work to run the home and care for the children while Mr Connor
built his career. After separation she had sole parental responsibility; he had limited contact.

He owed **$31,000 in unpaid child support** and told the court he was homeless and living on debt. He
held a **commerce degree**, had worked for a bank and was pursuing a wealth-creation business; the
court found he **had the capacity to work and was seeking to avoid paying**. He argued Ms Cosgrove
could work full time — the court rejected that; she had returned to part-time work at **$350 a week**.

The pool was about **$350,000** in assets and superannuation, against a joint debt of **$172,000**
that Ms Cosgrove negotiated down to just over **$50,000**.

## Held

Ms Cosgrove received **80%** of the pool, and was to pay the remaining $50,000 debt from it.

## Use it for

- **Property after separation** (`fam.2.3`) — the four-step approach in practice: identify the pool,
  assess **financial and non-financial contributions**, weigh **future needs** (care of five children,
  earning capacity, health), and ask whether the result is **just and equitable**.
- **Non-financial contributions valued in money.** The judge's reasoning — that her work at home
  enabled his career — is the clearest short statement of why homemaking counts.
- **Child support avoidance** as a factor the court can see through: a degree, a career and a claim of
  destitution did not survive contact with the evidence.
