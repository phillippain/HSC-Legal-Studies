A Thai national who first came to Australia to study in **2002** and has not returned home since
**2015**, the year he learned he was **HIV positive**. He applied for a protection visa in November
2019. A delegate refused it in **June 2022 without even interviewing him**, on the basis that his
application gave minimal detail — in the application itself, asked whether he had been harmed in
Thailand, he had answered "no".

The Tribunal set that aside and remitted the application on the basis that he satisfies
**s 36(2)(a)** of the *Migration Act 1958* (Cth): he is a refugee.

## Why the usual answer did not work

The Tribunal accepted the **country information against him**. Thailand integrated HIV services into
universal health coverage in 2006, has provided antiretroviral therapy to everyone since 2014
regardless of CD4 count, and has largely met the "90-90-90" targets; a **gold card** under the
Universal Coverage Scheme gives free care, and the Tribunal rejected his claim that he would be
ineligible to re-register after years abroad. His own drug, **Biktarvy**, is not on Thailand's
National List of Essential Medicines and costs about **5,800 THB** a month privately, which the
Tribunal accepted he could not afford — but his treating specialist gave evidence that an
**alternative antiretroviral available under the UCS** would keep his viral load suppressed. So the
Tribunal did **not** accept he would be denied HIV treatment.

He was found a refugee anyway.

## The reasoning that makes this worth teaching

The Member found him a member of the particular social groups "**people who are HIV positive in
Thailand**" and "**homosexual men who are HIV positive in Thailand**", and then assessed harm
**cumulatively**:

- visible rashes recur and would make his status **identifiable**; there is also a real chance of
  **unauthorised disclosure of HIV status by healthcare providers**, documented in the *People Living
  with HIV Stigma Index 2.0*;
- country information shows persistent stigma — about one in four young people living with HIV
  report discrimination in healthcare, and homophobia deters gay men from accessing HIV services;
- he is in his **late fifties**, from a conservative family and a small rural community (Surin), with
  **no family support**, a documented history of **major depression and suicidal ideation**, and has
  previously **ceased his medication** in the context of that depression.

Taken together: "a real chance, one that may not be 50 per cent but is not an insubstantial or remote
chance, the applicant will not be able to subsist as he will be denied access to services" — serious
harm for the essential and significant reason of his membership of that group. On **s 5J(2)**, the
Tribunal found **no effective protection measures**: given his reluctance to disclose his sexuality
or status, complaining to Thai police or the National Human Rights Commission would be "extremely
difficult", and legal protections there are unevenly enforced.

## Use it for

- **hr.3.1 — if the school's contemporary issue is LGBTIQ+ rights or the right to health**, this is a
  current, Australian, fully reasoned decision. If the issue is modern slavery, it still works for
  hr.2 as an example of rights enforced through **merits review** rather than a bill of rights.
- **"Persecution" is wider than violence.** Nobody threatened to kill him. The harm found was
  **inability to subsist** through accumulated stigma, ostracism and denial of services. The single
  best illustration in the library that serious harm can be social and economic.
- **The cumulative-harm method (hr.2.7)** — each factor alone would have failed; the Tribunal added
  them. Set it out as a list and you have a model of how an international obligation is applied to
  one person's facts.
- **Good law can coexist with real persecution (hr.t.sovereignty / hr.t.effectiveness)** — Thailand
  has free treatment, a Gender Equality Act and a national AIDS strategy, and the Tribunal still
  found no effective protection. Outstanding for arguing that formal legal protection is not the same
  as protection in practice, in Australia as much as anywhere.
- **Procedural fairness (inv.1.6 / dis.3.1)** — the delegate decided on the papers **with no
  interview**; the Tribunal held **two hearings** with Thai interpreters, took oral evidence from the
  treating specialist, and twice directed the applicant to produce further medical and country
  material. A precise comparison between primary decision-making and merits review.
- **Delay (inv.1.4)** — applied **November 2019**, refused **June 2022**, decided **March 2026**:
  more than six years, and the matter is only **remitted**, not granted. Use it for responsiveness.

## Caution

**Anonymised by file number** under s 369 of the *Migration Act*, with details replaced by generic
descriptions — never try to identify the applicant, and note the square-bracketed placeholders
throughout the judgment. A **first-instance merits review** by a single General Member: it is **not
binding precedent** and turns entirely on this applicant's evidence. The outcome is a **remittal with
a direction**, so the visa itself still had to be granted by the Department. The Tribunal **rejected**
several of his claims (ineligibility for the UCS, denial of treatment, and inability to find work),
so do not use it as evidence that Thailand denies HIV care — the finding is about **stigma and
inability to subsist**, which is a narrower and more interesting point. Late claims about the
Thailand–Cambodia conflict were not considered. The material includes **suicidal ideation, HIV status
and sexuality**; handle it carefully in class and do not reduce the applicant to his diagnosis.
