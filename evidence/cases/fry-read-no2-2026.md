Property orders had already been made in **Australia**. Later orders were made in the **United
States** that **conflicted** with them. By the time the question reached this Court the applicant had
**died**, and the fight was over money held in a solicitor's trust account.

## The question

Whether the **foreign judgment is recognised at common law**. (The *Foreign Judgments Act 1991* (Cth)
scheme applies only to the courts of countries listed in the Regulations, which is why the issue fell
to be decided under the common law.)

The Court's answer came in two steps:

1. The foreign judgment was **prima facie entitled to enforcement in Australia** — the ordinary
   position where a foreign court of competent jurisdiction has given a final money judgment.
2. But recognition can be defeated by established **defences** — fraud, denial of natural justice,
   or that enforcement would be contrary to **public policy**. Here **enforcement was refused
   because it would contravene public policy**, the conflict with the earlier Australian orders
   being the heart of it.

The funds in the trust account were ordered paid out, less $1,343.59 to the solicitor, and the
application was dismissed.

## Use it for

- **fam.1.2 (state and federal jurisdiction) extended outward** — the dot point is usually taught as
  state against Commonwealth. This is the next layer: **Australian courts against foreign courts**,
  and what happens when two valid orders cannot both be obeyed.
- **inv.6.2 (state sovereignty)** — the clearest small example available. A US court's orders have
  no automatic force here; they are recognised as a matter of **Australian** law, on Australian
  terms, and Australia can refuse. Far more concrete than a general statement about sovereignty.
- **Public policy as a limit (inv.1.7)** — the rule-of-law point cuts both ways. Finality and
  comity say enforce the foreign judgment; the integrity of Australian orders says do not. The Court
  chose the second, which is worth arguing about.
- **fam.2.3 / fam.2.5 (property, enforcement)** — a reminder that **getting an order is not
  getting the money**. Here enforcement outlasted the applicant's life and consumed at least two
  judgments, with the dispute ending over a trust account balance.
- **Forum shopping** — the practical lesson: a party dissatisfied with Australian property orders
  cannot simply obtain better ones elsewhere and bring them home.

## Caution

**Pseudonyms**; Pt XIVB applies. This is ***(No 2)***, and the earlier Australian property orders and
the first judgment are **not in the library**, so the facts behind the conflict are only summarised
here. The decision applies **common law recognition**, not the *Foreign Judgments Act* registration
scheme — do not conflate them, and check whether the United States is a listed country under the
Regulations before writing about which applies. "Public policy" is a **narrow and exceptional**
defence; this case should not be cited for any general proposition that Australian courts will
decline foreign judgments they disagree with. The reasoning draws on commercial authority
(*Amadio*, *Israel Discount Bank*, *Doe v Howard*) that is beyond the syllabus — use the outcome and
the principle, not the detail.
