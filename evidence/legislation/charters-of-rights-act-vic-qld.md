Australia is the only Western democracy without a national bill or charter of rights. Three
jurisdictions have legislated their own, all on the **"dialogue" model** borrowed from the UK's
*Human Rights Act 1998* and New Zealand's *Bill of Rights Act 1990*: rights are declared by
ordinary statute, binding on government but not entrenched, and Parliament can override them.

| | ACT 2004 | Victoria 2006 | Queensland 2019 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rights protected** | ICCPR civil and political rights; right to education (2012) and a right to a healthy environment (2024) added | ICCPR rights (20 sections) | 23 rights: ICCPR plus **education, health services** and Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander cultural rights |
| **Interpretation** | Courts must interpret all laws compatibly with rights "so far as it is possible" | Same (s 32) | Same (s 48) |
| **Declaration of incompatibility** | Supreme Court may declare; Attorney-General must respond in the Assembly | Same (s 36) | Same (s 53) |
| **Public authorities** | Must act compatibly and give proper consideration to rights; a direct cause of action in the Supreme Court | Same (s 38); no free-standing damages | Same (s 58); complaints to the **Queensland Human Rights Commission** for conciliation |
| **Parliament** | Compatibility statements with every bill; a scrutiny committee | Same; an **override declaration** (s 31) lets Parliament pass an incompatible law for five years | Same; override (s 43) |
| **Limits** | No power to invalidate legislation in any of the three | | |

## What the experience shows

- Courts have made very few declarations of incompatibility (Victoria's first, *Momcilovic*, 2011,
  was partly overturned by the High Court on separation-of-powers grounds, which still allowed the
  interpretive rule). Most effect is **upstream**: government lawyers vetting bills and policy.
- Public-authority obligations have been used in prisons, housing (public housing evictions in
  Victoria must consider the right to home — *Director of Housing v Sudi*), mental health and
  COVID-era detention.
- Queensland's **conciliation** route produced hundreds of complaints a year; its Act was
  overridden in 2023–24 to allow youth detention in watch-houses and "adult crime, adult time"
  sentencing — the clearest demonstration of the model's weakness and, its defenders say, its
  democratic point.

## Use it for

- **hr.2.14** — arguments *for* (rights made visible, bureaucracy must consider them, cheap
  remedies through conciliation) and *against* (judicial activism fears, little practical change,
  Parliament overrides anyway) with Australian rather than overseas evidence.
- **hr.2.8 / hr.2.9** — a statutory charter as the alternative to constitutional entrenchment; what
  the Constitution lacks and a statute can add.
- **ind.3.3** — the difficulty of enforcing rights without a damages remedy.
- **lip.2.1 / queensland-adult-crime-adult-time** — the Queensland override as a live Law in
  Practice example.

## Caution

NSW has no charter; proposals (2001, 2009 national consultation, 2023 AHRC model) have not been
adopted — say so rather than implying momentum. The *Momcilovic* point is subtle: the High Court
upheld the interpretive provision but divided on declarations. Rights lists and override instances
should be checked against each Act's current text.
