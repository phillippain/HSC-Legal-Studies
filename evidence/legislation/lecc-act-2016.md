Before 2017 complaints about NSW police went two ways: corruption to the **Police Integrity
Commission** and everything else to the **NSW Ombudsman**. After the Tink review (2015) the
*LECC Act* merged both into the **Law Enforcement Conduct Commission**, which began on 1 July 2017.

## Powers and functions

| Function | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Jurisdiction** | NSW Police Force and the NSW Crime Commission; "serious misconduct" (corrupt conduct, serious maladministration, conduct that could be a serious offence or warrant dismissal) and "officer maladministration" |
| **Investigation** | Royal-commission-style powers: compel witnesses and documents, search warrants, covert surveillance (with warrants), **public or private hearings** (Chief Commissioner must be a former judge) |
| **Oversight** | Most complaints are referred to the police for **internal investigation**, which LECC monitors and may review or take over; it audits critical incidents (deaths and serious injuries involving police) |
| **Reports** | Findings and recommendations to Parliament and the Commissioner of Police; it may recommend disciplinary action or referral to the DPP but **cannot discipline, charge or make binding orders** |
| **Prevention** | Research and systemic reviews — the 2020 strip-search inquiry (ss 31–34 LEPRA) is the best-known |
| **Inspector of the LECC** | An independent inspector audits the Commission itself |

## Use it for

- **ind.2.7** — the NSW model of a "statutory body": independent, powerful in inquiry, weak in
  outcome; the standard comparison is with ICAC (which can make corrupt-conduct findings) and the
  Ombudsman (which cannot investigate police at all since 2017).
- **crime.2.1 / crime.2.2** — the body that polices police powers; the strip-search inquiry
  found widespread unlawful searches of young people, and the Kempsey and Nowland records show what
  follows.
- **ind.3.3 (enforcing rights)** — a complainant gets an investigation and a report, not a remedy;
  compensation requires a separate civil action.
- **ls.1.7 / lip.1.3** — the rule of law applied to the police themselves.

## Caution

LECC's own annual reports give complaint numbers and the proportion it investigates itself (small,
typically under 5%). The Commission's public-hearing power has been used sparingly; most work is
private. Its recommendations are not always accepted — check the police response before claiming
an outcome. The Makalu record is a current example of a public hearing.
