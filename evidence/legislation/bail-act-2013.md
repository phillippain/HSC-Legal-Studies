## How a decision is made

1. **Show cause** offence? The accused must show why detention is not justified (s 16A).
2. **Unacceptable risk** test (s 17): failing to appear, committing a serious offence, endangering safety, interfering with witnesses (factors in s 18).
3. Can **conditions** manage the risk? Including accommodation requirements, residential rehabilitation (s 28).

## Reform timeline

| Year | Reform | Trigger |
|---|---|---|
| 2013 | Bail Act 2013 replaces the 1978 Act (commenced 20 May 2014) | NSW Law Reform Commission Report 133 (2012): a complex Act amended 80+ times, remand numbers up threefold |
| 2014 | Bail Amendment Act 2014: show cause (in force 28 Jan 2015) | Bail granted to Steven Fesus (16 June) and Mahmoud Hawi (23 June) a month after the new Act began; Hatzistergos review |
| 2015 | Bail Amendment Act 2015 (No 44): terrorism links considered; exceptional circumstances for some terrorism-related accused | Lindt Café siege (Dec 2014); Martin Place siege review |
| Apr 2024 | Bail and Crimes Amendment Act: **s 22C** — 14–17-year-olds charged with serious break-ins or car theft while on bail for similar offences must be refused bail unless there is a 'high degree of confidence' | Regional youth crime |
| Jun 2024 | Bail and Other Legislation Amendment (Domestic Violence) Act: show cause for serious DV offences, 'red flag' behaviour, stays of release, electronic monitoring | Murder of Molly Ticehurst by Daniel Billings, on bail (April 2024) |
| Jun 2026 | Organised crime links considered at bail | Gang violence |
| Sep 2026 | s 22C extended to April 2028 | — |

s 22C review: **454** young people faced the test, **70%** refused bail; Aboriginal young people **84%** of refusals.

## Why the 1978 Act was replaced (2012-2014)

The Bail Act 1978 had been amended by **more than 80 Acts**; presumptions for, against or none applied by offence; and remand numbers had **more than trebled in 15 years**. The Law Reform Commission (Report 133, April 2012) recommended a simpler 'justification' model with a presumption in favour of release; Parliament chose a single **unacceptable risk** test with no offence-based presumptions and not the presumption. See [NSWLRC Report 133](nswlrc-report-133-bail-2012).

## Hawi, Fesus and the 2014 amendments

- **R v Fesus** [2014] NSWSC 770 (Adams J, 16 June 2014): refused bail under the 1978 Act, Steven Fesus (charged with murdering his wife, Jodie) was released after new forensic evidence allowed a fresh application (s 74). See [R v Fesus](r-v-fesus-2014).
- **R v Hawi** (Harrison J, 23 June 2014): bail pending a retrial for murder, with a $700,000 surety and daily reporting. See [R v Hawi](r-v-hawi-2014).
- The Premier ordered a review (John Hatzistergos) about a month after the Act began. The **Bail Amendment Act 2014** (second reading 13 August 2014; in force 28 January 2015) introduced **show cause** (s 16A, offences in s 16B), made the risk test a single stage, added criminal associations and victims' views to s 18 and moved the presumption of innocence into a preamble. Brown and Quilter (2014) called the changes "premature, unnecessary ... in short, they are a mess".

## The Lindt siege and the 2015 amendments

Monis was on bail when he took hostages on 15 December 2014. The Coroner (24 May 2017) found police and prosecution errors in how his bail was handled; Nicholas Cowdery argued the case was too aberrant to justify tougher law. The Bail Amendment Act 2015 (No 44) nevertheless required bail authorities to consider terrorism links. See [the inquest findings](lindt-siege-inquest-findings-2017).

## s 22C extended (September 2026)

The statutory review (tabled 15 September 2026) found s 22C helped stabilise youth crime in regional NSW and recommended extending it. The Justice Legislation Amendment (Miscellaneous) Bill (No 2) 2026, introduced 15 September and passed **24 September 2026**, extended s 22C to **4 April 2028** (it was due to lapse on 1 October 2026). The government paired it with an $8.4m Tamworth diversion pilot for 8–13-year-olds (AAP, 15 Sept 2026). Critics, including the ALS, point to remand rising and Aboriginal young people making up most refusals.
