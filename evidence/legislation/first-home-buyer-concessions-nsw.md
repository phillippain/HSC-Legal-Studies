Governments respond to affordability on two sides. Supply-side law (planning, the HAFF) tries to
add homes; **demand-side law** gives buyers money or tax relief. These four schemes are the demand
side for first-home buyers, and they are the measures politicians announce most often.

| Scheme | Law | What it gives (2025–26) |
|---|---|---|
| **First Home Buyers Assistance Scheme** | *Duties Act 1997* (NSW) Ch 2 Pt 8 | Full exemption from transfer (stamp) duty on homes up to **$800,000**, concession to **$1 million** (new or existing); vacant land to $350,000/$450,000. Must live in it for 12 months. From 1 July 2023, replacing the short-lived land-tax option |
| **First Home Owner Grant** | *First Home Owner Grant (New Homes) Act 2000* (NSW) | **$10,000** for a **new** home up to $600,000, or a build up to $750,000 — new homes only since 2012, to steer demand to construction |
| **Home Guarantee Scheme** | *Housing Australia Act 2018* (Cth), program rules | Housing Australia guarantees up to 15% of the loan so a buyer needs a **5% deposit** without lenders' mortgage insurance; from **1 October 2025** open to all first-home buyers with no income cap or place limit, price cap $1.5m in Sydney |
| **Help to Buy** | *Help to Buy Act 2024* (Cth) | Commonwealth **shared equity** of up to 30% (existing) or 40% (new) of the price for 10,000 buyers a year, income limits $100,000 single/$160,000 couple; passed November 2024 after a Senate deal with the Greens, launched late 2025 |

## Use it for

- **shel.3.1 (affordability)** — the demand-side legal responses, with the standard critique: the
  Grattan Institute, the Productivity Commission and the RBA have each found that grants and
  guarantees are largely **capitalised into prices**, helping the buyer who gets in first and raising
  the bar for the next.
- **shel.2.1** — the concessions are claimed at settlement through the conveyancer; a buyer's
  eligibility affects the price they can pay.
- **inv.5.1 / inv.5.2** — NSW controls duty and the grant; the Commonwealth, with no property power, uses its
  spending power and a statutory corporation.
- **exp.5 / chg.1** — the 2022–23 NSW election fought over duty v land tax, and the 2024 Senate
  deal on Help to Buy: competing interests and the conditions for reform.

## Caution

**Thresholds change almost yearly** — check Revenue NSW and Housing Australia before quoting any
figure; the table is as at late 2025. These are schemes for buyers, so they do nothing for renters
or social-housing applicants — ACOSS's criticism of the 2026 Budget applies here too. The "prices
rise by the subsidy" finding is contested in degree, not direction.
