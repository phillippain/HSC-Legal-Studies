## The two provisions worth naming

**Section 36 — the guiding principle.** The Tribunal is to facilitate the **"just, quick and cheap
resolution of the real issues in the proceedings"**, and to use its procedure so that costs are
proportionate to the importance and complexity of the subject matter. It is both the design intent
behind the tribunal model and the yardstick for judging it.

**Part 3A — the constitutional repair.** Proceedings NCAT cannot hear for want of federal
jurisdiction may be **transferred to, or recommenced in, the Local Court** (up to $100,000) **or the
District Court** without a further fee, and those courts may make the orders NCAT could have made.

NCAT's shelter jurisdiction is not in this Act: it is conferred by the enabling statutes —
**ss 187–188** of the *Residential Tenancies Act 2010* (NSW) and **s 232** of the *Strata Schemes
Management Act 2015* (NSW).

## Use it for

- **Jurisdiction as conferred, not inherent.** A tribunal has exactly the power its enabling Acts
  give it, which is why the RTA and SSMA sections matter as much as the NCAT Act itself.
- **Part 3A is a legislative patch on a constitutional hole**, and its limits are the point: the
  Local and District Courts retain discretions on costs, evidence and representation that NCAT does
  not exercise, so the same dispute becomes riskier and more formal purely because of where the
  parties live.
- **s 36 against the annual report**: a statute that promises "quick and cheap" and a Division
  clearing 96.9% of 52,608 applications is one of the few places in the course where a legislative
  objective can be measured against published performance.
