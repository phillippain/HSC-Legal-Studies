An Act of the British Parliament that took effect on 1 January 1901, approved by referendums in the
colonies, and amendable only by referendum under **s 128** (8 of 45 proposals have passed; the
last in 1977; the 2023 Voice proposal failed 60–40). It does three jobs for Legal Studies.

## 1. Division of powers (ls.2.6, fam.1.2)

- **s 51** lists the Commonwealth's **concurrent** powers — 39 heads including trade (i), taxation
  (ii), defence (vi), **external affairs (xxix)**, **marriage (xxi)** and **divorce and matrimonial
  causes (xxii)**, corporations (xx), and referrals from the states (xxxvii). Everything not listed
  is **residual** to the states: criminal law, health, education, housing, child protection, police.
- **s 52 and s 90** — a few **exclusive** Commonwealth powers (the seat of government, customs).
- **s 109** — where a valid Commonwealth law and a state law are inconsistent, the Commonwealth law
  prevails.
- **External affairs** is the lever by which international law becomes domestic law:
  *Commonwealth v Tasmania* (1983) upheld the World Heritage Act; the Racial Discrimination Act,
  Sex Discrimination Act and Human Rights Commission Act all rest on it (hr.2.7, ls.2.14).

## 2. Separation of powers (ls.2.7)

Chapter I vests legislative power in **Parliament**, Chapter II executive power in the **Crown**
(exercised by the Governor-General on ministers' advice), Chapter III judicial power in the **High
Court and federal courts**. The separation is strict for the judiciary: *Australian Communist Party
v Commonwealth* (1951) — Parliament cannot declare its own law valid; *Kable* (1996) — states
cannot give their courts functions incompatible with federal judicial power.

## 3. The High Court (ls.2.8, ls.5.2)

**s 71** creates it; **s 75–76** give original jurisdiction (including s 75(v), the entrenched right
to seek writs against Commonwealth officers); **s 73** makes it the final court of appeal. It
interprets the Constitution (*Mabo* on common law; *Commonwealth v ACT* on marriage) and its
reading of s 51 has steadily widened Commonwealth power.

## 4. Rights — the five express and one implied (hr.2.8, hr.2.14)

| Section | Right | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| **s 41** | Vote (for those who had a state vote in 1901) | Read as spent |
| **s 51(xxxi)** | Acquisition of property on just terms | Commonwealth only |
| **s 80** | Trial by jury for indictable Commonwealth offences | Parliament decides what is "indictable" |
| **s 116** | Commonwealth may not establish a religion or restrict its free exercise | Does not bind the states |
| **s 117** | No discrimination on the basis of state residence | |
| **Implied** | Freedom of **political communication** (*Lange* 1997, *McCloy* 2015) | A limit on government, not a personal right |

There is no bill of rights; no right to equality, free speech generally, privacy, or due process.
That absence is the premise of the Charter debate (hr.2.14), and why Australia relies on statute
(hr.2.9), the common law (hr.2.10) and international reporting.

## Caution

The Constitution contains no reference to Aboriginal people since 1967 and no preamble recognising
them; "constitutional recognition" remains a live proposal. Case names are the ones usually taught;
the full citations are on the site's case cards. The text is at legislation.gov.au and on the
Parliament's website; s 51 is long — quote the placitum number.
