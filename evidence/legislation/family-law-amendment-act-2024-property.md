Described by practitioners as the biggest change to family law property settlements since **binding
financial agreements (2000)** and **superannuation splitting (2002)**. Assented **10 December 2024**;
the property provisions commenced **10 June 2025**.

## What changed

**The four-step process is codified.** The court must identify all **property and liabilities**,
assess each party's **contributions**, assess their **current and future circumstances** (replacing
the old list known as the "future needs factors"), and make only orders that are **just and
equitable**.

**Family violence becomes an express factor.** The **economic effect of family violence** must be
considered where relevant — both in evaluating **contributions** and in assessing **current and
future circumstances** — and it is an express factor in **spousal maintenance** as well. The
definition and examples in **s 4AB** are expanded to include **coercive and controlling behaviour in
relation to finances**, including **dowry abuse** and other financial abuse. As the
Attorney-General's Department puts it, one party controlling all the finances or the spending may
count as family violence.

**The housing needs of children.** Courts must consider the **care and housing needs of any
children** when assessing the parties' current and future circumstances.

**Companion animals.** Pets can be the subject of a property order, and the court must consider a
specified list of matters — including **animal abuse** (threatening an animal as a form of family
violence) and **each party's and the children's attachment** to the animal. Crucially, the court
**cannot order joint ownership or shared possession**: the media's "pet custody" is a misnomer,
because somebody gets the dog.

**Wastage.** Practitioner commentary records that **evidence of waste** by a party is added to the
current and future circumstances factors — and that what matters is the **effect** of material
wastage, not the waste itself.

**Disclosure.** The duty of **financial disclosure** moves out of the Court Rules and **into the
Act**, though its nature is unchanged.

**Application.** The changes apply to **all new and existing proceedings**, including applications
filed before 10 June 2025, **unless a final hearing had already commenced**. Existing orders are
unaffected.

## What was left out

The government **declined to create a statutory tort of family violence**, which the **ALRC** had
recommended in its final report — a cause of action under the *Family Law Act* allowing a perpetrator
to be liable for **general, special, aggravated and punitive damages**, with any award **excluded from
the net asset pool**. Compensation claims can still be brought in **state and territory courts**, and
practitioners note that for some victims a separate compensation claim may be a **better route** than
having the violence weighed inside a property settlement.

Practitioners also flag a likely **unintended consequence**: the redesigned provisions appear to
remove the ability to give credit for **caring for stepchildren**.

## Use it for

- **fam.2.3 — this is the current law, and most published explanations are not.** Any property
  answer written from a pre-2025 textbook is wrong about the process. Name the Act, the commencement
  date, and the four steps.
- **The most teachable change is family violence in a property settlement (fam.2.4).** Family
  violence used to be relevant to parenting, and to property only through the strained *Kennon*
  reasoning about contributions made more arduous. It is now **express** — including **financial
  abuse, coercive control of money and dowry abuse**. Pair it with the Family Law Council's systems
  abuse report and with `bocsar-coercive-control-june-2026`.
- **The pet provisions are a gift for teaching, and they carry a real point.** Students remember
  them, and the detail that the court **cannot order shared possession** is the kind of precision
  that earns marks — property law divides, it does not share. The **animal abuse** factor also shows
  the Act recognising threats to a pet as family violence.
- **Housing needs of children (shel.1.3 crossover)** — a family law provision responding to a
  **housing market**. Read it with `abc-2026-separated-couples-living-together`, where couples cannot
  afford to separate at all.
- **Law reform, fully traceable (chg.1.3)** — ALRC 135 (2019) → *Family Law Amendment Act 2023*
  (parenting) → *Family Law Amendment Act 2024* (property, commencing 2025). Three steps, six years,
  one inquiry. And the **refused** recommendation — the statutory tort — is the better half of the
  argument, because it shows Parliament choosing.
- **Retrospectivity (inv.1.2)** — the changes reach **existing proceedings** unless the final hearing
  had started. A live, current example for "characteristics of effective law" and for the usual
  presumption against retrospective operation.
- **Interpretation takes time** — practitioners expect **12 to 18 months** before primary judges and
  the Full Court settle the meaning, with the **housing needs of children** factor the most likely to
  be litigated. Useful for arguing that a reform's effect is not known on the day it commences.

## Caution

The library document is a **law firm article on Mondaq** (Paul Lewis, Carroll & O'Dea, **3 June
2025**) written **before commencement** — so its account is predictive in places, and the
page includes a mass of unrelated site furniture. The core changes in this record were **verified
against the Attorney-General's Department fact sheet**; two items were **not** and are attributed to
the practitioner commentary: the **wastage** factor and the **stepchildren** consequence. Check the
consolidated *Family Law Act 1975* before quoting any section.

"Pet custody orders" is the media's phrase and the article says why it is **ambiguous** — do not use
it as though it were a legal term. The **statutory tort** is a recommendation that was **not
enacted**: do not write that damages for family violence are available under the *Family Law Act*.
The predicted **12 to 18 months** of uncertainty and the view that the housing needs factor will be
controversial are **opinions**, not facts. As at late 2026 the provisions were about sixteen months
old, so look for the first appellate decisions interpreting them — `duffy-duffy-2026` shows the
amended s 79 process being applied, and is the best companion record.
