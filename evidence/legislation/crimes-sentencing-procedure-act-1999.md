- **s 3A** — the purposes of sentencing, set out in statute: punishment, deterrence, protection of
  the community, rehabilitation, accountability, denunciation and recognition of harm to the victim
  and the community.
- **s 4A** — a **domestic violence offender must receive full-time detention or a supervised order**
  (ICO, CCO or CRO with a supervision condition) unless the court decides another option is more
  appropriate **and gives reasons**.
- **s 22** — the **guilty plea discount**, which explains many sentencing outcomes that look lenient
  from outside.

## Use it for

- **Statutory guidelines limiting judicial discretion** — s 3A lists competing purposes without
  ranking them, so the discretion survives the statute.
- The jurisdictional gap: ss 4A and 14(4) of the *Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007*
  are built around **NSW** offences. A course of conduct answered by a **Commonwealth** offence is
  sentenced outside the domestic violence framework the state built for it.

## Guilty plea discounts since 2018 (s 25D) and victim consultation (s 35A)

- **s 25D** (Early Appropriate Guilty Plea reforms, 2018): **25%** for a plea in the Local Court before committal; **10%** after committal at least 14 days before trial; **5%** otherwise. Before 2018 the discount was up to 25% at the court's discretion (*R v Thomson and Houlton*, 2000; see *Shine v R*).
- **s 35A** (2010, after *R v Koch*): the court must not take account of negotiated charges or facts unless the prosecutor certifies the victim was consulted.

## Standard non-parole periods and guideline judgments

- **SNPPs** (Part 4 Div 1A, 2003): the non-parole period for an offence in the middle of the range of seriousness — murder 20 years; murder of a police officer or child 25 years. A guidepost, not a starting point (*Muldrock v The Queen*, 2011).
- **Guideline judgments** (Part 3 Div 4): the Court of Criminal Appeal, often on the Attorney-General's application, gives sentencing guidance — *Jurisic* (1998) and *Whyte* (2002) on dangerous driving, *Henry* (1999) armed robbery, *Ponfield* (1999) break and enter, *Thomson and Houlton* (2000) guilty pleas.

## Factors: s 21A and historical offences (s 21B)

- **s 21A(2)** aggravating factors (e.g. violence or a weapon, in company, in the victim's home, vulnerable victim, substantial harm, planned, on bail or parole) and **s 21A(3)** mitigating factors (e.g. no significant record, remorse, plea, assistance, good prospects of rehabilitation). Good character (s 21A(3)(f)) was removed by the *Good Character at Sentencing Act 2026*.
- **s 21B** (from 18 October 2022): historical offences are sentenced by sentencing patterns at the time of sentence, but the maximum penalty is the one in force when the offence was committed (the Moore Park cold case, 2026).
- **s 23** assistance to authorities; **ss 31–35** Form 1 offences taken into account.

## Penalties since 24 September 2018 (ss 5, 7–10, 17)

- **s 7** intensive correction order (imprisonment of up to 2 years served in the community; community safety paramount, s 66); **s 8** community correction order (up to 3 years); **s 9** conditional release order (up to 2 years); **s 10** dismissal or release without conviction. These replaced suspended sentences, home detention, community service orders and good behaviour bonds.
- **s 5:** prison only if no other penalty is appropriate; reasons are required for a sentence of 6 months or less. **s 17:** one penalty unit is $110.

## Victim impact statements (ss 26–30A)

- A primary victim (offences involving death, actual or threatened violence, or sexual offences) may describe the harm suffered; family members may give a statement where the victim died, and since 2014 the court may take a family member's statement into account in a homicide case. Statements are usually unsworn and not tested (Greta, 2025: 33 statements read; Billings, 2026).
