**Build to rent (BTR)** means large residential developments designed, built and **retained under
single institutional ownership** and rented long-term, rather than sold off lot by lot. Governments
promote it to lift rental supply, draw institutional capital and improve **tenure security** — and
the striking thing, for a Shelter answer, is that the legal instrument chosen was **tax law**.

## The Commonwealth: Treasury Laws Amendment (Build to Rent) Act 2024

Passed **November 2024**, with a companion *Capital Works (Build to Rent Misuse Tax) Act 2024*. For
an eligible "active BTR development":

- the **capital works (building) deduction rate rises from 2.5% to 4%** a year, shortening the
  write-off from 40 years to 25; and
- the **Managed Investment Trust withholding tax rate falls from 30% to 15%** on qualifying rental
  income and capital gains for foreign investors.

To qualify, a development must:

- contain **50 or more dwellings** available for rent to the general public;
- be held by a **single entity** for a **15-year compliance period**, starting when all dwellings are
  first available for rent (the entity may change);
- offer **leases of a minimum term**; and
- make **at least 10% of dwellings affordable**, managed with a **community housing organisation**.

Construction must have begun after **7:30pm on 9 May 2023**. The companion Act imposes a **misuse
tax**, which is the enforcement mechanism: claim the concession and then breach the conditions, and
the benefit is clawed back.

## NSW: land tax and planning

NSW combines **fiscal and planning** levers:

- a **50% reduction in land tax** for eligible BTR developments and **exemption from foreign investor
  land tax surcharges**, with recent budget measures making the concession **permanent**;
- eligibility mirroring the federal settings — **50+ dwellings**, **15 years** single ownership,
  **10% affordable**, planning compliance;
- recognition of BTR as a **distinct asset class** in planning policy, with accelerated approvals
  near transport hubs, inside the state's target of about **377,000 new homes by 2029**; and
- direct delivery through the state developer **Landcom**, including essential worker housing (see
  `smh-2025-landcom-parramatta-road-btr`).

## Use it for

- **shel.3.4 / shel.3.1 — a legal response built out of the tax Act.** When a question asks how the
  law addresses the supply of affordable rental housing, this is the most current answer, and the
  mechanism is unusual enough to be worth naming: **depreciation rates and withholding tax**, not a
  housing statute.
- **The conditions are the law doing the work.** Ten per cent affordable, fifteen years of single
  ownership, fifty dwellings, a minimum lease term. The state is **buying tenure security and
  affordable supply with forgone revenue**, and the misuse tax is what makes the bargain
  enforceable. Excellent for enforceability, because the sanction is specific and automatic.
- **Compare the two governments (inv.6.4 / shel.t.cooperation)** — the Commonwealth has **only** tax
  levers over housing, so it used them; NSW has tax **and** planning **and** a state developer, so it
  used all three. A clean, concrete illustration of what each level of government can actually do
  about housing.
- **Tenure security as a policy goal (shel.2.2)** — the whole point of single ownership for fifteen
  years is that the landlord cannot sell the apartment out from under the tenant. Read it beside
  `afr-2025-investors-quitting-apartments`, where small investors selling is exactly what destabilises
  renters, and the contrast makes the argument for institutional landlords.
- **The honest limit** — BTR is a very small share of total housing stock and completions are modest
  against demand; construction costs and labour shortages slow delivery. Tax incentives improve
  **feasibility**, not affordability directly, and the 10% affordable requirement means **90% is let
  at market rent**.
- **Who benefits (shel.t.values)** — concessions worth real money to superannuation funds and foreign
  institutional investors, in exchange for a minority of affordable dwellings. Set it against the NRAS
  record, where a similar bargain expired, and against the HAFF, which funds dwellings directly.

## Caution

The library document is an **unattributed explainer** with a **generic reference list** ("BTR News.
(2025). Policy shifts strengthen Australian build-to-rent investment"; "Grattan Institute. (2025).
Housing supply and rental market analysis reports") and reads as AI-drafted — the same pattern as the
rental subsidies and surrogacy explainers. The **Commonwealth figures in this record were verified
against practitioner commentary on the Acts as passed**, not taken from the document; the **NSW
figures were not** and should be checked against **Revenue NSW** and the current Budget Papers before
use, including whether the land tax concession is in fact permanent.

One detail is genuinely unsettled: the **minimum lease term**. The library document says **five
years**; practitioner commentary on the Act as passed reports **three**. **Check the Act** before
stating a number. Eligibility conditions, rates and thresholds in tax law change, so verify with the
**ATO** rather than quoting this record. The document's forward-looking and "media reporting"
sections describe **commentary, not law**. BTR concessions are **not a right to housing** and create
**no enforceable entitlement for any tenant** — a renter cannot sue to obtain a BTR dwelling. There
is as yet **no published evaluation** of whether the concessions have produced dwellings, so any claim
about effectiveness needs current completion data.
