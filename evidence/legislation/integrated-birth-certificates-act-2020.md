Before this Act, an adopted person in NSW received only a **post-adoptive birth certificate** — a
document recording their **adoptive parents in place of their parents at birth**. The original record
existed, but the certificate they carried through life did not acknowledge it.

The 2020 Act amended both the ***Adoption Act 2000*** and the ***Births, Deaths and Marriages
Registration Act 1995*** to create the **Integrated Birth Certificate (IBC)**.

## What an IBC is

- A new form of birth certificate recording an adopted person's **parents and older siblings at
  birth**, and their **parents and siblings after adoption** — both families in **one legal
  document**.
- It is an **official identity document**. From **16 November 2020** all adopted people in NSW are
  **automatically issued with both** a post-adoptive certificate and an IBC, and because **both can
  be used for legal identification**, the adopted person **chooses which to use**.
- It is available **retrospectively**: an adopted person, or an entitled family member, can apply for
  an IBC for adoptions registered in NSW **before** that date.
- It **does not change the status of the adoption**. Both certificates identify the **adoptive parents
  as the legal parents** and the adopted name as the legal name.
- The NSW Government acknowledges the impact of **past forced adoption and removal practices**, and
  that for some historical adoptions information about the birth family may not exist — where it is
  incomplete, Births Deaths & Marriages records **a dash or "unknown"**.

The stated purposes: it "enhances modern **open adoption** practice in NSW", "supports a child's
**right to know their origins**", "may assist openness in the adoptive family" and "encourages
ongoing relationships with birth families".

## Use it for

- **fam.1.6 — the best law reform record for adoption, and a short one.** A named Act, a
  commencement date, a concrete change to a document people carry, and an express rationale. Most
  adoption answers describe the *Adoption Act 2000* and stop; this shows the law still moving.
- **"The right to know their origins" is the phrase to quote.** It links straight to **CROC arts 7
  and 8** — a child's right to know their parents and to preserve their identity — and it is a rare,
  clean example of an **international obligation reaching a domestic statute** and changing a form.
  Use it for hr.2.7 as well as fam.1.6.
- **A document as a legal mechanism (inv.1.5)** — the reform does not alter anyone's legal parentage,
  custody or inheritance. It changes **what the certificate says**, and gives the adopted person the
  **choice** of which to present. Excellent for arguing that recognition and identity are legal
  interests in their own right, separate from rights over property or care.
- **Redress for past wrongs (fam.t.values)** — the Act is framed partly as a response to **forced
  adoption practices**, and the honest detail is the **dash or "unknown"** where records were never
  kept or were falsified. The law can offer the certificate; it cannot supply the information. That
  limit is the most powerful thing in the fact sheet.
- **Open adoption in practice (fam.3.2)** — read it with `lsj-2023-adoption-legacy-of-complexity`,
  where open adoption is the governing principle across all Australian jurisdictions, and with
  `australian-2025-adoption-aboriginality`. The IBC is open adoption written onto the identity
  document.
- **Retrospective and automatic (inv.1.2)** — issued automatically for new adoptions **and**
  available for adoptions registered decades earlier. Compare it with reforms that apply only
  prospectively, and note that an automatic entitlement needs no application, which removes an
  access barrier.
- **Pair it with a lived account** — `smh-2025-tsehay-hawkins-intercountry-adoption` is an adopted
  person searching for her birth parents at 18. The IBC is what the law can give her; the search is
  what it cannot.

## Caution

The library document is a **government fact sheet**, so it is reliable on the scheme but it is
**promotional in tone** and states the purposes the government intended, not an evaluation — there is
**no evidence here about take-up or outcomes**, and no critic. Published around the 2020
commencement, so **verify the current position** with NSW Births, Deaths & Marriages and the
consolidated *Adoption Act 2000*: eligibility to apply, fees and process may have changed.

This is **NSW only**. Integrated or integrated-style birth certificates exist in some other
jurisdictions and not others, so do not describe it as Australian law. It applies to **adoptions
registered in NSW**, which means many **intercountry** adoptees are in a different position — a
point worth making rather than eliding. The Act **does not** change legal parentage, name or the
adoption's status, so do not overstate it: an IBC is an identity document, not a restoration of the
birth family's legal relationship. And the **dash or "unknown"** entry is a real limitation for
exactly the historical adoptees the reform is partly for.
