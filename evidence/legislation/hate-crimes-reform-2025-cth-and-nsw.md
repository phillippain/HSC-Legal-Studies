Two legislatures, three weeks, after a wave of attacks on the Jewish community in late 2024 and early
2025. The library document captures the moment **before** the laws passed; this record records what
was enacted.

## The debate the article captures (4 February 2025)

Parliament had just voted **unanimously to condemn antisemitism**. Labor and the Coalition were
considering amendments to a hate speech bill they wanted passed "within days". Independent MP
**Allegra Spender** was negotiating an amendment to **criminalise a public act, done with intent, to
promote hatred** towards individuals or groups on the basis of **religion, race, disability, gender,
sexual orientation or other attributes** — going beyond the government's bill, which the **Executive
Council of Australian Jewry** said was too weak because it did not address the **promotion of
hatred**. **Equality Australia's Anna Brown**: the bill is "a welcome step, but [the laws] will only
prevent hate if they **stop it at its source**". Spender acknowledged the need to negotiate the
wording **because of free speech concerns** — the same ground as the decade-long Coalition campaign
to repeal **s 18C of the *Racial Discrimination Act 1975***.

## What passed

**Commonwealth — Criminal Code Amendment (Hate Crimes) Act 2025, passed 6 February 2025**, amending
the *Criminal Code Act 1995*:

- new offences of **threatening force or violence** against protected groups, their members and close
  associates;
- new offences of **advocating or threatening damage to or destruction of places of worship** or
  property owned or occupied by protected groups;
- the maximum for **Nazi symbol, Nazi salute and prohibited terrorist symbol** offences raised from
  **one year to five years**; and
- **mandatory minimum sentences** — **12 months** for those symbol offences, and **one to six years**
  for certain terrorism offences.

**NSW — package passed by 21 February 2025**, amending the *Crimes Act 1900* and the *Graffiti
Control Act 2008*:

- a new offence of **inciting racial hatred** — maximum **two years**, fines to **$11,000**
  (corporations **$55,000**), and it is **automatically repealed after three years**;
- **displaying Nazi symbols on or near synagogues, Jewish schools or the Sydney Jewish Museum** —
  maximum two years; and
- **blocking, harassing, intimidating or threatening** people accessing places of worship — maximum
  two years.

## What the Human Rights Commission said

- **Freedom of expression**: criminalising **advocacy or threats of physical violence** is a
  legitimate restriction, but proposals to criminalise **promoting hatred** need careful scrutiny,
  because the line between harmful speech and legitimate expression is hard to draw.
- **Peaceful assembly**: the NSW laws use **less precise language**, impose **greater penalties** than
  comparable laws, and **expand police powers** — and should have been **referred to a parliamentary
  committee**.
- **Mandatory minimums** undermine **judicial independence** and the court's ability to match
  punishment to the crime, and have an **unfair impact on disadvantaged groups**.

## Use it for

- **hr.2.9 (the role of statute law) — the best current example in the library.** Australia has no
  bill of rights, so rights are protected and limited by **statute**, and here two parliaments
  legislated within three weeks. Name the Acts, the dates, the offences and the penalties and the
  answer is specific rather than general.
- **Competing rights, with a real line (hr.1.1 / hr.2.14)** — freedom of expression against freedom
  from vilification and violence. The AHRC's distinction is the one to use: **threats of violence**
  are a legitimate restriction; **promoting hatred** is contested. Then the Spender amendment, the
  ECAJ's "too weak", and the s 18C history give you three positions instead of two.
- **Mandatory minimum sentences are the sharpest criticism (crime.4.3 / crime.4.5)** — a human rights
  commission arguing that a law intended to protect a minority will **fall hardest on disadvantaged
  groups** and **remove judicial discretion**. An unusually strong argument that a protective law can
  do harm, and it crosses into the Crime core.
- **A sunset clause (chg.2.1 / inv.1.2)** — the NSW incitement offence is **automatically repealed
  after three years**. Rare, and genuinely interesting: Parliament legislating its own doubt. Ask
  what should happen at the three-year mark and who will evaluate it.
- **Process as a rights issue (dis.3.2)** — passed "within days", **not referred to a committee**,
  and the AHRC says so. Use it for law-making process and scrutiny, not just content.
- **Federal and state together (inv.6.4)** — Commonwealth symbol and threat offences, state
  incitement and places-of-worship offences. Neither covers the field; both were needed.
- **Pair it with enforcement** — `smh-2025-swastika-flag-charges` is a charge laid in the same month
  under the pre-existing NSW offence, and `wertheim-v-haddad-2025` is the **civil** route under
  Commonwealth racial vilification law. Three mechanisms, one issue.

## Caution

The library document is a **news article from 4 February 2025**, written **before the laws passed** —
so the Spender amendment it describes is a **proposal**, and whether it was adopted in that form is
**not** established by the article. **The enacted detail in this record comes from the Australian
Human Rights Commission's explainer**, not from the article; check the consolidated *Criminal Code
Act 1995*, *Crimes Act 1900* (NSW) and *Graffiti Control Act 2008* for section numbers and current
penalties, which this record does not give for the Commonwealth offences.

The **NSW Act's name** is not given in the sources used, and the NSW incitement offence has a
**three-year sunset** that, from a February 2025 commencement, falls due around **early 2028** —
check whether it has been extended, replaced or allowed to lapse. The AHRC's criticisms are a
**statutory body's position**, not findings, and the Commission supports hate crime laws in principle
while opposing mandatory minimums. The quoted advocates — Equality Australia, the ECAJ, the
Australasian Union of Jewish Students — are **parties to the debate**. Finally, these laws were passed
in response to **specific antisemitic attacks**; the offences are drafted by **protected attribute**
and apply generally, so do not describe them as laws about one community.
