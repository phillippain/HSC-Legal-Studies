After *R v Singh* (2012) and a parliamentary review, NSW replaced provocation with **extreme
provocation**. The partial defence now requires that:

- the conduct of the deceased was a **serious indictable offence**;
- it caused the accused to **lose self-control**; and
- an **ordinary person** could have lost self-control to the extent of intending to kill or inflict
  grievous bodily harm.

Crucially, the reform was drafted so the defence **remains available to long-term victims of domestic
violence** who kill their abuser — the situation in which provocation has always had the strongest
moral claim.

## Use it for

- **A reform that narrows and preserves at the same time.** The defence was being used by men who
  killed departing partners, and was needed by women who killed violent ones. The amendment separates
  the two by requiring a criminal act by the deceased.
- **Responsiveness** — evidence that the criminal law can be changed quickly when public opinion is
  mobilised, and the counter-argument that responsiveness driven by one outrageous case is not the
  same as principled reform.
- Sits well against the **s 54D coercive control offence** and the *Crimes (Domestic and Personal
  Violence) Act 2007*: three NSW responses to the same underlying pattern of partner violence, aimed
  at three different points in it.
