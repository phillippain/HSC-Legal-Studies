A law reform commission is a permanent, expert, independent body whose only job is to recommend
changes to the law. NSW created the first in Australia in 1966 (the Act followed in 1967); the
Commonwealth followed in 1975 (re-enacted 1996). Both are the syllabus's first-named "agency of
reform".

## How they work

| | NSW Law Reform Commission | Australian Law Reform Commission |
|---|---|---|
| **Established** | *Law Reform Commission Act 1967* (NSW) | *Australian Law Reform Commission Act 1996* (Cth) |
| **Members** | A Chairperson (usually a judge or senior lawyer), full- and part-time commissioners, with the Chief Justice and others ex officio | A President (a judge) and commissioners, often with judges seconded |
| **References** | **Only on a reference from the Attorney-General** (s 10) — the Commission cannot choose its own topics | Same (s 20); the Attorney-General sets terms of reference and a reporting date |
| **Method** | Consultation papers, submissions, public hearings, empirical research; a final report tabled in Parliament | Same; discussion papers, then a final report; large-scale consultation (the 2019 family law review received over 1,000 submissions) |
| **Outcome** | Recommendations only; the Government responds (or does not); drafting of bills is Parliamentary Counsel's | Same |

## NSW LRC work on this site

- **Report 139, Sentencing (2013)** → the *Crimes (Sentencing Procedure) Amendment Act 2017*
  (ICOs and CCOs replacing suspended sentences).
- **Provocation** (the 2013 Legislative Council inquiry, not the LRC) → the 2014 amendment;
  **partial defences** reviewed by the LRC in 2023–25 (report on the *Crimes Act* defences).
- **Consent** (Report 148, 2020) → the *Crimes Legislation Amendment (Sexual Consent Reforms) Act
  2021*: affirmative consent.
- **Anti-Discrimination Act** review (reference 2023, report due) — the first full review of the
  1977 Act.
- **Open justice** (Report 149, 2022) and **coercive control** (the 2021 parliamentary
  inquiry preceded the LRC's role in drafting).

## ALRC work students cite

- *Family Law for the Future* (Report 135, 2019) — 60 recommendations; the 2021 court merger and
  the *Family Law Amendment Act 2023* (removing the presumption of equal shared parental
  responsibility) drew on it.
- *Pathways to Justice* (Report 133, 2017) — Indigenous incarceration; justice reinvestment.
- *Elder Abuse* (Report 131, 2017); *Religious Educational Institutions and Anti-Discrimination Laws*
  (Report 142, 2023); *Justice Responses to Sexual Violence* (Report 143, 2025).

## Use it for

- **ls.4.2 (agencies of reform)** — what distinguishes a commission from a parliamentary
  committee (standing, expert, independent, but reference-bound) and from a royal commission
  (permanent, no coercive powers).
- **ls.4.1 / ls.4.3** — the standard reform chain: social change → reference → report → bill;
  and the gap when the Government shelves a report.
- **ls.5.5 / crime.4.1** — the sentencing and consent reports as worked examples of a "contemporary
  law reform issue".
- **Evaluation** — implementation rates (the ALRC reports around 85% of its reports substantially
  implemented), time lags (often years), and the Attorney-General's control of the agenda.

## Caution

Report numbers and dates are as published; check the commissions' websites. "Implemented" is the
commissions' own assessment. The NSW LRC was largely dormant 2012–16 for lack of references — a
point about dependence on government that students can make.
