The Charter is both a **treaty** and the **constitution of the UN**. It was drafted at San
Francisco as the war ended, with Australia's H V Evatt a leading voice for small states, and it
sits above all later UN instruments: the UDHR (1948) was adopted *under* it, and the human rights
covenants give its art 55 pledge content.

## What it establishes

| Organ | Charter chapter | Human-rights role |
|---|---|---|
| **General Assembly** | IV | Adopts declarations (UDHR) and opens treaties for signature; resolutions are recommendations only |
| **Security Council** | V, VII | The only organ whose decisions **bind** members (art 25); may authorise sanctions and force under Chapter VII — subject to the **veto** of the five permanent members (art 27) |
| **Economic and Social Council** | X | Oversaw the Commission on Human Rights until the **Human Rights Council** (2006, a GA body) replaced it; the Universal Periodic Review runs there |
| **International Court of Justice** | XIV | The UN's judicial organ; jurisdiction only by consent (*South Africa v Israel* is a current example of a treaty-based case) |
| **Secretariat** | XV | The Secretary-General; the Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights (1993) sits here |

## The two commitments that pull against each other

- **Art 1(3), arts 55–56** — the purposes include "promoting and encouraging respect for human
  rights and for fundamental freedoms for all"; members "pledge themselves to take joint and
  separate action" to achieve them.
- **Art 2(1) and 2(7)** — the organisation rests on "the sovereign equality of all its Members"
  and nothing authorises the UN "to intervene in matters which are essentially within the domestic
  jurisdiction of any state" — except Chapter VII enforcement. **State sovereignty** (hr.2.1,
  inv.6.2) is therefore built into the Charter, and the Security Council veto is how the most
  powerful states keep it.
- **Art 2(4)** — the prohibition on the threat or use of force; **art 51** — self-defence.

## Use it for

- **hr.2.2 (role of the UN)** — the organs and what each can actually do; the binding/non-binding
  line (art 25 v GA resolutions) is the exam point.
- **hr.2.1 / inv.6.2 (state sovereignty)** — art 2(7) is the text to quote; the "responsibility
  to protect" (2005 World Summit) is the attempt to qualify it.
- **hr.2.3 (IGOs)** — the UN as the model IGO; regional bodies (EU, AU, ASEAN) sit outside it.
- **inv.6.4 / inv.6.6 / inv.6.7 / chg.3** — courts (ICJ), tribunals (the ad hoc tribunals created by Security
  Council resolution under Chapter VII) and NGOs (consultative status under art 71).
- **inv.6.3 / inv.7.2** — Australia incorporates Chapter VII sanctions through the *Charter of the United
  Nations Act 1945* (Cth) — the one Australian Act named after the Charter.

## Caution

The Charter has barely been amended (1965, 1973: enlarging the Councils); the veto cannot be
removed without the veto powers' consent (art 108). The Human Rights Council and the OHCHR are not
in the Charter text — they were created later by resolution. Use the UDHR and covenants for the
*content* of rights; the Charter supplies the *structure*.
