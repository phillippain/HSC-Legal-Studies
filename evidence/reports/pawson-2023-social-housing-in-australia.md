A conference paper delivered **to Legal Studies teachers** by a leading housing academic, which is
why it is unusually well pitched: definitions, then data, then the law.

## What social housing is

Rental housing provided by **government or a government-regulated not-for-profit**, where:

- rent is **administratively set below market** — in Australia usually **25% of tenant income**;
- **eligibility to register is strictly limited by income**;
- allocations are **prioritised by assessed need**; and
- tenure is **relatively secure** compared with the private market.

## The scale

- Australia has a **market-dominated housing system**. Social housing is **4% of total stock**, down
  from a **zenith of about 6% in 1996**.
- Within social housing, around **three quarters is public housing**.
- Not-for-profit **community housing has doubled in the past ten years to more than 100,000
  dwellings**. Larger NSW providers include **St George Community Housing, Bridge Housing and Home in
  Place** (formerly Compass).

## Access, and why the waiting list understates need

Applications are increasingly **online**, with "increasingly onerous documentation requirements to
justify possible priority status" — which makes **support workers' help in collating documents** an
important function in its own right. Registrations must be **confirmed periodically** (annually in
NSW and Queensland, six-monthly in Tasmania), and **failure to respond risks deletion** from the
register; how consistently that is applied "is not known".

The register is therefore not a measure of need:

- **far more people are homeless or in rental stress than are registered**;
- many never register because the chance of being housed is slim; and
- others **give up**, realising an offer could be years or decades away.

Census-based analysis (City Futures) for **2021**:

| Measure | NSW households |
|---|---|
| Unmet need for social **and affordable** housing | about **221,500** |
| Unmet need for **social** housing | about **143,900** |
| Social housing register 2022 (excluding existing tenants) | about **52,200** |

## Social housing and the law

- Terms of occupancy are **subject to state and territory *Residential Tenancies Act* legislation**:
  standard rental regulation applies, both the public housing service and community housing providers
  carry **landlord obligations**, and disputes — including eviction and tenant complaints about the
  landlord — go to the **Tribunal**.
- The differences between social and private tenants are "generally a matter of **policy** not a
  matter of **law**", though some *Residential Tenancies Acts* contain **limited carve-outs** varying
  their terms for social housing.

## Use it for

- **shel.3.4 — the three numbers to build an answer on.** **4% of stock** (from 6%), **25% of income**
  as rent, and a **waiting list of about 52,200 against unmet need of about 143,900** in NSW. The gap
  between the register and the need is the whole argument, and this gives you both figures from one
  source.
- **"The waiting list understates the need" is the insight most answers miss.** People do not
  register because registering is pointless, or are deleted for not replying to a form. Use it
  whenever a question invites you to quote a waiting list, and note the register is an
  **administrative artefact**, not a count of people who need housing.
- **Social housing tenants are tenants (shel.2.2 / shel.2.6)** — governed by the *Residential
  Tenancies Act 2010* like anyone else, with the same Tribunal. That is why `kelly-v-nsw-lahc-2026`
  looks like an ordinary tenancy case. And the key distinction — social tenants' different treatment
  is **policy, not law** — is a precise point about where their protections actually come from.
- **The shift from public to community housing (shel.2.9 / shel.2.8)** — community housing **doubled
  to 100,000+ dwellings** in a decade while public housing shrank as a share. Name the providers.
  Then ask the accountability question: a government landlord is answerable through Parliament and
  the Ombudsman; a not-for-profit landlord is answerable through a **regulator** (the
  *Community Housing Providers (National Law)*).
- **Access to justice before you even have a tenancy (inv.1.4)** — onerous documentation, online
  applications, periodic re-confirmation, deletion for non-response. A good example of
  **administrative burden** operating as a rationing mechanism.
- **International comparison** — the paper benchmarks Australia's share against European countries.
  Useful for arguing that 4% is a political choice, not a natural level.

## Caution

A **conference presentation from March 2023** — slides, not a peer-reviewed paper — so cite Pawson
and the date, and go to **City Futures** or AIHW/ROGS for the underlying data. Everything here is
**pre-dates the Housing Australia Future Fund's operation**: the paper describes the HAFF as a new
commitment of **$10 billion** generating investment income to fund **30,000** social and affordable
units over five years, which is the 2022 framing; the target has since been described differently
(see `anao-haff-2026`), so do not quote the 30,000 figure as current. The **unmet need** figures are
**census-based estimates** by one research centre, not official counts, and "need" is a modelled
concept — attribute them. The **4%** share, the **25% of income** rent setting and the register
figures will all have moved since 2022; verify before quoting. The paper is **national with NSW
examples**, and social housing law and policy differ in every state. Pawson is a housing researcher
who argues publicly for expanded social housing investment; the data is solid, the framing is a
position.
