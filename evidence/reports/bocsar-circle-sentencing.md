Circle sentencing brings Aboriginal Elders and community members into sentencing an Aboriginal
offender. Reducing reoffending is **a stated aim of the program**, and an earlier evaluation had
suggested it was working. BOCSAR tested it three ways.

| Question | Method | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| Does it reduce the **frequency** of reoffending? | 68 people circle sentenced before 2006, against a control group of 68 matched on Aboriginality, age, gender, priors, offence type and prior prison | **Yes — but so did the control group.** 77.9% of the circle group had fewer offences afterwards, against **76.5%** of the control group |
| Do participants take **longer** to reoffend? | Cox regression on 153 circle-sentenced people against **21,324** Aboriginal people sentenced in a traditional court, Feb 2002 – June 2007, controlling for sex, age, offence type, concurrent offences, bail status, priors and prior prison | **No significant difference** |
| Is subsequent offending **less serious**? | Same comparison | **No significant difference** |

## Use it for

- **Alternative methods of sentencing**, as the counter-evidence almost no answer has. Circle
  sentencing is routinely asserted to reduce reoffending; the best available NSW evaluation says it
  does not, and says so with a matched control group.
- **The difference between a program's aims and its effects** — and the harder point: a program can
  fail its stated aim and still be worth having. Circle sentencing also pursues participation,
  legitimacy and Aboriginal community involvement in a system that has excluded it. An answer that
  reports "it doesn't work" has read half the evidence; an answer that asks **what it should be
  measured against** is arguing.
- Pair with **BOCSAR CJB 279**, where a diversion program *did* cut reoffending by 16%. Two NSW
  evaluations, opposite results, same methodology family — that contrast is what makes a judgement
  on diversionary programs credible rather than assumed.

## Caution

An older study — check BOCSAR for any later evaluation before relying on it as current. The control
group was matched, not randomised, and 68 people is small for the first analysis. "No significant
difference" means the study could not detect one, not that none exists.
