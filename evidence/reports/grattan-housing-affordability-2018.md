The report that set the terms of the Australian housing debate. Its central finding is a claim about
**equality, not economics**:

> "Owning a home increasingly depends on who your parents are, a big change from 35 years ago when
> home-ownership rates were high for all levels of income."

Home ownership is falling among everyone under 65, fastest among those on low incomes, and the
people pushed out — increasingly renters — spend more of their income on housing. House prices **more
than doubled in real terms over 20 years**; since 2012 they rose **50% in Melbourne and 70% in
Sydney**.

## Why, on this account

A "perfect storm" of rising incomes, falling interest rates, rapid migration, **tax and welfare
settings feeding demand**, and **planning rules restricting supply**. Record construction was only
"the bare minimum" needed to keep up with record population growth, and a decade of accumulated
shortage pushes young people to form households later.

## The recommendations — demand and supply together

| Who | What |
|---|---|
| **Commonwealth** | Cut the **CGT discount to 25%**, phased 5 points a year; **limit negative gearing** by quarantining wage and salary income so investment losses offset only investment income |
| **Commonwealth** | Include the value of a high-priced home above a threshold (say **$500,000**) in the **Age Pension assets test**, and extend the Pension Loans Scheme |
| **States** | **Swap stamp duties for a general property tax**; broaden land tax to **owner-occupied** housing |
| **States** | Allow more homes in **inner and middle suburbs**, small-scale urban infill **without council approval**, and denser development **"as of right"** |
| **Commonwealth** | Enforce existing limits on **foreign investment** in established housing |
| **Commonwealth** | Develop a **population policy** articulating the goals of migration |

Building an extra **50,000 homes a year for a decade** could leave prices **5–20% lower** than
otherwise.

## Use it for

- **shel.3.1 (affordability)** — the analytical backbone of the dot point. Most students argue supply
  *or* demand; this report insists on both, and names the specific tax and planning levers.
- **Why reform does not happen (chg.4.2, dis.3.2)** — the report's most quotable passage is political,
  not economic: it took "neglectful governments two decades" to create the problem, they "preferred
  to appear to address" it, and the politics are hard "because most voters own a home or an
  investment property" and resist anything that might dent its price. That is an argument about
  **who law reform serves**, and it is unusual to have it stated so plainly in a think-tank report.
- **inv.5.2 (division of powers)** — the recommendations split cleanly: tax levers are Commonwealth,
  planning and stamp duty are state. Neither level can fix it alone, which is itself the explanation
  for inaction.
- **shel.1.2 / shel.1.3 (right to shelter, obligation to provide)** — set the market framing here
  against the ICESCR framing in `housing-shelter-or-asset-2026`. Same problem, two different
  languages.
- **A benchmark to measure later reform against** — the 2026 negative gearing and CGT changes are
  roughly what Grattan proposed in 2018. Eight years is the measure of responsiveness.

## Caution

**March 2018** — the oldest major source in this stack, and the figures (price growth to 2018,
interest rates, migration) are well out of date. Use it for its **analysis and recommendations**,
not its numbers; for current data use the Anglicare snapshot, Domain's rental reports or the more
recent Grattan work. It is an **independent think-tank**, not a government or a court: the authors
note the opinions are theirs, and its policy positions (broadening land tax to owner-occupiers,
including the family home in the pension assets test) are contested and were not adopted. Check
which recommendations have since been implemented before writing about them as proposals — several
have moved.
