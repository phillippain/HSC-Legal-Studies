BOCSAR compared **5,064 jury** and **805 judge-alone** trials finalised in the NSW District and
Supreme Courts, 2011–2019 (guilty pleas and mental-illness special verdicts excluded), matched by
entropy balancing, and interviewed 12 judges, prosecutors and defence lawyers.

## Key findings

| | |
|---|---|
| Share of trials that were judge-alone, 2011–2019 | **13.7%** (District 14.1%, Supreme 8.8%) |
| Trend | about 6% of higher court matters in 2005 to just under 18%; jumps after the 2011 "interests of justice" amendment and 2020 COVID measures |
| Probability of acquittal | **+9 percentage points** for judge-alone |
| Prison sentence | **7.6 months shorter** on average |
| Trial length | shorter for prejudicial and complex offences |

Practitioners said applications are mostly made where evidence is **prejudicial** (e.g. prior proven
offending) or **complex** (scientific or financial). The law: *Criminal Procedure Act 1986* (NSW)
ss 132–132A — the court must order a judge-alone trial if the prosecution agrees, may order it in
the interests of justice if not, and cannot order one unless the accused has had legal advice.

The literature review is useful in its own right: Trimboli (2008, CJB 119) found 55.4% of NSW
jurors read "beyond reasonable doubt" as "sure", 22.9% "almost sure" and 21.7% "very" or "pretty
likely"; a 2019 survey found 82.8% of judges think jurors have some difficulty with the law.

## Use it for

## Caution

BOCSAR's conclusion is explicit: the differences are **associations**; it cannot rule out
confounding, such as weaker prosecution cases going judge-alone. Do not write that judges are
"more lenient".
