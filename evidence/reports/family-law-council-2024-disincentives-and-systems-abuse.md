The **Family Law Council** — the Attorney-General's standing advisory body — was asked two
questions: do the laws **discourage victims from disclosing** family violence and child abuse, and
is the legal system itself **used to abuse or control** victims. It answered yes to both, and made
**eleven recommendations**.

## The victim's dilemma

The *Family Law Act 1975* puts the child's best interests first. But:

- the **2006 reforms** introduced the "**friendly parent**" consideration — parents were expected to
  support the child's relationship with the other parent — and **penalised knowingly false
  allegations**, which deterred disclosure;
- the **2012 amendments** removed the friendly parent provision, **widened the definition of family
  violence** and gave courts clearer power to prioritise protection from harm.

The residual problem the Council identified is the one worth teaching: parents still fear that if an
allegation of abuse is **not proven**, the court may read it as obstruction and **place the child
with the other parent**. That is the "**victim's dilemma**" — stay silent, or risk losing your child.
Cultural and systemic barriers make it worse for **Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander families**.

## Systems abuse

The Council's second finding is that the legal process is itself a tool of control: **repeated
applications and court events**, false reports, denial of financial access, and manipulation of the
family law, child protection and Centrelink systems — producing continuing stress and trauma while
appearing, on the surface, to be the exercise of legal rights.

## The recommendations worth knowing

- **Rec 6 — define it.** Add a new **s 4AB(2)(k)** to the examples of family violence: "**systems
  abuse** involving the use of systems and processes by one party to assert power and control over
  the other party including use of the legal system and litigation tactics to gain an advantage over
  or to harass, intimidate, discredit or otherwise control the other party."
- **Rec 8 — judicial notice.** A new section letting a judge proceed, **without proof**, on
  propositions including that family violence is **not limited to physical abuse**, **may consist of
  a single act**, or may consist of acts that look "**minor or trivial**" in isolation but form a
  pattern; that there is **no typical or proper response** to violence; that it is **not uncommon**
  to stay, to leave and return, or **not to report**; and that **leaving can increase the risk of
  harm**. Plus a list of controlling behaviours — dependence, isolation, monitoring day-to-day
  activities, restricting movement, humiliation, compelling unlawful conduct.
- **Rec 9 — social science evidence.** Allow opinions from the **National Domestic and Family
  Violence Bench Book** (Australian Institute of Judicial Administration) to be adduced **without the
  author giving evidence**.
- **Rec 2 — who gets appointed.** Amend **ss 11(2) and 111(2) of the *Federal Circuit and Family
  Court of Australia Act 2021*** (and s 11(3) of the WA Act) so that suitability for appointment as a
  family law judge includes "**demonstrated commitment to undertaking continuing education,
  including as to the nature, causes and impacts of family violence**".
- **Rec 1 — cultural safety.** Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people "must be empowered to
  design, lead and drive" culturally safe responses, services and professional training.
- **Rec 4 — check back.** The **2027 review** of the *Family Law Amendment Act 2023* should revisit
  whether disincentives to reporting remain.
- **Recs 5 and 11** — Attorney-General's Department guides on competing allegations of abuse and
  "**alienating**" behaviour, and on identifying coercive or controlling behaviour.

## Use it for

- **fam.2.4 — the best law reform source in the Family option.** A named body, a dated report,
  eleven numbered recommendations, and a **draft section** you can quote. Most students cite
  "calls for reform"; this lets you cite the words proposed for the Act.
- **"Systems abuse" is the concept to take away.** It reframes litigation itself as abuse, and
  it is the sharpest possible answer to "how effective is the family law system?" — because the
  system's own procedures are the mechanism of harm.
- **Rec 8 is a gift for the trial process and the rules of evidence (dis.2.4 / inv.4.1)** — judicial
  notice means **the burden of educating the court is taken off the victim**. Connect it to the
  practical point that proving a pattern of coercive control is hard precisely because each act looks
  trivial.
- **A reform that defeated its own purpose (chg.1.3)** — the 2006 "friendly parent" rule and the
  false-allegation penalty were designed to stop parents obstructing contact, and they silenced
  victims. An unusually clean example of **unintended consequences**, and of a later Parliament
  (2012) fixing it.
- **Judicial appointment criteria (inv.6.3)** — asking that family violence education be a condition
  of appointment is a structural, not a procedural, reform. Good for the courts' role and for
  accountability.
- **Self-determination (hr.2.9 / fam.t.values)** — Rec 1 is an Indigenous-led design principle, not a
  consultation requirement. Pair it with `nit-2024-family-is-culture`.
- **Pair it with a case.** *Melzer & Powles (No 2)* and the long interim histories in the 2026 family
  judgments show what repeat litigation looks like from the inside.

## Caution

**Recommendations, not law.** This is advice to the Attorney-General from an advisory council: as at
late 2026 **s 4AB(2)(k) had not been enacted**, and nothing here is in force — check the current
*Family Law Act* before stating any of it as law, and say "the Council recommended" every time. The
report is **Report 1a and 1b, 2024**, published in **March 2025**, so it predates the full operation
of the *Family Law Amendment Act 2023* and the 2024 property amendments; its own Rec 4 anticipates a
**2027 review**, which is the thing to watch. "Systems abuse" has **no settled legal definition** —
the Council proposed one. The report deals with **competing** allegations of abuse and parental
alienation (Rec 5 and Appendix C), which is contested territory: do not use it to suggest that
allegations of abuse are generally false, nor that alienation claims are generally pretextual. It is
a **Commonwealth family law** report and says nothing about state criminal coercive control offences.
The library also holds a short teaching **summary** of this report; the summary's framing is not the
Council's, so quote the report.
