Established in October 1987 by the Hawke Government with the states, after a campaign by families and the Committee to Defend Black Rights over a wave of deaths in police and prison cells, the Commission investigated every Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander death in custody between 1 January 1980 and 31 May 1989 — **99 deaths**, each the subject of its own report.

## Findings

- The deaths were not caused by foul play, with few exceptions; most followed failures of care, supervision and duty by custodial officers.
- Aboriginal people did not die in custody at a higher rate than non-Aboriginal prisoners — but they were held in custody at a grossly disproportionate rate (the Commission's 1988 survey found Aboriginal people about 27 times more likely to be in police custody), which was the underlying cause.
- The over-representation came from dispossession, poverty, policing practices and the criminalisation of public drunkenness; the Commission called for self-determination as the frame for every response.

## Key recommendations (of 339)

- **Rec 87–92**: arrest and imprisonment as a last resort; decriminalise public drunkenness.
- **Rec 161–165**: screening and care of prisoners at risk; hanging points removed; no person left alone in a cell when at risk.
- **Rec 224**: notification of the Aboriginal Legal Service whenever an Aboriginal person is detained (the Custody Notification Service, mandatory in NSW only from 2016).
- **Rec 339**: a process of reconciliation — which became the Council for Aboriginal Reconciliation.

## Caution

Implementation claims are contested: governments have reported high percentages "implemented"; a 2018 Deloitte review for the Commonwealth found 64% fully implemented, and Aboriginal organisations dispute the counting. Pair with the deaths-since-1991 record for the outcome evidence.
