**In-depth interviews with 53 private tenants** in NSW and Queensland, by researchers at UTS and the
University of Queensland, funded by the **Australian Research Council**. The authors note at the
outset that "**the paucity of official statistics makes it difficult to know exactly how common
evictions are**" — in **2019–20, 13.8% of private renters moved because their lease was terminated or
not renewed**, and beyond "a report or two" little is known about what happens to those households.

Crucially, **the interviews were conducted before no-grounds eviction was abolished in NSW**, and
such evictions are still allowed in other states and territories.

## The four findings

**1. Poor mental health.** The ease with which a landlord could end a tenancy "evoked persistent
anxiety for most of the interviewees", especially those on low incomes. *Susan*, on the Disability
Support Pension, lived in constant fear of homelessness: "if you are somebody who comes from a
non-English-speaking background, or you have a disability, or have no ability to enforce [the
legislation], **it's on the tenant to take up the laws and to do something about it**. And if you
don't have any of those abilities, you're just going to be on your way to homelessness very, very
soon." *Grace*, given a no-grounds termination she believed was so her rent could be raised: "I'm
still trying to settle into this new place with that trauma of being uprooted all of a sudden."

**2. Financial hardship.** *Sarah*, her husband and three children moved **at least six times since
2014**, mostly involuntarily: "when you get asked to move, you need to have a **bond ready to go at
the next place before you receive your bond back**, which is a killer" — plus cleaners, removalists
and bond deductions each time. *Brenda*, a single mother of two in regional Queensland, had **48
hours** to leave after her rental was condemned and had **$200** left after bills: "once I moved that
was it… I struggled the following week for everything."

**3. Reluctance to complain.** The knowledge that rent could be raised or the tenancy ended "evoked
**silent compliance**". *Alice* held off complaining for an extended period about mould that made her
grandson's bedroom unusable in a home they had rented for eight years: "that's why I didn't complain
because I said to everybody, 'as soon as I complain he'll kick us out'… If I hadn't complained, we'd
still be there." *Sarah* on a landlord's unannounced visits: "I was **petrified** of being kicked out
if we fought back and so… we let him onto the property **16 times in 10 months** and said nothing."
When they finally complained they were given notice, the landlord claiming maintenance required the
property to be vacant.

**4. Ending up in a worse home.** *Jan* and her partner, older renters on benefits, were evicted
after ten years when the property was sold to developers: her partner attempted suicide, the
relationship ended, and she **lived in a tent** on land her mother had bought. "We looked around for
somewhere else to rent and there was absolutely nowhere we could afford at all."

## Use it for

- **shel.2.2 — this is the best evidence in the library that a right you cannot safely use is not a
  right.** Tenants have statutory rights to repairs, to quiet enjoyment, to limited access by the
  landlord. Finding 3 shows they **do not use them** because the landlord can end the tenancy. Build
  an enforceability paragraph on that and it will beat any case citation.
- **"16 times in 10 months and said nothing"** and **"as soon as I complain he'll kick us out"** are
  the two quotes. Short, specific, devastating, and about the law rather than about feelings.
- **Susan's point is the access-to-justice thesis (inv.1.4)** — "it's on the tenant to take up the
  laws". The legislation is enforced by the party with the least capacity to enforce it, which is why
  tribunals, legal aid and tenants' advice services matter.
- **Why abolishing no-grounds eviction matters, and why it is not enough** — the research pre-dates
  the NSW reform, so use it as the **problem** the *Residential Tenancies Amendment Act 2024* was
  meant to solve, then pair it with `renovation-eviction-evidence-rollback-2025` on whether the
  specified grounds can be misused. That sequence — problem, reform, residual risk — is a complete
  law-reform answer.
- **Costs the law does not see (shel.2.3)** — needing a **new bond before the old one is returned**,
  plus cleaning and removalists. A concrete financial mechanism, better than "moving is expensive".
- **Compounding disadvantage (exp.6.2 / shel.3.3)** — disability, language, age and low income all
  made precarity worse, and Jan's eviction ended in a tent. The link from a tenancy termination to
  **homelessness** is drawn by evidence here, not asserted.
- **The data gap is itself a finding** — "the paucity of official statistics". Worth a sentence on
  responsiveness: a problem the state does not measure is one it cannot manage.

## Caution

**Qualitative research** — 53 in-depth interviews in two states, so it describes experience richly
but **proves nothing about frequency**; the only prevalence figure given is the **13.8%** of private
renters who moved on termination or non-renewal in **2019–20**, from official data. All names are
**pseudonyms** and the accounts are **tenants' own**, untested: *Grace* and *Alice* **believed** their
evictions were retaliatory, which is not a finding that they were. Published in **The Conversation**,
which is academic authorship in a public-facing outlet, and the authors are **ARC-funded researchers**
in housing policy whose conclusion ("a lot more needs to be done") is a position. Most importantly,
the interviews were conducted **before NSW abolished no-grounds terminations** — say so whenever you
use it, or the evidence will look out of date rather than foundational. It covers **NSW and
Queensland** private renters only, not social housing, boarders and lodgers, or land lease
communities. The material includes an **attempted suicide**; flag it before use.
