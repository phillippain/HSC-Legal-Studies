A short BOCSAR page that is more useful than its length suggests, because it is about **how you would
know** whether domestic violence is rising.

## The methodological problem

Police data on DFV assaults is easy to get but "**may not fully represent DFV trends in the
community, as changes in reporting practices and proactive policing can influence these figures**".
If an awareness campaign works, recorded crime goes **up**. BOCSAR therefore publishes **five
alternative measures** which, taken together, "offer a more accurate reflection of DFV trends than
recorded crime statistics alone".

## The five measures, and what they show

1. **Women and children murdered in DFV circumstances** (NSW Police). Because homicide is "almost
   always detected and recorded", it is a reliable indicator. **Relatively stable in NSW between 2018
   and 2024.**
2. **DFV-related hospitalisations** (NSW Health). Public hospitals record injuries inflicted by
   another person and the relationship to the alleged perpetrator, so this captures serious violence
   **independent of whether police were told**. **Relatively stable between 2018 and 2023.**
3. **Women who self-report experiencing violence** (ABS Crime Victimisation Survey) — the percentage
   of women aged 15+ reporting physical, threatened or sexual violence in the previous year. **No
   significant change from 2017/18 to 2023/24.**
4. **Adults who self-report DFV-related violence** (ABS CVS, pooled over three years for
   reliability). A **significant but modest decline, from 0.92% in July 2015 – June 2018 to 0.72% in
   July 2021 – June 2024.**
5. Plus police records for two serious DFV offences.

## Use it for

- **fam.2.4 — the measurement point, which almost no student makes.** When a question asks whether
  legal responses to family violence are effective, the first problem is that **the obvious statistic
  cannot answer it**: an increase in recorded DFV assaults might mean more violence, or more
  reporting, or more proactive policing. Saying that, and then naming BOCSAR's alternative measures,
  is a band 6 move.
- **Triangulation as a method (inv.1.2)** — three independent data sources (police, health,
  self-report survey), each with a different weakness, pointing the same way. Use it to argue a
  conclusion is **robust**, rather than quoting one figure.
- **Hospitalisation data is the cleverest measure here** — it captures serious violence whether or
  not anyone called the police, so it is immune to the reporting problem that makes crime statistics
  unreliable. Worth a sentence of its own.
- **The honest answer on effectiveness** — on every measure, NSW DFV has been **flat**, with one
  modest decline in self-reported victimisation. Set that against the volume of reform since 2018
  (coercive control criminalised, ADVO changes, the 2023 family law amendments, funding announcements)
  and you have a real evaluation: a great deal of law, and a prevalence line that has barely moved.
  Then the counter: these are **population measures** and reform effects are slow and partial.
- **Pair it with the coercive control monitor** (`bocsar-coercive-control-june-2026`) — 682 incidents,
  42 charged, 4 proven. One record measures the **problem**, the other the **response**.
- **Why victims do not report (fam.2.5)** — the whole reason these measures exist is that reporting is
  incomplete. Read it with the Family Law Council record on disincentives to disclosure, and with
  "**four undetected victim-survivors for every recorded one**" in the modern slavery records.

## Caution

A **web page** accessed in **July 2025**, not a report — it is a dashboard of trends, so the figures
and the trend statements **will have been updated**; check the live BOCSAR page before quoting any of
them, and note the measures end at different years (murders to 2024, hospitalisations to 2023,
self-report to 2023/24). The charts themselves **did not survive extraction**, so this record carries
BOCSAR's own trend statements, not the underlying numbers; go to the linked PDFs ("Domestic violence
related murder in NSW", "Trends in Self-Reported Violence Victimisation") for data. "**Relatively
stable**" and "**no significant change**" are statistical statements about **trend**, not claims that
levels are acceptable — do not write that domestic violence is "not a problem" or "under control"; the
levels are high and unchanged. The self-report decline (0.92% to 0.72%) is described by BOCSAR as
**significant but modest** and is pooled over three-year windows, so it cannot be attributed to any
particular reform. These are **NSW** figures. Self-report surveys undercount the most serious and
most controlled victims, who are least able to answer a survey honestly.
