The first comprehensive review of the family law system since the *Family Law Act* commenced in 1976.
**60 recommendations in seven groups.**

## The central finding

In the federal family courts, **family violence, child abuse or other complex factors now make up the
majority of cases** — but there is **no federal body with investigative powers akin to a child
protection department**, and the family courts **cannot compel a child protection department to
intervene or to investigate** the court's own concerns.

So children "**fall through the gaps**" between three systems: **federal family law**, **state and
territory child protection**, and **state and territory responses to family violence**. The ALRC's
view was that this can be remedied only by a **single court** able to resolve family law, child
protection and family violence issues **together** — which meant **returning family law jurisdiction
to the states and territories** and, eventually, **abolishing the federal family courts**.

## The seven groups of recommendations

1. **Closing the jurisdictional gap** — the single-court proposal above, plus expanding the
   information-sharing platform in the **National Domestic Violence Order Scheme** to include **family
   court orders and state child protection orders**.
2. **Simpler property division** — a starting position that separated couples made **equal
   contributions** during the relationship.
3. **Compliance with children's orders** — greater use of family consultants to help parents
   understand final parenting orders, and **limits on interim appeals**.
4. **Stricter case management** — clearer consequences for parties and advisers who do not resolve
   disputes "as quickly, inexpensively, and efficiently as possible, and with the least acrimony".
5. **Legislative simplification** — redraft the *Family Law Act* to make it easier to understand.
6. **Children's orders** — simplify the best-interests factors and **remove mandatory consideration
   of particular living arrangements**. The proposed list: what **best promotes the safety** of the
   child and the child's carers, including from family violence, abuse or other harm; **the child's
   views**; the child's **developmental, psychological and emotional needs**; the **benefit of
   maintaining relationships** with each parent and others significant to the child **where it is
   safe**; each carer's **capacity** to meet those needs, including willingness to seek support; and
   anything else relevant. And **replace the presumption of "equal shared parental responsibility"
   with a presumption of "joint decision making about major long-term issues"**.
7. **Encourage amicable dispute resolution** — more **FDR** and **legally assisted dispute resolution
   (LADR)**, with FDR available to clients affected by family violence **provided appropriate
   safeguards** manage safety risks and power imbalances, and LADR for families too complex for FDR
   who should not be pushed into a court pathway either.

## Use it for

- **fam.3.2 — this is where the 2023 amendments came from.** The *Family Law Amendment Act 2023*
  repealed the presumption of **equal shared parental responsibility** and replaced the best-interests
  factors with a shorter list headed by **safety**. Both were ALRC 135 recommendations. Being able to
  say "the 2023 reform implemented recommendations the ALRC made in 2019" is exactly the kind of
  law-reform chain a question rewards — and it shows **a four-year lag** between recommendation and
  statute.
- **The recommendation that was ignored is the more interesting one (fam.1.2 / inv.6.6)** — the ALRC
  recommended **abolishing the federal family courts** and returning jurisdiction to the states. The
  Commonwealth did the opposite: the *Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia Act 2021*
  **merged and entrenched** them. The library document itself asks, in a parenthesis, "has this been
  the case? The Federal Parliament seems to be heading in the other direction." Use it to show that
  law reform bodies **advise** and parliaments **choose**, and that the jurisdictional gap the ALRC
  identified has not been closed.
- **"No federal body with investigative powers"** is the sharpest sentence in the report. A court
  deciding where a child lives, confronted with allegations of abuse, **cannot order an investigation
  and cannot compel the state department to do one**. Pair it with the Family Law Council's **systems
  abuse** report and with `smh-2025-jack-died-in-state-care` to show both halves of the same gap.
- **Agencies and mechanisms of law reform (chg.1.3 / chg.2.3)** — a **statutory law reform
  commission**, a reference from the Attorney-General, 60 recommendations, partial implementation.
  The cleanest worked example in the Family option of how the law reform process actually runs.
- **ADR with safeguards (dis.1.5 / fam.2.5)** — the shift the ALRC endorsed is that FDR **should** be
  available to people affected by family violence with safeguards, rather than excluded. Contested,
  and useful for arguing both sides of mediation in violent relationships.
- **Simpler property division (fam.2.3)** — the proposed **equal contributions starting point**, which
  is not the law. Compare it with the actual s 79 process in `duffy-duffy-2026`.

## Caution

The library document is a **four-page teaching summary**, not the report — it paraphrases, it contains
the summariser's own parenthetical commentary, and it covers only some of the 60 recommendations.
**Go to ALRC Report 135 itself** before quoting anything as the Commission's words, and cite the
report, not this file.

**Recommendations are not law.** Some were implemented (the 2023 amendments on parental
responsibility and the best-interests factors); the headline recommendation — **returning family law
to the states and abolishing the federal family courts** — was **not**, and the opposite happened in
2021. Check the current *Family Law Act* before describing any of this as the law, and do not write
that "the ALRC abolished" or "the ALRC changed" anything: it recommended.

The report is from **2019** and the system has moved substantially since — the 2021 court merger, the
2023 amendments, the 2024 property amendments, and the Family Law Council's 2024 report, whose Rec 4
anticipates a **2027 review** of the 2023 Act. Read ALRC 135 as the **origin** of the current
settings, not as a description of them.
