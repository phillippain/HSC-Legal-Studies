Section 128 is the only way to alter the Constitution: a bill passed by both houses (or one house twice), then a vote of electors in which the proposal must win a **double majority** — a majority of voters nationally *and* a majority of voters in a majority of the states (four of six). Territory votes count in the national total only.

## The record

| | |
|---|---|
| Proposals put | 45 (19 referendum days), 1906–2023 |
| Carried | 8: Senate elections 1906; state debts 1910 and 1928; social services 1946; Aboriginal people 1967; casual Senate vacancies, territory voters and judges' retirement age 1977 |
| Failed with a national majority but not four states | several, including the 1937 aviation, 1977 simultaneous elections and 1984 proposals |
| Last success | 21 May 1977 |

Every proposal that passed had the support of both major parties; no proposal opposed by one of them has ever succeeded.

## Use it for

- **inv.5.5** — the process and the numbers; why the Constitution is described as rigid and why governments avoid referendums.
- **chg.3.1 / chg.4.2** — a legislative method of change with a high failure rate; and the contrast with change by High Court interpretation, which needs no vote (*Tasmanian Dam*).

## Caution

"45 referendums" counts questions, not days; some sources say 44 before 2023 and count differently. Plebiscites (conscription 1916–17, the 1977 anthem poll, the 2017 marriage survey) are not referendums and change nothing by themselves.
