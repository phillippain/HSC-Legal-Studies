The jurisdictional map for surrogacy, which is the part students most often get wrong. Surrogacy is
regulated **state by state** — commercial surrogacy is prohibited everywhere, altruistic surrogacy
permitted under strict conditions (*Surrogacy Act 2010* (NSW), *Assisted Reproductive Treatment Act
2008* (Vic), *Surrogacy Act 2010* (Qld)) — but the **Commonwealth** controls what matters once a
child is born abroad.

## The two federal levers

**Citizenship and passports — *Australian Citizenship Act 2007* (Cth).** A child born overseas
through surrogacy may acquire citizenship **by descent** where at least one intended parent is an
Australian citizen and a **biological connection** can be shown. The Department of Home Affairs
commonly requires **DNA testing**. Immigration and citizenship are Commonwealth heads of power, so
this is where the federal government is unavoidably involved.

**Parentage — *Family Law Act 1975* (Cth).** Parental responsibility and parenting orders are
federal. Some overseas jurisdictions recognise the intended parents immediately, while Australian law
may still treat the **surrogate** as the legal parent until a parentage order is made under state
law. The result is a child who is an Australian citizen with an Australian passport and **no
Australian-recognised parents**.

## The two cases

**Ellison & Karnchanit [2012] FamCA 602.** An Australian couple entered a commercial surrogacy
arrangement in **Thailand**, unlawful under Queensland's *Surrogacy Act 2010* (s 56). Ryan J raised
concerns about possible exploitation of the surrogate and uncertainty about consent and payments, and
weighed the illegality against the children's welfare — noting the harm to the children if their
parents were imprisoned. **Parenting orders were made**, conferring parental responsibility; they did
**not** transfer legal parentage.

**Bernieres & Dhopal [2017] FamCAFC 180.** The Full Court (Bryant CJ, Strickland and Ryan JJ)
dismissed an appeal and **declined to find the intended parents were legal parents**. The reasoning
is the important part: **s 60HB covers the field** for surrogacy arrangements, so the declaratory
power in s 69VA was not available; **s 60H does not apply** to a surrogacy arrangement at all (read
onto these facts it would make the **birth mother and her partner** the parents); being the
**biological father** "does not translate into him being a parent" under the Act; and neither
appellant was a "prescribed adopting parent", so leave for step-parent adoption under s 60G was
unavailable. The child had been granted an Australian **Certificate of Citizenship by Descent** and a
passport in 2014.

## Use it for

- **fam.1.2 / fam.3.3 — the federal gap, in one sentence.** "The Commonwealth will give the child a
  passport but not a parent." Citizenship by descent requires a biological link; legal parentage
  requires a state parentage order the family cannot get because the arrangement was commercial.
- **Bernieres is the authority to cite (fam.3.2)** — not the vague proposition that "Australian
  courts do not recognise overseas arrangements", but the precise one: **s 60HB covers the field**,
  **s 60H does not apply**, and **biology is not parentage** under the Act. Specificity like this is
  what earns marks.
- **Compare it with Masson v Parsons (2019)** — there a sperm donor **was** a parent because of his
  role in the child's life. Put the two together and the question becomes: *when does the Act look at
  conduct, and when at a statutory presumption?* That is a genuine, examinable tension.
- **Ellison for the welfare-versus-illegality balance** — the same conflict as the 2025 DPP referral
  record, decided the other way. Two cases, 13 years apart, opposite outcomes on parenting orders:
  excellent for consistency and the rule of law.
- **CROC (hr.2.7)** — the child's right to **identity and nationality** against the concern that
  international commercial surrogacy may involve trafficking or exploitation. Australia must weigh
  both.
- **Pressure for national reform (chg.1.3)** — the conclusion is the exam-ready one: "inconsistencies
  remain, showing the need for clearer national reform", and media coverage of children stranded
  overseas has driven that debate.

## Caution

This is an **unattributed explainer in the library**, written for study and **not an authoritative
source** — it reads as having been drafted with AI assistance, so treat every proposition as a lead
to check, not a citation. Two corrections have been made in this record: its claim that *Bernieres*
turned on refusing to recognise "a parentage order from India" is **not the Full Court's reasoning**
(the reasoning is s 60HB, s 60H and s 69VA, set out above), and *Ellison* produced **parenting
orders, not a transfer of parentage**. The cases are **2012 and 2017**; the *Family Law Act* has been
substantially amended since (notably by the *Family Law Amendment Act 2023*), so **check the current
text of ss 60H, 60HB, 60G and 69VA** before quoting. "Commercial surrogacy is illegal across
Australia" is right, but only **NSW, Queensland and the ACT** criminalise entering such an
arrangement **overseas**. The countries listed as destinations (India, Ukraine) have since
**restricted or closed** surrogacy to foreigners. The **ALRC Review of Surrogacy Laws** final report
went to the Attorney-General by **11 September 2026** and must be tabled within 15 sitting days, so
it was **not public** in October 2026 — this document predates it.
