Most students learn family dispute resolution as the thing that happens **before** court: s 60I requires a certificate from an accredited FDR practitioner before a parenting application can be filed. This fact sheet is about the other kind — the conference the court orders **after** proceedings have started, under s 13C(1)(b) of the *Family Law Act 1975* (Cth).

## Who runs it, and what that changes

The conference is run by a **registrar** acting as the FDR practitioner, usually with a **court child expert** acting as family counsellor. Neither decides the matter and neither gives legal advice; but the registrar can make the orders at the end, including final orders, so parties who reach agreement "will not be required to appear before a judge".

That is the structural point. A private mediator can only produce an agreement that someone then has to turn into a consent order. A registrar conducting court-based FDR can mediate and then seal the result in the same day — which is why the court describes the purpose as letting parties "formalise your agreement and exit the court system".

## The two parts

| | What happens | Length |
|---|---|---|
| **Part 1** | Phone or Teams call with the registrar: meet them, set expectations, raise safety concerns, confirm what to bring. Lawyers must attend. | ~45 minutes |
| **Part 2** | The substantive conference: opening, confirming agreed and outstanding issues, negotiation, documenting the agreement. Joint sessions or shuttle, as the registrar assesses. | Full day (9–4) or half day (9–1) |

Both parts are confidential and both are **privileged** — what is said and what is offered cannot be used in court later. The two exceptions are stated plainly: a suspicion or risk of child abuse, and violence or threats of violence, must be reported to the child welfare authority.

## Safety as a gatekeeping question

The fact sheet makes screening explicit at two points. Before ordering FDR, the judge or registrar "will carefully consider your matter and assess whether any issues, such as family violence or allegations of abuse, make FDR inappropriate or require the FDR conference to be conducted in a particular manner." And a party worried about safety "can request that the FDR take place by **shuttle**" — parties in separate rooms, the registrar and child expert moving between them.

The court adds that the registrar will consider how the conference should run "even if you do not make this request." That is the court acknowledging what the family violence literature has repeatedly found: the person most at risk is often the least likely to ask.

## Use it for

- **fam.2.5 — the roles of courts and dispute resolution methods.** The cleanest Australian example of the line between court and ADR being blurred on purpose: the court is both the forum and the mediator.
- An **accessibility and resource-efficiency** argument with specifics: no extra affidavits unless ordered, phone or Teams attendance, no judge appearance if it settles.
- A **counterweight to "ADR is always cheaper and kinder"**. Court-based FDR is ordered, not chosen; attendance is compulsory ("You must attend all parts of the FDR conference as required by the Orders"); and it is only offered "where it is safe and appropriate for all parties to participate meaningfully."
- Pair it with a judgment where FDR was refused or failed — *Taggart & Gowden* [2026] FedCFamC2F 673 is useful, where counselling and independent advice preceded a breakdown anyway.

## Caution

This is a plain-language fact sheet for litigants, not a statement of law: it paraphrases s 13C and does not cover the s 60I pre-filing certificate scheme, the s 10J inadmissibility provisions, or the exceptions in s 60I(9). Do not cite it for the content of those sections.

Its "Division 1 | Division 2" heading means it applies in both divisions of the merged court. The resource allocation ("the time and resources to be made available to your matter will be carefully considered") is an administrative decision, not an entitlement — a fair point to make when evaluating accessibility.
