The **family violence provisions** in Division 1.5 and Schedule 2 of the *Migration Regulations 1994*
let some visa applicants keep a permanent residence pathway after a relationship ends because of
violence. The provisions apply to **listed subclasses only**, and only where the perpetrator is the
**sponsor or primary applicant** — so coverage turns on the visa and the abuser's role, not on the
violence.

## Covered and not covered

| | Covered (partner, prospective marriage, some skilled and family secondary applicants) | Not covered (student, visitor, seasonal worker) |
|---|---|---|
| Permanent residence | Can still be granted after the relationship ends | No family violence pathway; often only a **protection (asylum)** claim |
| Which visas | Partner (820, 309), prospective marriage (300, since 1 July 2024) and secondary applicants on listed subclasses | Student (500) and other temporary visas are not listed; abuse by other relatives is never covered |
| Proof | Family violence shown by a protection order made after the perpetrator was heard, a conviction, a *Family Law Act* s 114 injunction, or other evidence | A real chance of serious harm if returned home |
| Waiting time | Home Affairs prioritises at-risk applications | A protection claim can take **up to five years**, one lawyer says; the Tasmanian case took about two years including a tribunal appeal; bridging visas may lack work rights |
| Centrelink, Medicare | Wider access once permanent, subject to waiting periods | Often no Centrelink; Medicare only via ministerial orders |
| Leaving Violence Program | Eligible **regardless of visa status** | Eligible regardless of visa status |
| Housing | Can join public and social housing waiting lists | Often barred from social housing; stays in refuges longer |

The **Leaving Violence Program** (national from 1 July 2025) pays packages of up to **$5,155**,
including up to $1,500 cash, but only to people **18+ leaving an intimate partner** — violence by
other relatives is outside it. A partner **cannot cancel** the victim's visa; police and a state
protection order (an ADVO in NSW) remain available whatever the visa.

## Use it for

- **fam.2.4 (domestic violence)** — the cleanest illustration that a protective law can be defeated by
  a law made elsewhere for another purpose. Pairs directly with the migration-crackdown argument in
  the companion record `conversation-2026-migration-dfv`.
- **fam.1.4 / fam.1.5 (alternative family relationships, rights of parents and children)** — relevant
  because the law's protection attaches to a recognised partner relationship on a listed visa; other
  family arrangements get nothing.
- **ls.4.1 / ls.4.2 (reform conditions and agencies)** — a 15-year reform story. The **ALRC's Report
  117** (2011) recommended easing the evidence rules **and a new temporary visa** for temporary
  secondary applicants; the visa still does not exist. The 2018 *Path to Nowhere* survey counted
  **387** women on temporary visas seeking violence support in one month. A **Temporary Visa Holders
  Experiencing Violence Pilot** ran 2021–24 (about $17m, roughly **8,800 people** helped by Red
  Cross, 92% female) and found cash help, free legal advice and support beyond 90 days were what
  mattered. A coalition of 30+ bodies now asks, in its **2026 Blueprint for Reform**, for a
  **three-year substantive family violence visa** with work rights, Medicare, social security and a
  permanent residence pathway, and for data collected by visa status — there is **no official count**
  of temporary visa holders experiencing violence.
- **Law reform that *did* happen** — the provisions were expanded in **three stages in 2024** (1 July,
  15 October, 17 December) with $8.9m funding, adding **20 subclasses**, and Home Affairs says further
  changes are in development. This matters because it is the government's answer to the article.
- **ind.2.6 / administrative review** — a refusal goes to the **Administrative Review Tribunal**,
  which replaced the AAT on 14 October 2024.

## Caution

A **class-made summary** of a **news report built on one case and advocates' views**, not a study.
The ABC **does not name the regulations or mention the 2024 expansion**, so the article overstates
how narrow the provisions now are — a student answer that repeats "only partner visas" will be wrong.
The 400+ caseload figure and the five-year protection-visa estimate are the service's and one
lawyer's, unverified; the Medicare ministerial order was not checked; the $1.2 billion crisis
housing figure is not specific to temporary visa holders. Older DSS pages still say $5,000 rather
than $5,155. **Never use details that could identify the featured victim-survivor**, and check Home
Affairs' current list of eligible visas before relying on any coverage claim.
