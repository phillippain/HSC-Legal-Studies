A panel of citizens selected at random from the electoral roll, whose job is to determine guilt on
the evidence presented at trial. *Jury Act 1977* (NSW).

- **Eligibility**: Australian citizens aged 18 and over, enrolled to vote.
- **Commonly exempt**: people over 65, pregnant women, full-time carers of children, people who do
  not speak English, emergency services workers, people with disability, people with criminal
  convictions, and **members of the legal profession**.
- **Challenges**, made after a juror is called and before being sworn: a **peremptory challenge**
  (up to three, no reason required) and a **challenge for cause** (prejudice alleged).
- **Numbers**: normally 12, with discretion to empanel up to three extra for long trials, balloted
  back to 12 before deliberation (*Jury Amendment Act 2007* (NSW)). A trial may continue if the
  panel is not reduced below 10, or to 8 or 9 with the written approval of both prosecution and
  defence.
- **Misconduct**: a juror who engages in misconduct must be discharged; the whole panel must be
  discharged where there is a risk of a substantial miscarriage of justice, and a retrial follows.

**R v Skaf** (2004): two jurors visited the crime scene to conduct their own investigation — the
case that drove legislative reform of juror conduct.

## Use it for

- **Community participation against consistency.** The jury is the clearest expression of the
  community in the criminal trial, and the exemption list is the easiest place to argue that the
  jury is not in fact representative of it.
- **Law reform with a trigger**: *R v Skaf* is a short, memorable chain from misconduct to
  amendment.
- The **adversary system** (crime.3.2, and Preliminary ls.2.2) — the jury is what the adversarial
  contest is staged for.
