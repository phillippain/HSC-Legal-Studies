**ICESCR art 11** recognises the right to an adequate standard of living including housing. What
differs between countries is not the norm but whether anyone can **enforce** it.

- **South Africa — *Government of the Republic of South Africa v Grootboom*.** The Constitutional
  Court held the state's housing programme **unreasonable** for making no provision for people in
  desperate need, and ordered a remedy. A justiciable socio-economic right, enforced against a
  government, without the court designing the policy itself.
- **Canada — *Tanudjaja v Canada (Attorney General)* 2014 ONCA 852.** A right-to-housing claim was
  **struck out as non-justiciable**: the court held housing policy was not amenable to judicial
  determination. The considered argument on the other side.
- **Europe — *McCann v United Kingdom* (2008) 47 EHRR 40 and *Yordanova v Bulgaria* (App No 25446/06,
  ECtHR, 24 April 2012).** A middle path: losing one's home must be **proportionate** and subject to
  **independent review** — a procedural standard rather than a right to be housed.

## Australia by contrast

Dualist, with **no bill of rights**, and the **ICESCR Optional Protocol not ratified** — so there is
no individual complaint mechanism and no domestic cause of action. Art 11 has no enforcement pathway
a person seeking shelter could actually use. NSW eviction law only began to approach the European
proportionality standard with the **2024 reforms**, which require a stated ground and evidence.

## Use it for

- **The judgement sentence**: international law has been highly effective at **initiating a normative
  change** — shelter as a right rather than a commodity — and almost entirely ineffective at
  **compelling a domestic response**.
- **Enforceability, argued structurally rather than rhetorically.** *Grootboom* proves the
  ineffectiveness in Australia is a choice of architecture, not an inevitability; *Tanudjaja* is the
  honest counter-argument that courts are poorly placed to run housing policy. Put both.
