A reference note.

## Strict liability (`crime.1.3`)

Offences where the prosecution need prove only the **actus reus** — no **mens rea**. Most are
regulatory: traffic, licensing, pollution, food safety, workplace safety. The justification is
volume and deterrence: proving intent for every speeding ticket would be unworkable.

The defence that survives is **honest and reasonable mistake of fact** — *Proudman v Dayman* (1941).
It does **not** apply to **absolute liability** offences, where even that is excluded.

**The argument to make**: strict liability trades a principle of criminal responsibility for
administrative efficiency, and the trade is defensible only while the penalties stay small and the
conduct stays regulatory.

## Summary and indictable offences (`crime.1.6`)

| | Summary | Indictable |
|---|---|---|
| **Heard by** | Magistrate alone, **Local Court** | Judge and jury, **District** or **Supreme Court** |
| **Examples** | Offensive conduct, minor assault, most traffic | Murder, sexual assault, robbery, serious drug offences |
| **Procedure** | Quick and cheap; no committal | Committal in the Local Court to test the prima facie case |

The middle category matters: **Table 1 and Table 2 offences** under the *Criminal Procedure Act 1986*
(NSW) are indictable offences that may be **dealt with summarily** unless the prosecution (Table 1) or
the accused (Table 2) elects otherwise. It is the main mechanism keeping court volume manageable —
**resource efficiency** against the right to a jury trial, decided by an election rather than by the
accused's choice alone.

## Parties to a crime (`crime.1.7`)

- **Principal in the first degree** — the person who commits the act.
- **Principal in the second degree** — present and assisting or encouraging.
- **Accessory before the fact** — helps or encourages beforehand, not present.
- **Accessory after the fact** — knowing the offence was committed, assists the offender to escape
  detection or punishment.

Related and separately examinable: **joint criminal enterprise**, where parties agreeing on a crime
are liable for what falls within the agreement.

## Use it for

- **Classification as a practical question**, not a vocabulary test: each label answers *what must be
  proved*, *where it is heard*, and *who can be convicted*.
- **Strict liability is the cleanest example in the course of the law trading a right for efficiency**
  — useful in any balance question.
