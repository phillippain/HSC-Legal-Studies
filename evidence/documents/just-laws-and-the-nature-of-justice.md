A reference note.

## Characteristics of a just law

- **Known and accessible** — published in advance, in terms people can find and follow.
- **Certain and clear**, so conduct can be planned around it.
- **General** — it applies to everyone, not to a named person. *Kable* is the counter-example: an Act
  directed at one man, applied to his liberty.
- **Prospective**, not retrospective.
- **Capable of being obeyed**, and enforceable.
- **Made by a legitimate authority through a proper process**.
- **Based on widely held values**, and **reformable** when those values change.
- **Protects individual rights** while meeting society's needs.

## The nature of justice

**Equality** — like cases treated alike. Formal equality (everyone may sue) is not substantive
equality (only some can afford to). *Dietrich* is the case where the difference became a legal
problem.

**Fairness** — the process, not only the outcome: notice of the case against you, a hearing, an
unbiased decision-maker (*Kioa v West*), reasons, and a right of appeal.

**Access** — the practical ability to use the law. Physical, financial, linguistic and informational:
legal aid's means and merits tests, the 500,000 Australians a year missing out, the 39% unmet need in
tenant advice, and a tenant at NCAT against an agent who appears there weekly.

## Use it for

- **The distinction that carries both dot points**: the **rule of law** asks whether power is
  exercised *under law*; **justice** asks whether the law is *good*. A tyranny can have impeccable
  procedure.
- **Equality, fairness and access as three separate tests.** Most answers use them as synonyms. Show
  one being satisfied while another fails — a fair hearing that a person cannot afford to attend — and
  the answer is immediately stronger.
