A 7.30 report on extended families and friends applying for rentals **as a group**. Domain's
September 2026 quarter put capital-city vacancy rates at **0.3% (Hobart, Darwin) to 1.5%
(Canberra)**, near historic lows; **30.6%** of occupied private dwellings were rented at the 2021
Census; social housing has fallen to **4.0%** of households in 2025, from 4.7% in 2013.

## What the 2024–25 NSW reforms changed, and what they did not

| | Before | After (Oct 2024 / May 2025) |
|---|---|---|
| Ending a lease | 'No grounds' permitted | Valid reason with evidence — sale, renovation, owner moving in |
| Notice (no breach) | Shorter for some leases | 90 days periodic or fixed term over six months; 60 days if six months or less |
| Rent increases | No single rule | Once in any 12 months, none in the first year, from 31 Oct 2024 |
| Application costs | Background check fees charged | Banned; one fee-free rent payment option required |
| Pets | Landlord could simply refuse | Valid reason needed; no reply in 21 days is approval |
| Bonds | New bond each move | Smart Rental Bonds statewide, same tenants, $25 transfer fee |
| **Co-tenant liability** | Landlord can pursue **any one** co-tenant for the full debt | **Unchanged** |

**No reform caps the amount of a rent increase** — only the frequency. A large one-off rise remains
lawful.

## How a group tenancy actually works in NSW

Everyone named on the lease is a **co-tenant**; others are sub-tenants or occupants. Co-tenants share
the agreement's rights and obligations **jointly**, so the landlord can recover all the rent or
damage from one of them. A periodic co-tenant leaving gives **21 days' notice** to the landlord and
each co-tenant; a transfer needs the landlord's written consent, not unreasonably refused if an
original tenant stays; changes go on a Change of Shared Tenancy Arrangement form. Under **s 102**,
**NCAT** may end a co-tenant's tenancy or the whole agreement in special circumstances and may order
a break fee — but NCAT generally **cannot** resolve money disputes between co-tenants apart from bond
payouts, so unpaid bills go to the Local Court.

## Use it for

- **shel.2.2 / shel.2.3 (rights and obligations, protection for tenants)** — joint and several
  liability is the specific legal content here, and the article's silence on it is itself the teaching
  point: the market pushes people into the arrangement that carries the most risk.
- **shel.2.5 / shel.2.6 (dispute resolution, tribunals)** — a precise example of NCAT's **limits**,
  not just its role: s 102 can end a co-tenancy, but a co-tenant chasing a housemate for rent is in
  the Local Court.
- **shel.3.1 / shel.3.4 (affordability, social housing)** — one Perth renter's rent rose from $315 to
  **$760** a week since 2019, a **141%** increase, against a Perth median house rent of $750; social
  housing has shrunk as a share of stock, so private renting has no alternative.
- **ls.4.3 (mechanisms of reform)** — National Cabinet's **A Better Deal for Renters** (August 2023)
  set shared standards that **each state then legislates itself**, which NSW did in 2024–25. A clean
  example of cooperative federalism producing uneven law.
- **Competing responses** — the ACTU wants two-year minimum lease offers and public housing at 6% of
  stock; the Tenants' Union welcomes portable bonds but says the $25 fee hits low-income renters
  hardest.

## Caution

A **class-made summary** of a **human-interest report, not data**. The headline "one in five
applications" is **one Brisbane agency's own experience**, and the Perth rent rise is one renter. The
"31% of Australians rent" line is a **2021 household** measure, not people, and is five years old.
The claim that the May 2026 Budget tax changes are driving investors out is **disputed**: the changes
start **1 July 2027** and grandfather existing holdings, Treasury forecasts about $2 a week, NAB's
25–30% figure was a scenario rather than a forecast, and Domain's own September 2026 report described
rents as **stalling** across most capital cities. Only an agent, an investor-landlord and an academic
are quoted — no tenant advocate, no minister. Tenancy law is **state-based**: the cases are in WA and
Queensland while the law set out here is NSW.
