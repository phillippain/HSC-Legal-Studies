## State sovereignty

A state has supreme authority within its territory and independence outside it. Everything in
international law follows from this: there is **no world legislature and no compulsory police**, so
international law binds a state largely to the extent it has **consented** to be bound. That is the
source of both its weakness (enforceability) and its legitimacy.

## The four sources

1. **Customary international law** — consistent state practice followed out of a sense of legal
   obligation. Binding without signature; slow to form.
2. **Treaties, conventions and covenants** — binding on the parties. Signature indicates intent;
   **ratification** binds.
3. **Legal decisions** — the International Court of Justice, the International Criminal Court, and
   regional and specialist tribunals.
4. **Legal writings** — the work of respected jurists, as a subsidiary means of determining rules.

## Who acts

The **United Nations** — General Assembly (one state, one vote; resolutions not binding), Security
Council (binding resolutions, five vetoes), the Secretariat, and the ICJ. **Intergovernmental
organisations** act by agreement between states; **non-government organisations** such as Amnesty
International and Human Rights Watch have no legal power and work by documenting, reporting and
shaming.

## How it reaches Australia

Australia is **dualist**: a treaty is not part of domestic law merely because it has been ratified.
It becomes enforceable here when **Parliament passes legislation** implementing it — and, as the
**Tasmanian Dam Case** shows, ratification can itself supply the constitutional power to do so under
s 51(xxix). Where an implementing Act departs from the instrument, **the Act prevails**: the *Family
Law Act* over the CROC, for example.

Short of implementation, an instrument can still matter — as an aid to interpreting ambiguous
statutes, as a source of a legitimate expectation in administrative decision-making (*Minister for
Immigration and Ethnic Affairs v Teoh* (1995) 183 CLR 273), and as a standard against which domestic
law is publicly measured.

## Use it for

- The **enforceability criterion** at its sharpest — this is the topic where a right can be
  internationally recognised and domestically unavailable, and the explanation is structural rather
  than political.
- **Ratification is not protection.** Any human rights answer that treats signing a covenant as
  compliance can be corrected with one sentence from here.
