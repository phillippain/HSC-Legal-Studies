A practitioner's table collecting the authorities on **reasonable suspicion** under **ss 21 and 36
of LEPRA** — the threshold for a search without a warrant — case by case, with the asserted grounds
for the stop in one column and what the court made of them in the other.

## The test, as everything else is measured against

***R v Rondo*** [2001] NSWCCA 540, Smart JA at [53]:

1. Reasonable suspicion is **less than a reasonable belief but more than a possibility**. There must
   be something creating in a reasonable person "an apprehension or fear". A reason to *suspect* a
   fact exists is more than a reason to look into the possibility of its existence.
2. It is **not arbitrary** — some factual basis must be shown. It may rest on hearsay or
   inadmissible material, but the material must have some probative value.
3. What matters is **the information in the officer's mind at the time**, judged against its source
   and content in the whole of the circumstances.

## Use it for

- **crime.2.1 (police powers)** — the single best thing in the library for showing that "reasonable
  suspicion" is a **real legal threshold with content**, not a formula. Pick two facts from the table
  — the hour of the night, a passenger, a car registered elsewhere — and show a court holding they
  were not enough.
- **crime.2.3 / crime.3.7 (investigating crime, use of evidence)** — the consequence of getting it
  wrong is **s 138 of the *Evidence Act 1995***: improperly obtained evidence may be excluded, as in
  *R v Large*. That is the practical sanction, and it is a discretion, not a rule.
- **Where the cases come from** — note that almost all of these are **District and Local Court**
  decisions. The law on the most commonly exercised police power is made mostly in courts that
  create no binding precedent, which is itself an argument about access to justice.
- **Pair it with the Balzan inquest**, where the suspicion was found never to have been formed, and
  with the knife-wanding scheme, where Parliament has removed the suspicion requirement altogether
  for scanning in a declared area.

## Caution

A **practitioner's quick-reference guide**, not a textbook or a judgment, and the author says so:
it "contains some paraphrasing" and "individual cases should be read if they are to be relied upon".
It was **prepared in January 2020** and has not been updated here, so later authority is not
captured and LEPRA has been amended since. Several citations in the table are incomplete (*Police v
Thao Phuon Nguyen*, *R v Fortescue* and *Ussher* carry no judgment number), so check them before
citing. Written to assist the defence, which shapes which cases are collected.
