The **National Rental Affordability Scheme** was a Commonwealth housing supply policy introduced by
the **Rudd government in 2008**. It paid annual tax and cash incentives to developers and community
housing providers who **built new dwellings** and rented them to low- and moderate-income tenants at
**at least 20% below market rent** for **ten years**. Its target group was the households who earn
**too much for social housing and too little for a decent private rental**.

The **Abbott government axed it in 2014**; the last new dwellings were approved in **2016**; and
because each allocation ran for ten years, the homes left the scheme one by one as their terms
expired.

## How it ended

- The scheme **finished in June 2026**.
- The **final 4,591 homes** were phased out — **over 2,000 in NSW**, **1,550 in Western Australia**,
  **596 in Tasmania**; Queensland participants had already exited.
- About **36,000** NRAS rentals were lost in total by the wind-up.
- **Tenants of private landlords face rent increases of 20% or more** at the end of their lease.
  Tenants in **community housing** NRAS homes may keep a subsidy and stay affordable.
- **Queensland** moved to **buy more than 400 dwellings** to keep them affordable. Most other states
  and territories did little, and the gap fell on charities and community housing providers.
- There is **no direct like-for-like replacement**.

## The assessment

**Steven Rowley** (Curtin University) argues it is surprising that no government has introduced a
**replacement minus the flaws**, to keep leveraging private investment into new affordable housing —
because the **Housing Australia Future Fund has not delivered as fast** and significant unmet need
remains.

The flaws were real: the **subsidy was set too high**, producing windfall gains to some developers
and landlords (Grattan's criticism), and it was a **flat subsidy** that ignored dwelling type and
location, so it was **poorly targeted**.

What it achieved was equally real: it **boosted affordable supply quickly**, with homes that would
not otherwise have been built, and it helped thousands of households who fell between social housing
and the private market.

## Use it for

- **shel.3.4 — the single best example of what "no enforceable right to housing" means in
  practice.** A scheme can be legislated, deliver 36,000 homes, be abolished by the next government,
  and expire tenant by tenant until the subsidy simply stops. **No tenant could enforce anything.**
  The sentence to argue: Australia's affordable housing framework rests on **discretionary policy**,
  not on an enforceable entitlement.
- **A complete political timeline (chg.1.3 / responsiveness)** — **2008 introduced, 2014 axed, 2016
  last approvals, June 2026 final exit, no successor.** Eighteen years, three phases, four
  governments. Few records in the library let you date a policy's whole life.
- **The 20% rent increase at the end of a lease** is the concrete consequence. It lands on a sitting
  tenant who did nothing, through a lawful rent increase under the *Residential Tenancies Act 2010*.
  Read it with `smh-2025-renting-retirees-grattan` and `conversation-2025-eviction-experiences` for
  what that does to a household.
- **Commonwealth and state responsibility (inv.6.4)** — the Commonwealth funded it and withdrew; only
  **Queensland** stepped in, buying 400+ dwellings. The clearest small illustration that housing
  sits in the gap between two levels of government, with **community housing providers and charities**
  absorbing the consequences.
- **Compare the successors honestly** — the **Housing Australia Future Fund** (see
  `anao-haff-2026` and `telegraph-2025-haff-costly-flop`) funds dwellings directly but slowly;
  **build to rent** concessions (`build-to-rent-tax-concessions-2024`) buy 10% affordable dwellings
  with forgone revenue; **Commonwealth Rent Assistance** subsidises the tenant instead. NRAS is the
  benchmark all three should be measured against.
- **ICESCR article 11 (hr.2.7)** — the right to adequate housing exists in international law and is
  **not enforceable domestically**. NRAS's expiry is the evidence, not the assertion.

## Caution

The library document is a **study-aid card** summarising an **opinion and analysis article by one
academic** — Rowley's prescription (a replacement scheme) is an argument, and the card's own note
says to balance it with Treasury, DSS and Grattan sources. Quote **Rowley** and the **ABC**, not this
card.

**There are two versions of this card in the library and they contradict each other.** One is dated
**1 July 2025** and says the final properties left in **June 2025**; the other is dated **1 July
2026** and says **June 2026**. The **2026 version is correct**: ABC News reported on **5 February
2026** that the scheme ends in **June 2026**, with **4,591** homes then being phased out. The 2025
card has been set aside. Both cards also give the final figure as **3,600**, which the ABC's **4,591**
supersedes — use the verified number.

The headline totals are approximate and vary by source ("35,000+" on the card, "about 36,000" in the
ABC report); say "about 36,000". The **20% below market rent** was the scheme requirement, not a
measure of what tenants actually saved. The claim that there is "no replacement" is a judgement about
like-for-like design, and the government points to the **HAFF** (target cited by the ABC as **55,000**
social and affordable homes) and an increase to **Commonwealth Rent Assistance**; a fair evaluation
names those and then explains why they are not equivalent, rather than ignoring them.
