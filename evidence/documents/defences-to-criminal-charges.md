**Complete defences** justify the accused's actions — charges dropped, or an acquittal. **Partial
defences** are mitigating circumstances that reduce the offence or the sentence.

## The complete defences, and who bears the onus

| Defence | Substance |
|---|---|
| **Automatism** | An act done without the control or direction of the will — sleepwalking, post-traumatic loss of control after a head injury, anaesthetic, epilepsy |
| **Mistake** | An honest and reasonable mistake **of fact** (ignorance of the law is no excuse), so the mens rea could not form. **Applies to strict liability offences** — *Proudman v Dayman* (1941) HCA — but not to absolute liability offences |
| **Necessity** | Circumstances, natural or human, induce the accused to break the law to avoid more dire consequences |
| **Self-defence** | The accused believed the conduct necessary and a reasonable response, to defend a person, prevent unlawful deprivation of liberty, protect property or prevent criminal trespass — **not available** for intentional or reckless infliction of death to protect property or prevent trespass |
| **Duress** | Choices overborne by a serious threat to the accused or their family |
| **Consent** | For some offences, that the victim freely and voluntarily agreed |

In every one of them, once the defence is raised on the evidence, **the prosecution must negative it
beyond reasonable doubt**.

## Use it for

- **The standard of proof doing real work.** Students describe defences as things the accused
  "proves". They are not: the accused raises them, and the prosecution disproves them. That single
  correction is worth marks, and it is the strongest illustration of the presumption of innocence in
  the topic.
- **Strict liability** (crime.1.3): *Proudman v Dayman* is why mistake still matters even where mens
  rea does not have to be proved — the one point of contact between the two dot points.
- **Moral and ethical standards**: necessity and duress are the law conceding that a person may
  rightly break it.
