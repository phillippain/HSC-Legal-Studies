The record that joins the two options this school teaches. Rising rents, limited supply and
cost-of-living pressure have made it **financially impossible** for many people to secure separate
accommodation after a relationship breaks down — so separated and divorced couples **keep living
together**.

The consequences the article identifies:

- **emotional strain** and complex living arrangements, with effects on **children, mental health and
  family dynamics**;
- **legal and safety implications**, particularly where there is family conflict or **domestic
  violence**, where continued cohabitation may **increase risk**;
- a situation that, experts argue, **exposes gaps in both family law protections and housing
  policy** — and shows how interconnected the two are.

## The law that already deals with it, and the law that does not

**Separation under one roof** is legally recognised. A couple can satisfy the twelve-month separation
requirement for divorce under the *Family Law Act 1975* (Cth) while living in the same home,
supported by evidence that the relationship has ended. So **procedurally the law is flexible** — it
does not force you to move out in order to be separated.

What the law does not do is make leaving **possible**. The *Family Law Act* requires the **best
interests of the child** to be the paramount consideration in parenting matters; the
**housing needs of children** became an express property factor on **10 June 2025**; **AVOs and
ADVOs** under the *Crimes (Domestic and Personal Violence) Act 2007* (NSW) can exclude a person from
a home. Each is a right that depends on there being **somewhere else to go**.

## Use it for

- **fam.2.3 / fam.2.1 — the best Family–Shelter crossover record in the library.** A family law
  question about the consequences of separation, answered with housing evidence. If a question asks
  about the effectiveness of legal responses to separation, "the parties cannot afford to separate"
  is a devastating and genuinely current point.
- **"Separation under one roof" is the detail to use (fam.2.1).** It shows the law being
  **procedurally accommodating** — you can be legally separated without moving — and simultaneously
  **substantively useless**, because it was designed as a concession for couples who choose to stay,
  not a solution for couples who cannot leave.
- **Rights that need a vacancy to be real (inv.1.4).** An exclusion order on an ADVO removes the
  perpetrator from the home; where do they go, and if the victim leaves instead, where do they go?
  Read it with `conversation-2025-eviction-experiences` (bond needed before the old one is returned)
  and `anglicare-rental-affordability-2026`. The pattern is the same across the whole library: **a
  legal right with no practical means of exercise**.
- **Family violence risk, explicitly (fam.2.4)** — experts warn cohabitation after separation may
  increase danger. The protective framework is strong on paper and is defeated by a housing market.
  One of the most serious access-to-justice arguments available.
- **The best interests of the child against economic reality (fam.2.2)** — children remaining in
  high-conflict households because neither parent can move. The principle is in the Act; the
  outcome is set by the rental market.
- **Fragmented jurisdiction (inv.6.4)** — family law is **Commonwealth**, tenancy and housing are
  **state**. Neither can fix this alone, and nobody owns the problem. Pair it with
  `alrc-135-family-law-for-the-future-2019`, which made exactly that argument about child protection.
- **The 2024 property amendments as a partial response** — Parliament added the **housing needs of
  children** to the property factors in 2025, which is the law noticing this problem. Whether a
  property order can conjure a second home is the question.

## Caution

This is a **teaching evidence table summarising an ABC News article**, not the article: the "Legal
Response" and "Evaluation of Effectiveness" columns and the model paragraph are **the summariser's
analysis**, so do not quote them as findings, and go to the ABC piece (**5 May 2026**) before using
anything as evidence. The article itself describes a **trend** reported by experts — there are **no
figures in the summary** for how many couples this affects, so do not imply a measured prevalence;
if you need numbers, take them from the ABS, Anglicare's rental affordability snapshot or the
Productivity Commission rather than from this document.

The table's citations should be checked before reuse: *MRR v GR* [2010] HCA 4 is a real High Court
decision about s 65DAA and the practicability of equal time, but the proposition attributed to it
here is loosely stated, and the "Productivity Commission" reference is unparticularised. "Separation
under one roof" is correctly described as available for **divorce** purposes, but it has evidentiary
requirements (often a corroborating affidavit) that the summary omits. Nothing here is a court
decision or a government report. Finally, the underlying situations involve **family violence and
children in high-conflict homes**; flag it before class use and keep the discussion on the legal
structure.
