## Law, custom and rule

| | Made by | Applies to | Enforced by |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Custom** | Repeated practice over time | Those who share the practice | Social pressure, expectation |
| **Rule** | A group, club, family, school | Members, by their choice to join | The group — exclusion, sanction |
| **Law** | A sovereign authority — parliament, courts | **Everyone in the jurisdiction**, chosen or not | The state, through courts and enforcement agencies |

Law is **binding on everyone, enforceable by the state, and officially recorded and administered**.
Custom is where much of it began — the common law grew out of the custom of the realm, and Aboriginal
and Torres Strait Islander customary law is a legal system in its own right, unwritten and
kinship-based, recognised by the Australian legal system only in part (`ls.2.9`, `ls.2.10`).

## Values and ethics

**Values** are what a society holds to be important; **ethics** are the reasoning that applies them
to conduct. Law is not identical to either — it usually lags behind changing values, and can be
technically valid while being ethically indefensible. That gap is the engine of law reform, and the
reason "the law should reflect society's values" is a claim to be argued, not assumed.

## Anarchy and tyranny — the two failures

- **Anarchy** is the absence of law and government. Without an authority able to enforce rules,
  disputes are settled by force, and the weak fare worst. It is the argument *for* law, and Hobbes's
  version of it — life "nasty, brutish and short" — is still the clearest statement of the case.
- **Tyranny** is law without limit: the state's power exercised arbitrarily, for its own ends, with
  courts unable to check it. It is the argument for the **rule of law** specifically, rather than for
  law in general.

Both matter because they mark the two directions in which a legal system fails, and the syllabus's
other Part I concepts — just laws, procedural fairness, the rule of law — are the safeguards between
them.
