International crime is criminal conduct with a significant international dimension: it either
offends the international community as a whole, or it crosses borders and requires cooperation
between states. The 2009 syllabus places it inside **Core Part I: Crime**.

| | Crimes against the international community | Transnational crimes |
|---|---|---|
| Core idea | Conduct so serious it concerns the whole international community, creating **individual** responsibility | Cross-border planning, conduct, victims, money, data or effects; domestic enforcement joined up by cooperation |
| Sub-types | Genocide; crimes against humanity; war crimes; the crime of aggression | Drug trafficking; trafficking in persons; people smuggling; arms trafficking; cybercrime; money laundering |
| Main responses | ICC, ICTY, ICTR, SCSL, domestic war crimes units, universal jurisdiction | UNTOC 2000 and the Palermo Protocols, extradition, mutual legal assistance, Interpol, AUSTRAC / AFP / Border Force |
| The judgement | Effective for norm-setting and major-offender accountability; limited by arrest, sovereignty, delay and selectivity | Effective where states cooperate operationally; limited by scale, profit, technology, corruption and uneven state capacity |

## The evidence that carries the argument

The **ICC is complementary** to domestic courts — it acts where states are unwilling or unable, and
depends on state cooperation for arrest and surrender. The **ICTY indicted 161 people and accounted
for every accused**, which is real accountability, and it operated for more than two decades, which
is delay and cost. One fact, used twice, in opposite directions: that is what "use evidence as proof,
not decoration" means.

## Use it for

The whole of **crime.6**, which is the thinnest-covered section in the Crime topic and the one
students most often answer with assertion. Note that BOCSAR — the source behind most of the Crime
statistics — is NSW-only and says nothing about international crime.
