## Legal aid — the three tests

Under the *Legal Aid Commission Act 1979* (NSW), representation in court is granted on:

| Test | What it asks |
|---|---|
| **Means** | The applicant's income and assets |
| **Merits** | The likelihood of a successful outcome — usually **not** applied to criminal matters except on appeal |
| **Jurisdiction** | Whether the matter falls in a funded area of law |

Most assisted clients still make a contribution. *Unaffordable and Out of Reach* (2012) found that
chronic underfunding forces services to limit eligibility to people on very low incomes, and that an
estimated **500,000 Australians a year miss out** on legal assistance altogether — the working poor
who fail the means test but cannot pay.

## Burden and standard of proof

- The **burden (onus)** answers *who must prove it*: in a criminal trial, the prosecution.
- The **standard** answers *how convincingly*: **beyond reasonable doubt**.

Two reforms cut across that:

- **Bail** — for *show cause* offences under the *Bail Act 2013* (NSW) the accused must show cause why
  detention is not justified, **on the balance of probabilities**. The onus moves.
- **Juries** — majority verdicts of 11–1 or 10–1 under the *Jury Amendment (Verdicts) Act 2006* (NSW)
  arguably dilute "beyond reasonable doubt", since a reasonable doubt held by one juror no longer stops
  a conviction.

## Use it for

- **Accessibility** and **resource efficiency** pulling against each other: the merits test saves public
  money by refusing weak cases, and denies representation to exactly the people least able to judge
  whether their case is weak.
- A reform paragraph on the **quiet erosion of the standard of proof** — not by amending it, but by
  moving the onus in bail and by changing what counts as a verdict.
