The **vendor's** side of the transaction, from the Law Society of NSW. Most Shelter answers are
written from the buyer's point of view; the obligations here are the reason purchasing works at all.

## The obligations

**You must have a contract before you market the property.** "Putting your house on the market
without having a proper contract is an **offence** under NSW law and could lead to you being fined."
That single sentence is the most useful thing in the document: vendor disclosure is **enforced by
penalty**, not left to negotiation.

**The Vendor Disclosure Requirements.** Certain documents must be attached and certain promises are
**deemed to be made** — "known legally as '**warranties**'" — about the property. The common
attachments:

- a **zoning certificate** from the local council (called a "section 149 certificate" in this
  document, now a **section 10.7 planning certificate**), showing planning controls and matters such
  as proposed road widening;
- a **drainage diagram** showing sewer lines;
- a copy of the **certificate of title**; and
- copies of any documents creating **easements, rights of way, restrictions or covenants**.

With advice, a vendor may also attach an **identification survey**, a **building certificate** and a
**home owners warranty insurance certificate**.

**Strata adds three more**: the property certificate for the lot and common property, the strata plan
showing the lot, and any **change of by-law** affecting the use of common property.

The checklist then runs through **what happens if the contract does not comply**, **standard or
tailored terms**, **private treaty versus auction**, **what is included in the sale**, **agent's
fees**, **exchange**, **the deposit**, **stamp duty, GST and CGT**, early access for a buyer, and
**settlement**.

## Use it for

- **shel.2.1 — vendor disclosure as a legal response, with teeth.** "It is an offence to market
  without a compliant contract" is a far better sentence than "the vendor must disclose certain
  things". Pair it with the **List of Documents** in the McKell Building record, which shows what is
  actually attached.
- **Deemed warranties (dis.2.1 / dis.2.2)** — the vendor promises things **by operation of law**,
  not by agreeing to them. A clean illustration of statute overriding freedom of contract to protect
  the party with less information, and it links straight to remedies if a warranty is breached.
- **Information asymmetry is the problem the law is solving (inv.1.2)** — the vendor knows about the
  easement, the sewer line and the road widening; the purchaser does not. Disclosure plus warranties
  plus a right to rescind for non-compliance is the mechanism. Good for "characteristics of effective
  law".
- **The same event from two sides (shel.2.5)** — read this with the purchasing guide record: the
  vendor must disclose, the purchaser must search, and most settlement disputes come from the gap
  between the two. Dispute **prevention** rather than dispute resolution.
- **Why a solicitor (shel.2.9 / inv.1.4)** — the document's framing question is "Do I really need a
  solicitor to sell my house?", and it is published by the professional body whose members answer
  yes. Note the interest, but the underlying point about complexity stands.
- **Three taxes in one line** — **stamp duty** (state, on the purchaser), **GST** (Commonwealth,
  usually not on an established residence) and **CGT** (Commonwealth, with the main residence
  exemption). A compact way into the tax records, including the family home tax break.

## Caution

**Published 2012** by the **Law Society of NSW**, and the age shows: "**section 149 certificate**" has
been **section 10.7** since the EP&A Act was renumbered in 2018, the *Conveyancing (Sale of Land)
Regulation* has been remade, and electronic conveyancing and eContracts have changed the mechanics of
exchange and settlement entirely. Use it for the **structure of a vendor's obligations**, and check
the current prescribed documents on the NSW legislation site or Fair Trading before stating any
requirement as law. It is a **professional body's consumer publication** promoting the use of
solicitors, and it **disclaims liability** and says it is general information and not a substitute
for legal advice — so it is evidence of the obligations, not authority on them. It covers **NSW**
only. It does not state the statutory sections, so a response citing "vendor disclosure" should cite
**s 52A of the *Conveyancing Act 1919*** and the Regulation rather than this document.
