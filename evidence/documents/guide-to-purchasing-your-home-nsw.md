A consumer guide that sets out purchasing as a **sequence**, which is how the dot point is examined
and how students rarely learn it.

## The thirteen steps

Looking for property → **checking the contract of sale** → expressing an interest → applying for a
loan → arranging inspections → **deciding on type of ownership** → **exchanging contracts** →
**paying the deposit** → **paying the stamp duty** → after exchange → insurance → a final check →
**the day of settlement**.

## The parts worth teaching

**Two ways to buy, with very different rights.** By **private treaty** there is a cooling-off right.
**At auction there is none** — "once the hammer falls you are bound to purchase the property. There
is no cooling-off period." The guide's auction advice is itself legal content: know your finance
limit, and note that **the seller has the right to one bid**.

**Cooling off.** After exchange there is a **five business day** cooling-off period in which the
purchaser may cancel, forfeiting **0.25% of the price** — $250 per $100,000, so **$1,250 on a
$500,000 home**. It does not apply at auction, or to a contract exchanged on the same day as an
auction that was passed in. It can be **waived by a section 66W certificate**, or shortened or
extended by written agreement. Off-the-plan contracts get **ten business days**.

**What must be attached.** The contract must include a **cooling-off statement** and a notice
directing the parties to **section 52A of the *Conveyancing Act 1919*** and the **Conveyancing (Sale
of Land) Regulation**; for a strata unit, the folio of the Register for the lot and common property
and a copy of the strata plan.

**Gazumping**, and the trap it creates. Buyers are pressured into exchanging — sometimes "with or
without the cooling-off period" — **before finance is approved in writing**, having already paid the
lender's establishment and valuation fees. The guide's warning is blunt: "It is unwise to enter into
any contract without first obtaining legal advice."

**Five kinds of ownership**: **Torrens title, strata title, common law (old system) title, community
title and company title.**

**Who does the legal work**: a solicitor, a licensed conveyancer, or do-it-yourself conveyancing.

## Use it for

- **shel.2.1 — the process, in order, with the law attached to each step.** "Exchange of contracts"
  and "settlement" are different events weeks apart, and knowing which rights attach to which is the
  difference between a vague answer and a precise one.
- **Private treaty versus auction is the best mechanism contrast in the topic** — the same purchase,
  the same statute, and a cooling-off right that exists in one and not the other. Excellent for
  whether the law protects consumers **consistently**, and for why auction culture in Sydney matters
  legally and not just financially.
- **0.25% against a 10% deposit (dis.2.3)** — withdraw inside the cooling-off period and you lose
  0.25%; walk away afterwards and the 10% deposit is at risk. Two prices for changing your mind, and
  a neat way to show how contract law allocates risk at a point in time.
- **Section 66W waiver as the loophole in the protection (inv.1.4)** — a right that can be signed
  away under pressure to secure a property is a weaker right than it looks. Pair it with the
  gazumping passage.
- **Access to justice in a private transaction (shel.2.9 / inv.1.4)** — solicitor, conveyancer or
  do-it-yourself. The law permits self-help on the largest purchase most people make; whether that is
  accessibility or exposure is a real argument.
- **Unconscionability where the process fails (amadio-1983)** — the guide's own warnings about
  pressure, unapproved finance and signing without advice are the factual setting in which *Amadio*
  and the *Contracts Review Act 1980* operate.

## Caution

An **undated consumer guide** and plainly an **old one** — it refers to the **Conveyancing (Sale of
Land) Regulation 2000** (since remade), gives a worked example on a **$250,000** house, and lists
"**section 149 certificate**" style requirements that have been renumbered (a s 149 planning
certificate is now a **section 10.7** certificate). Treat it as a guide to the **structure** of a
purchase, not to current detail. The cooling-off figures in this record were **checked against the
current NSW Government guidance** (five business days, 0.25%, no cooling off at auction, 66W waiver,
ten business days off the plan) rather than taken from the guide; verify them again before teaching,
as they change. It is **general information and says so** — "must not be relied on as a substitute
for legal advice" — and it describes **NSW** only; cooling-off rights, stamp duty and title systems
differ in every state. **Stamp duty rates, first home buyer concessions and thresholds are not
current** in a document of this age; take those from Revenue NSW.
