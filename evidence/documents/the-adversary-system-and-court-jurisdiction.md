## Jurisdiction

**Original jurisdiction** is the authority to hear a matter in the first instance; **appellate
jurisdiction** is the authority to review a decision of another court. Where a matter is heard turns
on the **nature and severity of the offence** and the **age of the offender**. Either side may appeal
on fact or law, against conviction or sentence; **leave** is usually required, and **juries take no
part in appeals**.

## The adversary system

Two opposing parties present their cases to an impartial judge or jury, who decides between what is
put before them. In the **inquisitorial** systems of continental Europe, the judge directs the
investigation and can call for evidence.

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| Each party has an equal opportunity to present its case | Parties are not in fact equal — resources, skill and knowledge differ |
| Less prone to abuse or bias by an official running the case | The decision-maker cannot call for evidence neither side wants heard |
| The jury observes without external influence | Complex cases are hard for a jury to follow |

Evan Whitton, in an ABC Radio interview (2010), put the criticism at its bluntest: the adversarial
system is "a cartel run by lawyers and judges for their own benefit", and the European investigative
model is more efficient and more just.

## Use it for

- **Any "to what extent is the criminal trial process just" question.** The strongest structure is:
  the system's fairness is *procedural* — equal opportunity to be heard — and procedural equality
  delivers substantive justice only when representation is equal. That takes you straight into
  *Dietrich*, legal aid rationing, and self-represented accused.
- **Legal personnel** — the judge as umpire rather than investigator is the single feature that
  distinguishes the two systems, and it explains why the prosecution's disclosure duties and the
  s 138 exclusion discretion matter so much.
