**Eight minutes.** Bradley Balzan, 20, left his home in St Marys at 10:11am on 23 December 2020.
At 10:18am four plain-clothes officers of the Nepean Proactive Crime Team, in an unmarked car,
saw him walking 200 metres away and got out. At 10:20am he was dead in his own back yard,
shot twice by Senior Constable Vingerhoed.

The inquest was mandatory: the death happened in a police operation, so **s 23 of the
*Coroners Act 2009*** required it.

## The chain the Coroner found

| Step | Finding |
|---|---|
| The stop | The officers **expected to search him before they spoke to him**. A/Sgt Parkinson's intention was more than to talk; the others thought he "looked suspicious"; the team had searched three sets of people in the preceding 90 minutes |
| Reasonable suspicion | Rejected the argument that suspicion "built incrementally" — on these facts it did not |
| The grab | Persisting after Balzan made clear he would not engage, approaching from behind and **grabbing his arm to force him to stop**, amounted to **constructive detention**, "or at the very least detention that was not justified by any power or statute" |
| The pursuit | **"Not authorised by LEPRA"** — a continuation of the unjustified detention. Running from an unlawful hold cannot justify continuing it. **"Objectively unreasonable"** |
| The shooting | Balzan was shot **in defence of A/Sgt Parkinson** after a struggle — but "events should not have reached the point they had" |

> "The origin point for the tragic encounter was the earlier unjustified roadside detention."

## The seven recommendations

Four of the seven are about **body-worn video**: record the reasons when it should have been on
and was not; **mandate activation when statutory powers are used** or are likely to be; define
"must" and "operational policing" in the procedures. The rest go to proactive patrols — a standard
operating procedure and training covering constructive detention, s 21 LEPRA and reasonable force,
including whether people should be **told they are not required by law to stop and answer
questions**; consistent use-of-force manuals requiring communication and a verbal warning before
force; and authorising trained plain-clothes officers to carry tasers.

## Use it for

- **crime.2.1 (police powers)** — the strongest current Australian illustration that a police power
  has a threshold, and that everything downstream of an unlawful stop is also unlawful. The
  "constructive detention" line is unusually quotable: an officer may never say "you are detained",
  but standing in someone's path, identifying as police and asking questions can imply it, and
  **persisting after refusal, especially with force, is what crosses the line**.
- **crime.t.discretion** — proactive patrolling as discretion in its purest form: nobody directed
  these officers to stop this person. Note the base rate the Coroner relied on (three searches in
  90 minutes) as evidence of what the patrol was actually for.
- **ls.1.7 / ls.1.6 (rule of law, procedural fairness)** — the police submitted it was procedurally
  unfair to find constructive detention when they had never been *trained* in the concept. The
  Coroner rejected that, and said the lack of training "underscores the importance of express
  guidance". Both halves are usable.
- **ls.4.2 (agencies of reform)** — a coroner as a reform agency: no power to punish, seven
  recommendations, and a civil suit by the father running alongside.
- **Evaluating effectiveness** — accountability in layers that each stopped short: the shooting
  itself was found to be in defence of a colleague, so the finding lands on the *stop*, not the
  shot. Good for the gap between "wrong" and "punishable".

## Caution

A **coronial inquest is not a criminal or civil trial**: the Coroner makes findings on the balance
of probabilities, cannot determine guilt, and recommendations are **not binding**. Do not write that
any officer was convicted or disciplined — the findings do not say so, and the related civil
proceedings were still on foot. Non-publication orders apply to parts of the material, and one
officer is identified only as "Officer 4"; keep to what the published findings say. The findings run
to 170 pages and this record summarises only the police-powers thread.
