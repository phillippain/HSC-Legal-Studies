Cultural burning is cool, patchy, seasonal burning read from Country — the right fire for each plant community, lit by the people with responsibility for it. It is an obligation under Lore, suppressed for most of the twentieth century and re-emerging through Indigenous-led practice.

## Firesticks Alliance

- An Aboriginal-led not-for-profit (founded as a network in 2009, incorporated 2018) that trains Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people and partners — Rural Fire Service brigades, councils, landholders — in cultural fire; it runs the National Indigenous Fire Workshop and a cultural fire mentoring program.
- Its principle: fire knowledge belongs to the Traditional Owners of each Country, so the Alliance supports local fire practitioners rather than delivering a standard method.

## The Royal Commission (2020)

The Royal Commission into National Natural Disaster Arrangements, after the 2019–20 fires that burned 24 million hectares, devoted a chapter to Indigenous land and fire management and recommended that governments engage with Traditional Owners to explore the relationship between Indigenous land management and natural disaster resilience and to incorporate it in their own programs.

## Use it for

- **inv.2.4** — a responsibility of Lore (land management by fire) and its contemporary practice, recognised by a royal commission as something the mainstream law of land management needed.
- **inv.2.2** — Elders and senior practitioners as the holders of the knowledge and the authority to use it.

## Caution

Cultural burning is not the same as hazard-reduction burning and its practitioners resist the comparison; do not present it as a fire-fighting technique. Legal barriers remain: permits, liability and tenure still sit with state agencies, which is the law reform angle.
