## The legislative process

A **bill** is drafted, usually from cabinet decision or a review recommendation. It passes the
**first reading** (introduction), **second reading** (the debate on principle), the **committee
stage** (clause by clause, where amendments are made), and the **third reading**, then repeats the
process in the **other house** — the Legislative Council in NSW, the Senate federally. Both houses
must pass the identical text. **Royal assent** by the Governor or Governor-General makes it an Act,
and the Act commences on assent, on a stated date, or by proclamation.

Bicameralism is the point: a house of review can amend or block, which slows the process and is
either a safeguard or an obstruction depending on the argument being made.

## Delegated legislation

Parliament confers on the executive the power to make **regulations, ordinances, rules and by-laws**
under an enabling Act. It exists because parliament has neither the time nor the technical knowledge
to legislate the detail, and because detail needs to change faster than an Act can be amended.

The controls are real but thin:

- it must be **within the power** the enabling Act gave — beyond it, it is *ultra vires* and invalid,
  a matter for the courts;
- it is **tabled in parliament** and may be **disallowed** within a set period;
- scrutiny committees review instruments for whether they trespass on rights.

## Use it for

- **Volume versus visibility**: delegated legislation makes up the great majority of law made each
  year, and almost none of it is debated. That single comparison answers most questions on `ls.2.5`.
- **Responsiveness versus the rule of law** — the trade is speed and expertise against law made by
  people no one elected, reviewed only if a committee or a litigant raises it.
- Concrete instances elsewhere in this finder: rental law detail carried in regulations after the
  2024 amending Act, and police powers whose operation depends on codes and regulations under LEPRA.
