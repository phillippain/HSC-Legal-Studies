Households that cannot buy alone are buying **together** — families, friends and retirees forming
co-living groups in the Illawarra, where the median house price is around **$1.3 million** after a
**50% rise in five years**.

## Where the law is, and is not

- **Co-ownership is permitted.** Property law has always allowed **joint tenancy** and **tenancy in
  common**, and the standard contract for sale has a tick box for each — so two or six people can
  hold a title.
- **Finance is the barrier.** Lenders are **hesitant to lend to multiple unrelated borrowers**;
  mortgage structures were built for a couple. Some groups constitute themselves as **collectives or
  companies** to get a loan. Banking and finance regulation recognises **joint mortgages** but is
  risk-averse about non-traditional households.
- **Governance is left to private agreement.** Groups draft "**household constitutions**" covering
  decision-making and **exit strategies**. Contract law lets them, and nothing requires or standardises
  it.
- **Planning law is largely silent** on multi-household ownership, so these arrangements get little
  formal recognition or protection.
- The social benefits are real and entirely non-legal: **reduced isolation, shared childcare, support
  in retirement**.
- **ABS projects about 5% of households will be group households by 2046.**

## Use it for

- **shel.2.1 — the most current example of ownership adapting faster than the law.** The right to buy
  together exists; the **finance, governance and planning frameworks** have not followed. Good for
  responsiveness without resorting to "the law is slow".
- **Joint tenants versus tenants in common finally matters (inv.4.3)** — for a couple it is a
  formality. For six unrelated buyers the difference between **survivorship** and a **divisible share**
  decides what happens when one dies, marries or wants out. Teach the distinction here and it sticks.
- **"Household constitutions" are private ordering (dis.2.3)** — people writing their own
  constitution because no statute governs them. A striking example of **contract filling a legislative
  gap**, and of the limits of that: an agreement between six people is only as good as their
  willingness to enforce it against each other.
- **Where the gap actually is (inv.1.2)** — not in property law but in **lending practice and
  prudential risk appetite**. A sophisticated point: a legal right can be unusable because a private
  institution will not finance it.
- **Exit is the hard problem (shel.2.5)** — one co-owner wanting out of an indivisible asset. Worth
  asking what remedy exists (sale, partition, a buy-out clause) and how long and how much it costs.
- **Changing household structures (fam.1.4 / shel.1.1)** — retirees and friends as a household. Use
  the **5% by 2046** projection to argue that the law needs to recognise households that are not
  families, and pair it with `group-renting-cotenancy-2026` for the renting equivalent.
- **Set it beside Help to Buy** — two responses to the same deposit problem: the state becomes a
  co-owner, or your friends do. One is legislated, the other improvised.

## Caution

This is a **teaching evidence table summarising an ABC News article**, not the article and not
research — the "Legal Response" and "Evaluation of Effectiveness" columns are **the summariser's
analysis**, so do not quote them as findings. Go to the ABC piece (**10 September 2025**) before using
any figure. The **$1.3 million median** and **50% in five years** are as reported for Wollongong at
that date and will have moved; the **ABS 5% group households by 2046** figure is a **projection**
attributed in the table but not sourced, so verify it with the ABS. The claim that planning law is
"largely silent" and that lending is "ineffective" is **the table's judgement**; the underlying
reality is that co-ownership is lawful and financed case by case, so the accurate statement is that
there is **no tailored framework**, not that the arrangement is unlawful. Nobody in a failed shared
ownership arrangement is quoted, and the obvious risks — a co-owner's bankruptcy, death, divorce or
refusal to sell — are not examined. Specific legal and financial advice is essential before anyone
enters such an arrangement, and a classroom resource should say so.
