## NSW

| Court | Original jurisdiction | Appellate |
|---|---|---|
| **Local Court** | Summary offences, indictable matters dealt with summarily, committals, civil claims to $100,000; magistrate, no jury | — |
| **District Court** | Most indictable offences except murder and treason; civil to $750,000 | Appeals from the Local Court |
| **Supreme Court** | Murder, treason, serious drug offences; unlimited civil | — |
| **NSW Court of Criminal Appeal** | — | Criminal appeals from the District and Supreme Courts |
| **NSW Court of Appeal** | — | Civil appeals |

Specialist courts and tribunals sit alongside: the **Children's Court** (closed, no jury, names
suppressed), the **Coroner's Court**, the **Drug Court**, and **NCAT** for tenancy, consumer and
anti-discrimination matters.

## Federal

**Federal Circuit and Family Court of Australia**, Divisions 1 and 2 — family law, migration, and
general federal law; the **Federal Court**; the **Administrative Review Tribunal** (which replaced
the AAT in October 2024) for merits review of Commonwealth decisions; and above them all the **High
Court of Australia**, which hears constitutional matters, appeals by special leave from any Australian
superior court, and is the final court of appeal since the Australia Acts 1986.

## Why a hierarchy

1. **Precedent needs it.** *Stare decisis* only works if it is clear whose decisions bind whom:
   a court is bound by courts above it in the same hierarchy, persuaded by courts elsewhere.
2. **Appeals need it.** A rehearing or a review of law requires a court above the one that decided.
3. **Resource efficiency.** Minor matters are dealt with quickly and cheaply, and judicial time is
   spent in proportion to what is at stake — which is also the accessibility argument against it,
   since the cheap court is the one most people meet and the one with the least procedural protection.
