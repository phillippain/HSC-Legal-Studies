A women's group from **Wadeye and Peppimenarti**, about four hours south of Darwin, presented
*Mapping Women and Children's Safety* to the NT and federal governments in late September 2026. The
ABC reported it on 15 October. Neither government had formally responded.

## The gap, in their words

| Asked for | What exists |
|---|---|
| A safe house at Peppimenarti — the most urgent priority | None; nearest refuge **and** police are in Wadeye, about an hour away |
| Double or triple Wadeye's capacity for ~2,500 people | **Four beds**, often full; women told to ring police instead |
| A men's behaviour change program | NT says programs are under review |
| Counselling and help for women leaving | Women say support is inadequate |
| A sobering-up shelter | None reported locally |
| More police and security | 212 new public safety officers funded; postings unspecified |
| A share of NT's promised extra $36m a year | NT's DFSV budget **fell** from $71m to $63m in 2026–27, about **11%** |

## Where the legal pathway breaks

Under the *Domestic and Family Violence Act 2007* (NT), **any adult** must report serious domestic
violence harm or threats to police (s 124A). Police may make a DVO in urgent situations or where a
court order is impractical (s 28); a court DVO comes from the Local Court (s 52) and breach is a
criminal offence (s 120). Every rung assumes police can get there: the Police Commissioner told the
2024 inquest that DV response times had **risen** with demand and officer shortages, and clan
fighting slows response further. A DVO then assumes somewhere safe to be while it takes effect.

## Use it for

- **fam.2.4 (domestic violence)** — the strongest available answer to "are legal responses
  effective?" that turns on **resources rather than law**. The orders exist; the enforcement and the
  accommodation behind them do not.
- **fam.2.6 / hr.2.12 (non-government and community organisations)** — First Nations women
  researching and publishing their own findings, then using them to lobby two governments. The 2026
  national First Nations plan *Our Ways – Strong Ways – Our Voices* (2026–2036) funds up to 40
  community-controlled organisations and endorses exactly this model.
- **ls.4.1 / ls.4.2 (conditions and agencies of reform)** — a **coronial inquest** as a reform
  agency: Coroner Elisabeth Armitage's November 2024 findings into four Aboriginal women's deaths made
  **35 recommendations**, including more safe house beds and men's programs. NT supported 21,
  accepted 11 only "in principle" and rejected three, including a DFSV peak body. The ALRC's
  *Pathways to Justice* (2017) Rec 11-2 had already called for police training in prompt family
  violence response in Aboriginal communities.
- **Access to justice (ls.1.5)** — distance, not eligibility, is the barrier. Closing the Gap Target
  13 seeks a 50% cut in family violence by 2031.
- **Federalism** — the two governments blame each other: the Commonwealth says it funds Wadeye
  services including the safe house; the NT points to a record $36m a year while its own budget fell.

## Caution

A **class-made summary** of a **news report about an unpublished community report**. The figures on
beds, distances, population, children's exposure to violence and teenage pregnancy are **community
evidence, not independently verified**, and the report's method and sample cannot be checked. The
funding claims from both ministers are political and partly contradict each other; the federal $120m
figure could not be found itemised — the July 2026 federal NT package was almost $17m. Avoid
identifying individuals beyond what the article publishes, and avoid treating the two communities'
findings as describing all remote communities, though the women say the gaps are common.
