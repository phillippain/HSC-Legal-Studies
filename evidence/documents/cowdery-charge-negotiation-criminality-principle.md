Written by **Nicholas Cowdery**, Director of Public Prosecutions for NSW from 1994 to 2011 — so this
is the person who ran the office explaining how charge negotiation is supposed to work and what
pressures bend it.

## Why charge negotiation keeps growing

"Financial imperatives increasingly require criminal justice agencies, including prosecutors and
courts, to '**do more with less**'." The pressure is to reduce the number of contested hearings of
serious offences, shorten them, and turn cases over faster with "fewer and more junior personnel". In
practice that means three things:

- **increased charge negotiation** and agreement of facts between the parties — "often at the
  instigation of the prosecution";
- **pre-trial case management** to identify pleas and contested issues in advance; and
- **incentives, often legislated, to plead guilty** in order to reduce potential penalties.

That last point is the link to the *Criminal Procedure Act 1986* discount regime and the 2018 early
appropriate guilty plea reforms: the system **pays** for pleas.

## The questions he poses

They are better than anything a textbook asks, and they are examinable almost verbatim: What happens
behind the scenes? **Are the innocent being convicted?** How are rights protected? What effect do
these procedures have on the prosecutor's ability to remain a "**minister of justice**", striking the
right balance "without stepping into the role of judge"? **Should the courts be involved?** And how
are the interests of **victims, witnesses and the community** preserved — how can the community be
confident the result is just?

## The Samuels inquiry and the criminality principle

The **Samuels inquiry** — *Review of the NSW Director of Public Prosecutions' Policy and Guidelines
for Charge Bargaining and Tendering of Agreed Facts*, by the late **Hon Gordon Samuels AC CVO QC** —
was commissioned by the NSW Attorney General on **18 September 2001** after public criticism of
charge bargains in which prosecutors had **communicated inadequately with victims and police**. Two
cases stood out: ***AEM and others*** and ***Laupama***. The report was published **7 May 2002**.

Samuels' formulation is the thing to quote:

> "the optimum outcome of a criminal prosecution is resolution by a plea of guilty to a charge which
> **adequately represents the criminality** revealed by facts which the prosecution **can prove beyond
> reasonable doubt**, and which give the sentencer an **adequate range of penalty**. A charge bargain
> must not compromise the principle — which I will call 'the **criminality principle**' — made up of
> these three ingredients."

Essentially, Cowdery says, it is a question of **adequacy**.

## Use it for

- **crime.3.4 (pleas and charge negotiation) — the standard, not just the practice.** Most answers say charge
  negotiation saves time and risks under-punishment. This gives you a **three-part test** from a named
  inquiry, in quotable words, and a former DPP endorsing it. Learn the three ingredients: adequate
  representation of the criminality, provability beyond reasonable doubt, and an adequate sentencing
  range.
- **Resource efficiency against justice (crime.t.balance)** — "do more with less" is the honest
  reason the practice grew. Pair it with the **22.5% / 10% / 5%** sentencing discounts under the
  *Criminal Procedure Act* and `early-appropriate-guilty-plea-reforms-2018`: the system does not
  merely permit pleas, it **prices** them.
- **"Minister of justice" is the phrase for prosecutorial discretion (crime.t.discretion)** — the DPP
  is not a party trying to win, and the risk Cowdery names is that negotiating hard enough makes the
  prosecutor a de facto **judge**, choosing the sentencing range by choosing the charge. Connect it to
  the `odpp-prosecution-guidelines`.
- **"Are the innocent being convicted?"** — the plea discount gives an innocent accused a rational
  reason to plead guilty. State it as the question Cowdery states it as, and it is a serious argument
  rather than a rhetorical one.
- **Victims (exp.5.3 / crime.3.9)** — the Samuels inquiry was triggered by **failures to consult
  victims and police**. That is why the ODPP guidelines now require consultation, and it is the best
  available explanation of *why* a procedural safeguard exists.
- **Pair it with a real plea** — `smh-2025-thompson-guilty-plea-eve-of-trial` is a plea entered on
  the eve of trial after three years of denial, which is the system working exactly as the incentives
  predict, and `r-v-koch-2009` for agreed facts.

## Caution

The library file is a **.docx whose article is embedded as images**, with no text layer; the content
in this record was read by **OCR** after converting the document, so **verify any quotation against
the original** before using it. It is an **alumni magazine article**, not research or law — Cowdery
is writing reflectively, and he is an advocate for prosecutorial independence.

It is **undated on its face** and its subject is a **2001–02 inquiry**, so this is **historical
context, not current practice**: the ODPP Prosecution Guidelines have been reissued several times
since, the *Criminal Procedure Act 1986* now contains the **charge certification and case
conferencing** regime introduced in 2018 that replaced committals, and the plea discount structure is
statutory. **Cite the current guidelines and the Act for the law**, and use Cowdery and Samuels for
the **principle** and the reasons behind it. The two trigger cases (*AEM*, *Laupama*) are described
only in annexures to the Samuels Report and are not summarised here. Cowdery's questions are posed,
not answered — the answers are the student's job.
