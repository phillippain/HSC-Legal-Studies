Key terms

Human right
A basic entitlement every person holds, recognised in international law.
Ratify
To formally agree to be bound by a treaty.
Dualist system
A system in which a treaty has no effect in domestic courts until Parliament passes an Act.
Progressive realisation
The duty to move towards a right over time, using the maximum of available resources.
Justiciable
Able to be decided by a court.
Concluding observations
A UN committee's findings and recommendations after reviewing a country. They are not binding.
Optional Protocol
An extra treaty that lets individuals complain to the UN committee. Australia has not ratified the ICESCR one.

2.1The right in international law

What it isInternational law recognises housing as a human right: everyone's right to an adequate standard of living, including housing.
The lawUDHR art 25(1) (1948) is a declaration, not a binding treaty. ICESCR art 11(1) (1966) is binding on Australia, which ratified it in 1975. Under art 2(1) the duty is one of progressive realisation.
An exampleOn 17 February 2026 Australia appeared before the UN Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights. It was urged to adopt a rights-based national housing strategy, build more social housing and pass a national Human Rights Act.
Why it mattersInternational law sets the standard for judging Australian law. It has changed how people think about housing, but a committee can only recommend. It cannot compel.

✏️ Check yourself: A right on paper

1. What is the difference in legal force between the UDHR and the ICESCR?

The UDHR is a declaration: it states agreed standards but is not itself a binding treaty. The ICESCR is a treaty, so it binds the countries that ratify it, including Australia, in international law.

2. What does 'progressive realisation' let a government say when it is criticised? Why does that weaken the right?

It lets a government say it is moving towards the right as fast as its resources allow. Because there is no deadline or fixed standard, it is hard to prove a breach, so the right works as a goal instead of an entitlement.

2.2Why Australians cannot enforce it

What it isThere is no general legal right to shelter in Australia. The treaty binds Australia internationally but binds no Australian court until Parliament legislates.
The lawAustralia is dualist. The ICESCR has not been enacted. There is no right to housing in the Constitution, no national Human Rights Act and none in NSW. The Optional Protocol is not ratified, so individuals cannot complain to the UN.
An exampleThe Housing Act 2001 (NSW) sets objects for public housing, not rights. 67,721 households were on the NSW waiting list in October 2025; none could sue for a home.
Why it mattersThis is the enforceability gap. Rights exist only in pieces: tenancy law, discrimination law, housing policy. The ACT will recognise a right to housing from January 2027, but with no new complaint mechanism.

ANCHOR S1

Grootboom: a right a court can enforce

South Africa shows the gap in Australia is a choice
s 26a constitutional right to housing that a court can apply
  • South Africa's Constitution, s 26: everyone has the right of access to adequate housing; the state must take reasonable measures within its available resources
  • Irene Grootboom and about 900 others, more than half of them children, were evicted and living on a sports field under plastic sheeting
  • Constitutional Court (2000): the state's housing programme was unreasonable because it made no provision for people in desperate need
  • The court did not order a house for each person, and it did not design the policy
  • Contrast: in Tanudjaja v Canada (2014) a right-to-housing claim was struck out as not justiciable
Law: Government of the Republic of South Africa v Grootboom (2000), Constitutional Court of South Africa
How this case shows the right to shelter
In South Africa the right is written into domestic law, so a court could test the government's housing programme against it. An Australian court has no such power. The gap in Australia is a choice of legal design, not something inevitable.
Your turn: Why could Mrs Grootboom take her government to court when a person sleeping rough in Sydney cannot?
South Africa's Constitution contains a right of access to adequate housing, so the right is part of domestic law and justiciable. In Australia the ICESCR has not been enacted and there is no constitutional or statutory right to housing, so there is nothing for a court to apply.

2. Give two reasons a person cannot enforce a right to shelter in an Australian court.

Any two: Australia is dualist and the ICESCR has not been enacted; there is no right to housing in the Constitution; there is no national or NSW Human Rights Act; the Optional Protocol is not ratified, so there is no individual complaint to the UN.

3. Tanudjaja says housing policy is not a question for courts. Write one sentence for that view and one against it.

For: housing involves budgets and trade-offs that elected governments, not judges, should decide. Against: Grootboom shows a court can test whether a programme is reasonable without designing it, which gives the most vulnerable a way to be heard.
