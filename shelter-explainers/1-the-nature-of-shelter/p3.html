The obligation to provide shelter

Key terms

Division of powers
The split of law-making power between the Commonwealth and the states under the Constitution.
Residual power
A power not given to the Commonwealth, so it stays with the states. Housing is one.
Tied grant
Commonwealth money given to a state on conditions, under s 96 of the Constitution.
National Housing Accord
An agreement between governments and industry (2022). Its target, raised in 2023, is 1.2 million homes in five years from 1 July 2024.
Housing Australia Future Fund
A Commonwealth investment fund (2023) whose returns pay for social and affordable homes.
Commonwealth Rent Assistance
A Commonwealth payment that helps people on income support pay private rent.
Homes NSW
The NSW agency (2024) that runs public housing and homelessness services.

3.1Who is responsible for what?

What it isResponsibility is shared. The Constitution gives the Commonwealth no power over housing, so it is a residual power of the states. The Commonwealth controls most of the money.
The lawCommonwealth: tax, banking and social security powers (s 51) and tied grants (s 96), the basis of housing agreements since 1945. NSW: tenancy, planning, strata, conveyancing and public housing (Housing Act 2001 (NSW)).
An exampleOne renter, three governments: the Commonwealth pays Rent Assistance, NSW law sets the tenancy rules, and a council approved the building under state planning law.
Why it mattersSplit responsibility means reform needs cooperation, and it lets each level blame the other when targets are missed.

2. The Constitution gives the Commonwealth no power over housing. How does it still shape housing policy?

Through its tax, banking and social security powers in s 51, and through tied grants to the states under s 96, which fund national housing agreements on the Commonwealth's conditions.

3.2Is the obligation being met?

What it isThe obligation is moral, political and international. It is not a legal duty a person can enforce at home. Governments set targets, not duties.
The lawThe Housing Act 2001 (NSW) states objects. The Housing Australia Future Fund Act 2023 (Cth) creates a fund. The National Housing Accord is an agreement, not a law.
An example307,635 homes were completed under the Accord to March 2026; about 420,000 were needed to be on track. 1,432 of a targeted 40,000 HAFF homes had been delivered by May 2026 (ANAO).
Why it mattersA missed target has no legal consequence. Accountability comes from audits, the media and elections, which are non-legal responses.
CASE STUDY

Targets without duties

What the Auditor-General found about the Housing Australia Future Fund
1,432of a targeted 40,000 homes delivered as at May 2026
  • The Housing Australia Future Fund Act 2023 (Cth) set up a fund whose returns pay for social and affordable homes
  • Target: 40,000 homes
  • The Australian National Audit Office reported in July 2026 that 1,432 had been delivered as at May 2026: under 4%
  • The ANAO is an independent statutory auditor. It published a number the government would not have chosen to publish
  • No person on a waiting list has a legal claim because the target was missed
Law: Housing Australia Future Fund Act 2023 (Cth)
How this shows the obligation to provide shelter
The Commonwealth accepted an obligation, wrote a target and funded it. Because the obligation is a target and not a duty, the only consequence of falling short is scrutiny: an audit, media reporting and political pressure.
Your turn: Is an audit report a legal or a non-legal response? How effective is it at holding a government to its obligation?
The ANAO is created by statute, but its report cannot order anything, so it works as accountability through publicity. It is effective at exposing failure (transparency) but limited: it creates no remedy for people who are waiting.

2. To what extent are governments meeting their obligation to provide shelter? Make a judgement and support it with one figure.

To a limited extent. Governments have accepted the obligation and funded it, but delivery is far behind: 1,432 of 40,000 HAFF homes by May 2026, and the Accord about a quarter short of its on-track pace. Because the obligation is a target, not a duty, there is no legal consequence for the shortfall.
