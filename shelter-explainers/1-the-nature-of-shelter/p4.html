Key terms

Owner-occupier
A person who owns the home they live in, outright or with a mortgage.
Tenant
A person with a lease: the right to exclusive possession of a home in return for rent.
Boarder or lodger
A person who pays to live in someone else's premises without exclusive possession. Not a tenant.
Social housing
Rental housing provided below market rent by government (public housing) or non-profit providers (community housing).
Land lease community
A residential park where people own their home but rent the site it sits on.
Squat
Occupying land or a building without the owner's permission.
Indefeasibility
The registered owner's title cannot be defeated by an earlier unregistered claim.

4.1The types of shelter

What it isShelter is classified by tenure: the legal basis on which a person lives there. The main types are owning, renting privately, social housing, boarding, residential parks, aged care, and squats.
The lawEach type has its own Act. There is no single 'Shelter Act'. Owners: Real Property Act 1900 (NSW). Tenants: Residential Tenancies Act 2010 (NSW). The other types follow in the table.
An exampleAt the 2021 Census about 31% of Australian households owned outright, 35% had a mortgage and 31% rented.
Why it mattersA person's type of shelter decides which law applies, which tribunal or court hears a dispute, and how secure they are.

4.2The tenure ladder

What it isThink of the types as a ladder. The higher the rung, the stronger the legal protection. The person with the most secure shelter has the most protection of it.
The lawTop: registered owner, with near-absolute protection. Middle: tenant, with substantial protection that is stronger since 2024. Lower: boarder or lodger, excluded from the tenancy Act. Bottom: a person sleeping rough, governed by trespass and public space law.
An exampleA boarder in an unregistered share house can be asked to leave on short notice. A tenant next door can only be evicted on a ground set out in the Act.
Why it mattersThis is the idea the whole option turns on: the law protects tenure, not need. Use it to argue effectiveness in any question.

ANCHOR S2

Four people, four levels of protection

An illustration to use in any Shelter response
Tenurenot need, decides how much protection a person receives
  • Lin owns her unit. Her title is registered and cannot be defeated by an earlier unregistered claim
  • Sam rents a flat. Since 19 May 2025 his landlord needs a ground set out in the Act to end the lease
  • Jo pays weekly for a room in a share house with the owner living there. Jo is a lodger, outside the tenancy Act
  • Kai sleeps in a park. No shelter law protects Kai; move-on and trespass laws regulate where Kai can be
Law: Real Property Act 1900 (NSW); Residential Tenancies Act 2010 (NSW); Boarding Houses Act 2012 (NSW)
How this shows types of shelter
The four people need shelter equally, but the law gives them very different protection because each holds a different kind of tenure. Kai has the greatest need and no protection at all.
Your turn: Which person's protection has changed most in the last three years, and what changed it?
Sam's. The Residential Tenancies Amendment Act 2024 (NSW) ended no-grounds evictions from 19 May 2025, so a landlord must now have and prove a ground. Lin's protection is unchanged, and Jo and Kai remain outside the Act.

2. Explain what is meant by 'the law protects tenure, not need'.

Legal protection depends on the kind of shelter a person already has. Owners and tenants have Acts that protect them; people with the weakest tenure, or none, have the greatest need and the least protection.
