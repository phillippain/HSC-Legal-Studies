How this site works

1Start with your course

The buttons at the top right say Both, Preliminary and HSC. Pick yours and the whole page follows — the list on the left, the grid, the search and the counts. The site remembers your choice next time.

You will still see useful evidence from the other course. It carries a dashed HSC evidence or Preliminary evidence label so you know where it really belongs. That is deliberate: a Crime case can be perfect for a Preliminary question.

2Three ways to find something

  • Content — walk the syllabus: topic, then section, then dot point.
  • Themes — the themes and challenges. Big essay questions are usually built from these, so start here for a 25-marker.
  • Kind — find all your cases, or all your Acts. It also lists the eight criteria (enforceability, accessibility and so on), so you can pull up everything you have for one of them at once.

Or just type in the search box. It searches titles, citations and summaries at the same time.

3Reading a card

The stripe down the left is the topic. The icon at the top is the form of evidence — case, Act, report, media, international instrument, document.

Underneath, the tags are in labelled rows, because they do not all make the same claim:

  • About — the dot points this evidence is about. Lead with these.
  • Also useful for — dot points where it helps, though it is really about something else. Worth a sentence, not a paragraph.
  • Themes and challenges — the ones it carries, which is how the big essay questions are built.
  • Effectiveness criteria — the syllabus criteria for evaluating effectiveness it speaks to, such as enforceability or accessibility.

Every tag is a button: click one to see everything else filed against it.

4The grid: what you have and what you don’t

One square per dot point. Darker means more evidence. A dashed square with a 0 means nothing is written up yet, and a dotted square means there are documents filed but nobody has written them up. Click a square to jump straight to that dot point.

5Your list: the sheet and the map

Tick the box on any card. A bar appears at the bottom with what you have chosen, and two ways to take it away:

  • Evidence sheet — one entry per piece: title, citation, the one-line significance and its tags. Good for revision.
  • Evidence map — the same evidence sorted by dot point, and within each dot point grouped by form of evidence. Good for checking a topic is covered, or for planning an answer. It lists each piece under the dot points it is about; press + supporting points to list it under the points it merely supports as well. Copy map puts the same thing on your clipboard as plain text.

Both open your browser’s print box, so you can print on paper or save as a PDF.

6A worked example

“Evaluate the effectiveness of the legal system in addressing issues relating to care and protection of children.” (HSC 2020, Family, Q27(a))

  • Set the course to HSC, open Content, choose Family → Contemporary issues → Care and protection of children.
  • Tick two or three cases, plus an Act and a report — the card colours make the mix easy to see, and a good answer needs more than cases.
  • The question says evaluate, so open Kind and add something from effectiveness criteria such as enforceability or accessibility.
  • Press Evidence map. You now have your evidence grouped under the dot point, with the citations already written out — that is your paragraph plan.

7Starting over

Start again (top right, or beside the search box) clears everything — the course, the filters, the search and anything you have ticked — and brings you back to this start page. If you clear a list by accident, press Undo in the message that appears.

8Where the evidence comes from

New material is dropped into the inbox folder in the shared Evidence Finder drive, then filed, tagged and published from there. Ask your teacher if you have something to add.
